Habitat and Adaptation

A habitat is the place where an organism lives and gets its needs met. Organisms are adapted in structure and behaviour to survive there.

2 worked examples · 3 practice questions

Key points

  • Biotic components are living (plants, animals, microbes); abiotic components are non-living (air, water, soil, light, temperature).
  • Adaptations can be structural (camel's hump), behavioural (migration) or physiological (sweating).
  • Terrestrial habitats include deserts, grasslands and forests; aquatic habitats include ponds, rivers and oceans.
  • Cacti have spines instead of leaves, thick waxy stems and deep roots to conserve water.
  • Fish have gills to breathe dissolved oxygen and streamlined bodies to move through water.

Worked examples

Example 1 The camel's survival kit

A camel stores fat in its hump, has long eyelashes to keep out sand, nostrils it can close, and wide padded feet. It can lose water slowly and tolerate high body temperature — every feature is an answer to desert conditions.

Example 2 From water to land: the frog

A tadpole has gills and a tail and lives in water. As it becomes a frog it develops lungs and legs. The same animal occupies two habitats with two sets of adaptations at different life stages.

Practice questions

Try each question before opening the answer.

  1. MCQ The camel's hump stores:
    A. FatB. WaterC. SandD. Air
    Show answer

    Answer: Fat

    The hump is a fat store that can be broken down for energy; it is not a water bag.

  2. MCQ Gills help fish to:
    A. Breathe oxygen dissolved in waterB. Swim fasterC. Float on waterD. Smell food
    Show answer

    Answer: Breathe oxygen dissolved in water

    Gills absorb dissolved oxygen from water.

  3. Short answer Define habitat.
    Show answer

    The place where an organism lives and obtains food, water, shelter and air.
