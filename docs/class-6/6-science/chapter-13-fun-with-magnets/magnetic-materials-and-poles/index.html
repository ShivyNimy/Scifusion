Magnetic Materials and Poles

Magnets attract iron, nickel and cobalt, and have two poles where the effect is strongest. A free-suspended magnet always points north–south.

2 worked examples · 3 practice questions

Key points

  • A magnet's ends are its north and south poles; like poles repel, unlike poles attract.
  • A freely suspended bar magnet aligns along the Earth's north–south direction; the end pointing north is the north pole.
  • Magnets attract magnetic materials, not all metals (copper and aluminium are not attracted).
  • A compass is a small magnet that indicates direction, used by sailors and travellers.
  • Magnetic force acts through air and even through some materials, and is strongest near the poles.

Worked examples

Example 1 Finding north without a compass

Tie a bar magnet with a thread and let it hang freely. After it settles, the pole pointing towards geographic north is the magnet's north pole. Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet.

Example 2 The attraction test

A magnet picks up iron nails and steel pins but not copper coins or aluminium foil. Attraction is a test for magnetic material, not for 'being a metal'.

Practice questions

Try each question before opening the answer.

  1. MCQ Which pair of poles attracts?
    A. North and southB. North and northC. South and southD. All pairs repel
    Show answer

    Answer: North and south

    Unlike poles attract, like poles repel.

  2. MCQ Which of these is NOT attracted by a magnet?
    A. Copper coinB. Iron nailC. Nickel pinD. Cobalt clip
    Show answer

    Answer: Copper coin

    Copper is not a magnetic material.

  3. Short answer How does a compass help in finding direction?
    Show answer

    Its magnetised needle aligns along Earth's magnetic field and points north–south.
