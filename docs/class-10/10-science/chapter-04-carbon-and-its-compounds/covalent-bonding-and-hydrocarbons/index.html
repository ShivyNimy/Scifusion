Covalent Bonding and Hydrocarbons

Carbon has four valence electrons and forms four covalent bonds. It bonds with itself and many elements, giving millions of compounds.

2 worked examples · 3 practice questions

Key points

  • Covalent bonds form by sharing electrons; carbon forms single, double and triple bonds.
  • Carbon shows catenation (self-linking) and tetravalency.
  • Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen: alkanes, alkenes and alkynes.
  • Alkanes (CnH2n+2) are saturated; alkenes (CnH2n) and alkynes (CnH2n−2) are unsaturated.
  • Structural isomers have the same formula but different arrangements.

Worked examples

Example 1 Same formula, different molecules

Butane and isobutane both have the formula C4H10 but different branching patterns, so they are structural isomers with different properties.

Example 2 Why there are so many carbon compounds

Carbon can form long chains, branches and rings and bond to O, N, S and halogens, producing an enormous variety of compounds.

  1. MCQ The general formula of alkanes is:
    A. CnH2n+2B. CnH2nC. CnH2n−2D. CnHn
    Show answer

    Answer: CnH2n+2

    Saturated hydrocarbons have the maximum number of hydrogen atoms.

  2. MCQ Which bond is present in ethene?
    A. Double bondB. Single bondC. Triple bondD. Ionic bond
    Show answer

    Answer: Double bond

    Ethene has a C=C double bond.

  3. Short answer What is catenation?
    Show answer

    The ability of an element, especially carbon, to form long chains by bonding with itself.
