1.1 Coulomb's Law and Electric Field

Charged objects exert forces described by Coulomb's law. The electric field describes the force per unit charge at a point.

Key points

  • Coulomb's law: F = kq1q2/r2, with k = 9 × 109 N·m2/C2.
  • Electric field E = F/q = kq/r2, a vector pointing away from positive charge.
  • Field lines point from positive to negative and never cross.
  • Superposition: the net field is the vector sum of individual fields.
  • Electric flux φ = E·A cos θ, measured in N·m2/C.

Examples

Force between two charges. Two 1 μC charges 1 m apart repel with F = 9 × 109 × 10−6 × 10−6/1 = 9 × 10−3 N.

Electric field of a point charge. At 0.3 m from a 2 μC charge, E = 9 × 109 × 2 × 10−6/(0.3)2 = 2 × 105 N/C.

Practice

  1. Q. The SI unit of electric field is: Ans. N/C. Electric field is force per unit charge.
  2. Q. Electric field lines due to a positive charge point: Ans. Away from the charge. Field lines point away from positive charges.
  3. Q. State Coulomb's law. Ans. The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance.

1.2 Electric Dipole and Gauss's Law

A dipole consists of two equal and opposite charges. Gauss's law relates electric flux through a closed surface to enclosed charge.

Key points

  • Dipole moment p = q × 2a, directed from −q to +q.
  • Torque on a dipole in a uniform field: τ = pE sin θ.
  • Gauss's law: φ = qenclosed/ε0.
  • The field inside a conductor is zero in electrostatic equilibrium.
  • Field due to an infinite sheet: E = σ/(2ε0); inside a spherical shell: zero.

Examples

Field of a charged sheet. A sheet with σ = 10−6 C/m2 produces E = 10−6/(2 × 8.85 × 10−12) ≈ 5.6 × 104 N/C, independent of distance.

Torque on a dipole. A dipole of moment 2 × 10−6 C·m in a field of 104 N/C at 90° feels τ = 0.02 N·m.

Practice

  1. Q. Gauss's law relates electric flux to: Ans. Enclosed charge. Flux depends only on the charge inside the closed surface.
  2. Q. The electric field inside a charged conductor is: Ans. Zero. Charges reside on the surface in equilibrium.
  3. Q. Why does a charged spherical shell produce no field inside? Ans. By Gauss's law no charge is enclosed by an interior surface, so the flux and the field are zero.

2.1 Potential and Potential Energy

Electric potential is potential energy per unit charge. Equipotential surfaces simplify calculations of work.

Key points

  • Potential V = kq/r for a point charge; potential is a scalar.
  • Potential difference VAB = WAB/q.
  • Potential energy of two charges U = kq1q2/r.
  • Equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to field lines.
  • The field is the negative gradient of potential: E = −dV/dr.

Examples

Potential of a point charge. At 0.2 m from a 1 μC charge, V = 9 × 109 × 10−6/0.2 = 4.5 × 104 V.

Work in moving a charge. Moving 2 C through a potential difference of 10 V requires W = qV = 20 J.

Practice

  1. Q. Electric potential is a: Ans. Scalar. Potential has magnitude but no direction.
  2. Q. Equipotential surfaces are ______ to electric field lines. Ans. Perpendicular. No work is done moving along an equipotential surface.
  3. Q. Why is the potential inside a charged conductor constant? Ans. The field is zero inside, so no work is done moving a charge and the potential does not change.

2.2 Capacitors and Dielectrics

Capacitors store charge and energy. Their capacitance depends on geometry and the dielectric between plates.

Key points

  • Capacitance C = Q/V; the unit is the farad (F).
  • Parallel plate capacitor: C = ε0A/d; with dielectric C = Kε0A/d.
  • Energy stored U = ½CV2 = Q2/2C.
  • Series: 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2; parallel: C = C1 + C2.
  • A dielectric polarises and reduces the field, increasing capacitance.

Examples

Energy in a capacitor. A 10 μF capacitor charged to 100 V stores U = ½ × 10−5 × 104 = 0.5 J.

Effect of a dielectric. Inserting a dielectric with K = 4 multiplies the capacitance by 4 and stores more charge at the same voltage.

Practice

  1. Q. Capacitance is measured in: Ans. Farads. The farad is the unit of capacitance.
  2. Q. Energy stored in a capacitor is: Ans. ½CV2. U = ½CV2.
  3. Q. Why does a dielectric increase capacitance? Ans. It polarises and reduces the effective field between plates, allowing more charge at the same voltage.

3.1 Ohm's Law, Resistance and Drift Velocity

Current flows due to potential difference. Resistance depends on material, length and area.

Key points

  • Current I = dQ/dt; conventional current flows opposite to electron flow.
  • Ohm's law: V = IR at constant temperature.
  • Resistance R = ρL/A; resistivity ρ is a material property.
  • Drift velocity vd = I/(neA); typical values are very small.
  • Current density J = I/A = nevd.

Examples

Resistance of a wire. A copper wire of length 2 m, area 10−6 m2 and resistivity 1.7 × 10−8 Ω·m has R = 1.7 × 10−8 × 2/10−6 = 0.034 Ω.

Drift velocity. A 1 A current in a wire of area 10−6 m2 with n = 8 × 1028 m−3 has vd = 1/(8 × 1028 × 1.6 × 10−19 × 10−6) ≈ 7.8 × 10−5 m/s.

Practice

  1. Q. Resistance of a wire is directly proportional to: Ans. Length. R = ρL/A.
  2. Q. The SI unit of resistivity is: Ans. Ω·m. Resistivity has units of ohm-metre.
  3. Q. Why is drift velocity small even though lights turn on instantly? Ans. The electric field propagates near light speed, so all electrons start moving almost simultaneously.

3.2 Cells, EMF and Kirchhoff's Rules

Circuits are analysed with EMF, internal resistance and Kirchhoff's rules.

Key points

  • EMF ε is the maximum potential difference a cell can provide.
  • Terminal voltage V = ε − Ir, where r is internal resistance.
  • Kirchhoff's junction rule: sum of currents entering a junction equals sum leaving (charge conservation).
  • Kirchhoff's loop rule: sum of potential changes around a loop is zero (energy conservation).
  • A potentiometer and Wheatstone bridge measure EMF and resistance precisely.

Examples

Internal resistance. A cell of EMF 2 V with internal resistance 0.5 Ω delivering 1 A has terminal voltage 2 − 0.5 = 1.5 V.

Wheatstone bridge balance. The bridge is balanced when P/Q = R/S, so an unknown resistance can be found from three known ones.

Practice

  1. Q. Terminal voltage of a cell is: Ans. EMF minus Ir. Internal resistance causes a voltage drop.
  2. Q. Kirchhoff's junction rule is based on: Ans. Conservation of charge. Charge entering a junction must leave it.
  3. Q. What is the internal resistance of a cell? Ans. The resistance offered by the electrolyte and electrodes to current flow inside the cell.

4.1 Magnetic Force and Motion of Charges

Moving charges experience magnetic forces and produce magnetic fields. The force is perpendicular to velocity and field.

Key points

  • Force F = qvB sin θ; a charge at rest experiences no magnetic force.
  • A charge moving along the field direction feels no force.
  • Circular motion in a uniform field: r = mv/(qB).
  • Cyclotron frequency f = qB/(2πm).
  • Biot-Savart law gives the field of a current element.

Examples

Radius of a circular path. An electron with v = 106 m/s in B = 0.01 T has r = 9.1 × 10−31 × 106/(1.6 × 10−19 × 0.01) ≈ 5.7 × 10−4 m.

Why a charge follows a helix. If velocity has a component along the field, that component stays constant while the perpendicular component circles, producing a helical path.

Practice

  1. Q. A stationary charge in a magnetic field experiences: Ans. No force. Magnetic force requires motion.
  2. Q. Radius of circular motion in a magnetic field is: Ans. mv/(qB). r = mv/(qB).
  3. Q. Why does a magnetic force do no work on a moving charge? Ans. The force is always perpendicular to velocity, so it changes direction but not speed.

4.2 Current-Carrying Conductors and Magnetism

Current-carrying wires produce magnetic fields and experience forces in external fields. This is the basis of motors and meters.

Key points

  • Field of a long straight wire: B = μ0I/(2πr).
  • Field inside a solenoid: B = μ0nI.
  • Force on a wire: F = BIL sin θ.
  • Two parallel wires attract if currents are in the same direction.
  • A moving-coil galvanometer uses torque on a coil in a magnetic field.

Examples

Field near a wire. A 10 A current at 0.05 m gives B = 4π × 10−7 × 10/(2π × 0.05) = 4 × 10−5 T.

Motor principle. A current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences torque, making it rotate — the basis of an electric motor.

Practice

  1. Q. The magnetic field inside a long solenoid is: Ans. μ0nI. The field is uniform and equals μ0nI.
  2. Q. Two parallel wires with currents in the same direction: Ans. Attract. Each wire's field exerts an attractive force on the other.
  3. Q. State Ampere's circuital law. Ans. The line integral of the magnetic field around a closed loop equals μ0 times the total current through the loop.

5.1 Bar Magnets and Earth's Magnetism

Magnetic materials interact through fields. A bar magnet behaves like a magnetic dipole, and Earth has its own magnetic field.

Key points

  • A magnetic dipole has a magnetic moment m from south to north pole.
  • Torque τ = mB sin θ; potential energy U = −mB cos θ.
  • Earth's magnetic field has components: declination, dip and horizontal component.
  • Magnetic field lines emerge from the north pole and enter the south pole.
  • Magnetic materials align along field lines.

Examples

Compass needle. The needle aligns with the horizontal component of Earth's field, pointing toward magnetic north.

Magnetic torque. A magnet of moment 0.5 A·m2 in a 0.2 T field at 30° experiences τ = 0.5 × 0.2 × 0.5 = 0.05 N·m.

Practice

  1. Q. The magnetic moment of a magnet is directed from: Ans. South to north pole. The magnetic moment points from the south to the north pole inside the magnet.
  2. Q. The angle of dip at the magnetic equator is: Ans. 0°. The field is horizontal at the magnetic equator.
  3. Q. What is the angle of declination? Ans. The angle between magnetic north and geographic north at a place.

5.2 Magnetic Materials and Hysteresis

Materials respond differently to magnetic fields: diamagnetic, paramagnetic and ferromagnetic. Hysteresis affects energy loss in transformers.

Key points

  • Diamagnetic materials are weakly repelled by magnetic fields.
  • Paramagnetic materials are weakly attracted; alignment is lost when the field is removed.
  • Ferromagnetic materials can be strongly magnetised and retain magnetism (domains).
  • Soft iron has a narrow hysteresis loop and is used in electromagnets.
  • Steel has a wide loop, retaining magnetism — used for permanent magnets.

Examples

Why soft iron for transformer cores. Its narrow hysteresis loop means little energy is lost as heat during repeated magnetisation cycles.

Why steel holds magnetism. Domain walls resist movement in steel, so the magnetisation remains after the external field is removed.

Practice

  1. Q. Ferromagnetic materials: Ans. Are strongly attracted to magnets. They have domains that align strongly with fields.
  2. Q. A material with a narrow hysteresis loop is good for: Ans. Electromagnet cores. Low hysteresis loss suits repeated magnetisation.
  3. Q. What is hysteresis? Ans. The lag between magnetisation and the applied magnetic field in a ferromagnetic material.

6.1 Faraday's and Lenz's Laws

Changing magnetic flux induces an EMF. Lenz's law gives the direction of the induced current.

Key points

  • Magnetic flux φ = BA cos θ.
  • Faraday's law: induced EMF ε = −dφ/dt.
  • Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change producing it.
  • A changing field or changing area can change flux.
  • Induced EMF appears in a conductor moving through a field: ε = Bvl.

Examples

Motional EMF. A rod 0.5 m long moving at 2 m/s perpendicular to a 0.1 T field develops ε = 0.1 × 2 × 0.5 = 0.1 V.

Dropping a magnet through a coil. The changing flux induces a current whose field opposes the magnet's motion, so the magnet falls more slowly.

Practice

  1. Q. Faraday's law says the induced EMF equals: Ans. Negative rate of change of flux. ε = −dφ/dt.
  2. Q. Lenz's law is a consequence of conservation of: Ans. Energy. The induced current opposes the change, conserving energy.
  3. Q. Why does a magnet fall slowly through a copper tube? Ans. The induced eddy currents create a field opposite to the magnet's motion, producing a retarding force.

6.2 Self and Mutual Induction

Changing current in a coil induces EMF in the same or a nearby coil. This is inductance.

Key points

  • Self-inductance ε = −L dI/dt; L is measured in henries (H).
  • Energy stored in an inductor U = ½LI2.
  • Mutual inductance M relates flux in one coil to current in another.
  • Inductors oppose changes in current, analogue to inertia.
  • Transformers use mutual induction to change AC voltage.

Examples

Energy in an inductor. A 2 H inductor carrying 3 A stores U = ½ × 2 × 9 = 9 J.

Transformer turns ratio. A transformer with 100 primary turns and 500 secondary turns steps 220 V up to 1100 V (ignoring losses).

Practice

  1. Q. The unit of inductance is the: Ans. Henry. Inductance is measured in henries.
  2. Q. Energy stored in an inductor is: Ans. ½LI2. U = ½LI2.
  3. Q. Why is mutual induction useful in transformers? Ans. Changing current in the primary creates a changing flux that induces a voltage in the secondary.

7.1 AC Circuits and Reactance

Alternating current varies sinusoidally. Resistors, capacitors and inductors respond differently to AC.

Key points

  • RMS values: Irms = I0/√2 and Vrms = V0/√2.
  • Inductive reactance XL = ωL; capacitive reactance XC = 1/(ωC).
  • Impedance Z = √(R2 + (XL − XC)2).
  • Resonance occurs when XL = XC at ω = 1/√(LC).
  • Power in AC: P = VrmsIrms cos φ, where cos φ is the power factor.

Examples

Impedance calculation. With R = 30 Ω, XL = 40 Ω and XC = 0, Z = √(900 + 1600) = 50 Ω, so Irms = 220/50 = 4.4 A.

Resonance in a radio. Tuning a capacitor changes the resonant frequency of an LC circuit so it matches the station.

Practice

  1. Q. Power factor is: Ans. cos φ. The power factor is the cosine of the phase angle.
  2. Q. At resonance in an LCR series circuit: Ans. Impedance is minimum. XL and XC cancel, leaving only R.
  3. Q. Calculate the RMS value of a sinusoidal current with peak 10 A. Ans. Irms = 10/√2 ≈ 7.07 A.

7.2 Transformers and Power Transmission

Transformers change AC voltages for efficient transmission of power over long distances.

Key points

  • A step-up transformer increases voltage; a step-down transformer decreases it.
  • Turns ratio: Vs/Vp = Ns/Np.
  • Power is roughly conserved: VpIp ≈ VsIs.
  • High-voltage transmission reduces I2R losses in cables.
  • Transformers work on AC and cannot change DC voltages.

Examples

Why high voltage for long lines. For the same power, raising voltage lowers current; losses I2R fall with the square of the current.

Step-down at home. Substations step 11 kV down to 220 V for homes, reducing voltage for safety.

Practice

  1. Q. A transformer works on: Ans. AC. It depends on changing flux, which AC provides.
  2. Q. High-voltage transmission reduces: Ans. I2R losses. Lower current means less resistive loss.
  3. Q. Why can a transformer not change DC voltage? Ans. DC produces a constant flux, so no EMF is induced in the secondary coil.

8.1 Displacement Current and EM Waves

Maxwell introduced displacement current, showing light is an electromagnetic wave. EM waves have electric and magnetic components oscillating perpendicular to each other.

Key points

  • Displacement current Id = ε0 dφE/dt completes Ampere's law.
  • EM waves are transverse; E and B are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation.
  • Speed of EM waves in vacuum c = 1/√(μ0ε0) ≈ 3 × 108 m/s.
  • In a medium v = c/n, where n is the refractive index.
  • EM waves carry energy and momentum and need no medium.

Examples

Why EM waves need no medium. Oscillating fields regenerate each other, so they propagate through vacuum, unlike sound.

Light through glass. Light slows in glass because its interaction with atoms gives a smaller effective speed, n > 1.

Practice

  1. Q. In an EM wave, E and B are: Ans. Perpendicular to each other. The E and B fields oscillate at right angles.
  2. Q. The speed of light in vacuum is about: Ans. 3 × 108 m/s. Light travels at about 3 × 108 m/s.
  3. Q. What is displacement current? Ans. A current-like term due to a changing electric field that produces a magnetic field, completing Ampere's law.

8.2 The Electromagnetic Spectrum

EM waves span radio to gamma rays. Wavelength determines energy and applications.

Key points

  • Radio waves: long wavelength, communication and broadcasting.
  • Microwaves: radar, microwave ovens and satellite communication.
  • Infrared: remote controls and thermal imaging.
  • Visible light: 400–700 nm; ultraviolet: sterilisation and vitamin D synthesis.
  • X-rays and gamma rays are ionising and used in imaging and cancer therapy.

Examples

Microwave oven. Microwaves match the natural frequency of water molecules, heating food from within.

Why UV is harmful. Ultraviolet photons carry enough energy to break chemical bonds, so they damage DNA and skin.

Practice

  1. Q. Which waves have the shortest wavelength? Ans. Gamma rays. Gamma rays have the highest frequency and shortest wavelength.
  2. Q. Microwave ovens work by: Ans. Heating water molecules. Microwaves excite water molecules, generating heat.
  3. Q. Why are X-rays used in medical imaging? Ans. They penetrate soft tissue but are absorbed by dense bone, producing contrast images.

9.1 Reflection, Refraction and Lenses

Light bends at surfaces and through lenses. Ray diagrams predict image position and size.

Key points

  • Refractive index n = c/v; Snell's law n1 sin i = n2 sin r.
  • Lens formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f; power P = 1/f in dioptres.
  • Magnification m = v/u.
  • Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from denser to rarer medium beyond the critical angle.
  • Optical fibres use total internal reflection for communication.

Examples

Critical angle for glass. For glass of n = 1.5, the critical angle is sin−1(1/1.5) ≈ 41.8°.

Power of a lens combination. Powers add: +2 D and +3 D lenses in contact give +5 D, so focal length 0.2 m.

Practice

  1. Q. Total internal reflection occurs when light goes from: Ans. Denser to rarer medium. It needs the denser-to-rarer case beyond the critical angle.
  2. Q. The power of a lens is measured in: Ans. Dioptres. Power = 1/f in metres.
  3. Q. Why does a diamond sparkle? Ans. Its high refractive index gives a small critical angle, so light undergoes total internal reflection many times.

9.2 Optical Instruments and the Eye

Microscopes and telescopes use lenses to magnify. The human eye is an adjustable optical system.

Key points

  • A simple microscope uses a convex lens to give angular magnification.
  • A compound microscope uses objective and eyepiece lenses.
  • An astronomical telescope produces a magnified image of distant objects.
  • The eye's accommodation adjusts for near and far vision.
  • Defects: myopia (concave lens), hypermetropia (convex lens), astigmatism (cylindrical lens).

Examples

Telescope magnification. M = fo/fe for a telescope at normal adjustment; a longer objective and shorter eyepiece give more magnification.

Why near objects strain the eye. The ciliary muscles must contract more to increase lens power, which tires the eye over time.

Practice

  1. Q. A myopic eye is corrected by a: Ans. Concave lens. A diverging lens moves the focus back to the retina.
  2. Q. Microscope magnification is increased by: Ans. Shorter focal length objective. Magnification is inversely related to focal length.
  3. Q. Why does a compound microscope use two lenses? Ans. The objective magnifies the specimen and the eyepiece magnifies that image further.

10.1 Interference and Young's Double Slit

Light behaves as a wave in interference and diffraction. Coherent sources produce a stable interference pattern.

Key points

  • Interference produces bright and dark fringes from path differences.
  • Path difference for bright fringe: Δ = nλ; for dark fringe: Δ = (2n − 1)λ/2.
  • Fringe width in YDSE: β = λD/d.
  • Coherent sources have constant phase difference and the same frequency.
  • Diffraction also confirms the wave nature of light.

Examples

Fringe width. With λ = 600 nm, D = 1 m and d = 0.5 mm, β = 6 × 10−7 × 1/(5 × 10−4) = 1.2 mm.

Why fringes are coloured in white light. Different wavelengths give different fringe widths and positions, so colours separate.

Practice

  1. Q. Fringe width in YDSE is directly proportional to: Ans. Wavelength. β = λD/d.
  2. Q. Coherent sources must have: Ans. Constant phase difference. Coherence is required for stable interference.
  3. Q. What is diffraction? Ans. The spreading of light around obstacles or edges, showing its wave nature.

10.2 Polarisation and Resolving Power

Polarisation shows light is transverse. Optical instruments have limits on resolution set by diffraction.

Key points

  • Polarisation restricts oscillations to one plane.
  • Brewster's law: tan θp = n gives the polarising angle.
  • Polaroids and polarising filters can block specific orientations.
  • Resolving power depends on wavelength and aperture diameter (Rayleigh criterion).
  • Polarised sunglasses reduce glare from horizontal surfaces.

Examples

Polarising sunglasses. Reflected glare is partly polarised horizontally; vertical polarising filters block it, reducing glare.

Brewster angle for glass. For n = 1.5, θp = tan−1(1.5) ≈ 56.3°, at which reflected light is fully polarised.

Practice

  1. Q. Polarisation shows light is: Ans. Transverse. Only transverse waves can be polarised.
  2. Q. Polaroid films work by: Ans. Blocking one polarisation direction. They transmit only oscillations along one axis.
  3. Q. What is Brewster's angle? Ans. The angle of incidence at which reflected light is completely polarised, given by tan θp = n.

11.1 Photoelectric Effect

Light behaves as particles called photons. The photoelectric effect shows that light of sufficient frequency ejects electrons.

Key points

  • A photon has energy E = hf = hc/λ.
  • Photoelectric emission needs frequency above a threshold, regardless of intensity.
  • Kinetic energy: Kmax = hf − φ, where φ is the work function.
  • Stopping potential V0: eV0 = Kmax.
  • Intensity affects the number of electrons, not their maximum energy.

Examples

Threshold frequency. A metal with work function 2 eV needs light above f = φ/h ≈ 4.8 × 1014 Hz.

Photon energy calculation. A 400 nm photon has E = hc/λ = 1240/400 ≈ 3.1 eV.

Practice

  1. Q. The photoelectric effect shows that light: Ans. Behaves as particles. Photons explain the threshold frequency.
  2. Q. Maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons depends on: Ans. Frequency of light. Kmax = hf − φ.
  3. Q. Why does increased intensity not increase maximum kinetic energy? Ans. Intensity increases the number of photons, not the energy per photon.

11.2 De Broglie Waves and Matter Waves

Particles also show wave behaviour. The de Broglie wavelength connects momentum and wavelength.

Key points

  • de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p = h/(mv).
  • Heavier or faster particles have shorter wavelengths.
  • Electron diffraction experiments confirm matter waves.
  • Matter waves explain the Bohr quantisation condition.
  • Wave-particle duality applies to both light and matter.

Examples

Electron wavelength. An electron accelerated through 100 V has λ ≈ 0.12 nm, comparable to atomic spacing, so crystals diffract electrons.

Why we do not see a cricket ball diffract. Its momentum is enormous, so its de Broglie wavelength is far too small to notice.

Practice

  1. Q. de Broglie wavelength is: Ans. h/p. Wavelength is inversely proportional to momentum.
  2. Q. A heavier particle has a ______ de Broglie wavelength. Ans. Shorter. λ = h/(mv), so larger mass means shorter wavelength.
  3. Q. Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of a 0.1 kg ball moving at 10 m/s. Ans. λ = h/(mv) = 6.6 × 10−34/(0.1 × 10) = 6.6 × 10−34 m, unmeasurably small.

12.1 Rutherford's Model and Bohr's Theory

Rutherford's scattering experiment revealed the nucleus. Bohr's model explained atomic spectra by quantising angular momentum.

Key points

  • Rutherford showed the atom is mostly empty space with a dense positive nucleus.
  • Bohr's postulates: electrons occupy discrete orbits and emit/absorb photons between levels.
  • Angular momentum is quantised: mvr = nh/2π.
  • Energy of hydrogen levels: En = −13.6/n2 eV.
  • The Bohr model explains the hydrogen spectrum but fails for multi-electron atoms.

Examples

Hydrogen transition. The n = 2 to n = 1 transition emits a photon of energy 13.6 − 3.4 = 10.2 eV, in the ultraviolet (Lyman series).

Radius of the first orbit. Bohr radius r1 ≈ 0.53 Å; radii scale as n2.

Practice

  1. Q. The energy of the hydrogen ground state is: Ans. −13.6 eV. E1 = −13.6 eV.
  2. Q. Bohr's model quantises: Ans. Angular momentum. mvr = nh/2π is the quantisation condition.
  3. Q. Why do atomic spectra consist of discrete lines? Ans. Electrons transition between discrete energy levels, emitting photons of specific energies.

12.2 Spectral Series and Lasers

Hydrogen spectra fall into series. Stimulated emission underlies lasers.

Key points

  • Lyman series: transitions to n = 1 (UV).
  • Balmer series: transitions to n = 2 (visible).
  • Paschen, Brackett and Pfund series lie in the infrared.
  • 1/λ = R(1/n12 − 1/n22) gives the Rydberg formula.
  • Lasers use population inversion and stimulated emission to produce coherent light.

Examples

Balmer line. The n = 3 to 2 transition gives the red H-alpha line at 656 nm.

Laser in surgery. Coherent, intense, monochromatic laser light can cut tissue precisely with minimal damage to surrounding areas.

Practice

  1. Q. The Balmer series lies mainly in the: Ans. Visible region. Transitions to n = 2 produce visible lines.
  2. Q. A laser produces light that is: Ans. Coherent. Laser light has a constant phase relationship.
  3. Q. State the Rydberg formula. Ans. 1/λ = R(1/n12 − 1/n22), giving wavelengths of hydrogen spectral lines.

13.1 Nuclear Structure and Binding Energy

Nuclei contain protons and neutrons. Mass defect and binding energy explain nuclear stability.

Key points

  • Atomic number Z = protons; mass number A = protons + neutrons.
  • Mass defect Δm = mass of nucleons − mass of nucleus.
  • Binding energy = Δm c2; binding energy per nucleon measures stability.
  • Iron-56 has near maximum binding energy per nucleon.
  • Nuclear forces are short-range and strong, overcoming proton repulsion.

Examples

Binding energy per nucleon. Iron-56 has about 8.8 MeV per nucleon, the highest among common nuclei, making it very stable.

Why heavy nuclei fission. Splitting a heavy nucleus increases binding energy per nucleon, releasing energy.

Practice

  1. Q. The mass number of a nucleus is: Ans. Protons + neutrons. Mass number counts nucleons.
  2. Q. Which nucleus has the highest binding energy per nucleon? Ans. Iron-56. Iron-56 is near the peak of the binding energy curve.
  3. Q. What is mass defect? Ans. The difference between the sum of masses of nucleons and the mass of the nucleus, appearing as binding energy.

13.2 Radioactivity and Nuclear Reactions

Unstable nuclei decay by alpha, beta or gamma emission. Fission and fusion release energy.

Key points

  • Alpha decay: emission of a helium nucleus; A decreases by 4 and Z by 2.
  • Beta decay: a neutron converts to a proton, emitting an electron and antineutrino.
  • Gamma decay: emission of a high-energy photon without change in A or Z.
  • Half-life is the time for half the nuclei to decay.
  • Fission splits heavy nuclei; fusion joins light nuclei.

Examples

Half-life calculation. After 3 half-lives, 1/8 of the original sample remains.

Carbon dating. Living things maintain a constant carbon-14 level; after death it decays with a 5730-year half-life, dating organic remains.

Practice

  1. Q. Alpha decay changes the mass number by: Ans. −4. An alpha particle removes 4 nucleons.
  2. Q. In beta-minus decay, a neutron becomes a: Ans. Proton. A neutron converts to a proton, emitting an electron.
  3. Q. Why does fusion release energy? Ans. Light nuclei combine into a more tightly bound nucleus, so the mass defect converts into energy.

14.1 Semiconductors and Doping

Semiconductors have conductivity between conductors and insulators, controlled by doping.

Key points

  • Silicon and germanium are the common semiconductors.
  • N-type semiconductors are doped with pentavalent atoms, adding electrons.
  • P-type semiconductors are doped with trivalent atoms, creating holes.
  • The depletion region forms at a p-n junction.
  • A p-n junction diode conducts in one direction.

Examples

Doping silicon. Adding phosphorus (5 valence electrons) creates free electrons, making n-type silicon.

Holes as current carriers. In p-type silicon, missing electrons behave as positive holes that move under an electric field.

Practice

  1. Q. N-type semiconductors have: Ans. Extra electrons. Pentavalent doping provides electrons.
  2. Q. A diode conducts when: Ans. Forward biased. Forward bias reduces the depletion barrier.
  3. Q. Why do p-n junctions act as one-way valves? Ans. They allow current in forward bias but block it in reverse bias.

14.2 Diodes, Rectifiers and Transistors

Semiconductor devices are used in power supply, amplification and digital electronics.

Key points

  • A half-wave rectifier uses one diode; a full-wave rectifier uses a bridge of four.
  • A Zener diode works in reverse breakdown to stabilise voltage.
  • A transistor has emitter, base and collector; a small base current controls a large collector current.
  • Transistors operate as amplifiers or switches.
  • Logic gates and integrated circuits are built from semiconductors.

Examples

Full-wave rectifier. Four diodes in a bridge convert both halves of the AC cycle into DC.

Transistor as a switch. A small base current turns on a larger collector current, allowing a tiny signal to control a large load.

Practice

  1. Q. A transistor has: Ans. Emitter, base and collector. These are the three regions of a bipolar junction transistor.
  2. Q. A Zener diode is used for: Ans. Voltage regulation. It stabilises voltage in reverse breakdown.
  3. Q. Explain why a transistor can amplify a signal. Ans. A small change in base current produces a much larger change in collector current.
