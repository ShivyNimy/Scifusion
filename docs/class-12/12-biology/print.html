1.1 Flower Structure and Pollination

Flowers contain male and female reproductive organs. Pollination transfers pollen and precedes fertilisation.

Key points

  • The stamen (androecium) produces pollen in the anther; the pistil (gynoecium) contains ovules.
  • Pollination may be self or cross, aided by wind, water, insects, birds or bats.
  • Wind-pollinated flowers make light, dry pollen in large amounts.
  • Insect-pollinated flowers offer nectar and have showy petals and scent.
  • Self-incompatibility prevents self-fertilisation in many plants.

Examples

Why wind-pollinated flowers lack bright petals. They do not need to attract insects; resources go into producing large amounts of pollen instead.

Fig and wasp mutualism. Fig wasps pollinate fig flowers while laying eggs inside them — a tight mutualistic partnership.

Practice

  1. Q. Pollen grains are produced in the: Ans. Anther. The anther contains pollen sacs.
  2. Q. Which agent pollinates colourful, scented flowers? Ans. Insects. Colour and scent attract insects.
  3. Q. Differentiate self-pollination and cross-pollination. Ans. Self-pollination transfers pollen within the same flower or plant; cross-pollination transfers pollen between different plants.

1.2 Double Fertilisation and Seed Development

Angiosperms show double fertilisation: one sperm fertilises the egg and another fuses with polar nuclei, forming endosperm.

Key points

  • After pollination, pollen germinates and a pollen tube carries male gametes to the ovule.
  • One gamete fuses with the egg to form the zygote; the other with two polar nuclei to form triploid endosperm.
  • The ovule becomes the seed and the ovary becomes the fruit.
  • Parthenocarpy is fruit development without fertilisation (seedless banana).
  • Apomixis produces seeds without fertilisation, preserving hybrid traits.

Examples

Coconut structure. The coconut's liquid endosperm nourishes the embryo during development, and the hard shell protects the seed.

Seedless grapes. Growers induce parthenocarpy with hormones so fruits develop without seeds.

Practice

  1. Q. Endosperm in angiosperms is: Ans. Triploid. Double fertilisation produces triploid endosperm.
  2. Q. A fruit develops from the: Ans. Ovary. The ovary wall becomes the fruit.
  3. Q. What is double fertilisation? Ans. Two male gametes fuse: one with the egg (zygote) and one with polar nuclei (endosperm).

2.1 Male and Female Reproductive Systems

Human reproduction involves specialised organs and gametes. Hormones control gamete production and the menstrual cycle.

Key points

  • The testes produce sperm and testosterone; sperm mature in the epididymis.
  • Ovaries produce eggs and oestrogen and progesterone.
  • Fertilisation occurs in the fallopian tube.
  • The menstrual cycle averages 28 days, with ovulation around day 14.
  • The placenta exchanges nutrients, gases and wastes.

Examples

Hormonal control of the cycle. FSH stimulates follicle growth, LH triggers ovulation, and progesterone maintains the uterine lining.

Why the placenta is essential. It links the foetal and maternal circulations without mixing blood, allowing exchange of gases and nutrients.

Practice

  1. Q. Fertilisation in humans occurs in the: Ans. Fallopian tube. Sperm meets the egg in the fallopian tube.
  2. Q. Ovulation is triggered by a surge of: Ans. LH. The LH surge induces ovulation.
  3. Q. What is the function of the corpus luteum? Ans. It secretes progesterone, which maintains the uterine lining for implantation.

2.2 Embryonic Development and Parturition

The zygote divides to form a blastocyst, which implants in the uterus. Development proceeds through trimesters until birth.

Key points

  • Cleavage produces a blastocyst that implants in the endometrium.
  • Gastrulation forms the three germ layers.
  • Organogenesis builds organs from the germ layers.
  • Oxytocin triggers uterine contractions during birth.
  • Lactation is stimulated by prolactin.

Examples

Identical twins. A single zygote splits into two embryos, so identical twins share the same genetic material.

Oxytocin in childbirth. Oxytocin causes strong uterine contractions and is also used medically to induce labour.

Practice

  1. Q. The zygote implants in the: Ans. Uterus. Implantation occurs in the uterine wall.
  2. Q. Which hormone initiates labour contractions? Ans. Oxytocin. Oxytocin stimulates uterine muscle contraction.
  3. Q. Why are identical twins genetically the same? Ans. They develop from a single fertilised egg that splits into two embryos.

3.1 Reproductive Health and Family Planning

Reproductive health means well-being in all aspects of reproduction. Family planning helps couples decide the number and timing of children.

Key points

  • Reproductive health includes safe motherhood, contraception and prevention of STIs.
  • Contraceptive methods include barrier, hormonal, IUD and surgical methods.
  • Condoms also protect against sexually transmitted infections.
  • Awareness and counselling are essential for reproductive health.
  • Lactational amenorrhea is a natural, temporary method of birth control.

Examples

Why condoms are dual-purpose. They prevent pregnancy and reduce the transmission of infections such as HIV.

IUD action. Copper IUDs release copper ions that affect sperm motility and prevent fertilisation.

Practice

  1. Q. Which contraceptive method also prevents STIs? Ans. Condoms. Barrier methods block both sperm and pathogens.
  2. Q. Reproductive health includes: Ans. Physical, mental and social well-being. It is a broad state of well-being related to reproduction.
  3. Q. Why is counselling important for reproductive health? Ans. It helps people make informed, safe choices and avoid misinformation and infection.

3.2 Infertility, ART and Prenatal Care

Assisted reproductive technologies help couples with infertility. Prenatal care protects mother and child.

Key points

  • Infertility may be caused by hormonal, anatomical or other factors in either partner.
  • ART includes IVF, ZIFT, GIFT and surrogacy.
  • IVF fertilises eggs outside the body and transfers the embryo to the uterus.
  • Prenatal diagnosis detects genetic disorders but raises ethical issues when misused.
  • Good nutrition, avoiding alcohol and tobacco and regular check-ups improve outcomes.

Examples

IVF procedure. Eggs are collected, fertilised with sperm in the laboratory, and the resulting embryo is transferred to the uterus.

Why folic acid matters. Adequate folic acid before and during early pregnancy reduces the risk of neural tube defects.

Practice

  1. Q. IVF stands for: Ans. In vitro fertilisation. Fertilisation occurs outside the body in a dish.
  2. Q. Which is used to detect genetic disorders before birth? Ans. Prenatal diagnosis. Prenatal tests examine foetal cells or fluid.
  3. Q. What is surrogacy? Ans. An arrangement in which a woman carries and delivers a child for another couple or person.

4.1 Mendelian Genetics and Deviations

Inheritance follows patterns described by Mendel. Some traits deviate from simple dominance.

Key points

  • Law of dominance, segregation and independent assortment describe Mendelian inheritance.
  • Incomplete dominance blends traits (pink flowers in snapdragon).
  • Codominance expresses both alleles (AB blood group).
  • Multiple alleles exist for blood groups (IA, IB, i).
  • Pleiotropy: one gene affects many traits.

Examples

Blood group inheritance. A child of parents with blood groups A (IAi) and B (IBi) can be AB (IAIB), A, B or O.

Incomplete dominance. Red and white snapdragons cross to give pink F1 flowers because neither allele completely dominates.

Practice

  1. Q. In incomplete dominance, the F1 phenotype is: Ans. Intermediate. Neither allele masks the other fully.
  2. Q. AB blood group is an example of: Ans. Codominance. Both A and B antigens are expressed.
  3. Q. State Mendel's law of independent assortment. Ans. Genes for different traits are inherited independently of each other during gamete formation.

4.2 Linkage, Sex Determination and Genetic Disorders

Genes on the same chromosome are linked. Sex-linked traits and chromosomal abnormalities show characteristic inheritance patterns.

Key points

  • Linked genes tend to be inherited together.
  • Sex determination in humans is XY: males XY, females XX.
  • Haemophilia and colour blindness are X-linked recessive disorders.
  • Down syndrome is caused by trisomy of chromosome 21.
  • Pedigree analysis traces inheritance through families.

Examples

Haemophilia inheritance. A carrier mother (XHXh) has a 50% chance of an affected son (XhY) with each pregnancy.

Down syndrome. An extra copy of chromosome 21 causes developmental differences; risk increases with maternal age.

Practice

  1. Q. Colour blindness is: Ans. X-linked recessive. The gene is on the X chromosome.
  2. Q. Down syndrome is due to: Ans. Trisomy of chromosome 21. Three copies of chromosome 21 cause the condition.
  3. Q. Why are more males affected by X-linked recessive disorders? Ans. Males have only one X chromosome, so a single recessive allele is expressed.

5.1 DNA Structure, Replication and Transcription

DNA stores genetic information. Replication copies it, and transcription produces RNA.

Key points

  • DNA is a double helix with antiparallel strands and complementary base pairing.
  • Semi-conservative replication produces two molecules, each with one parental strand.
  • Transcription uses one DNA strand as a template for mRNA.
  • In eukaryotes, mRNA is processed (splicing, capping, poly-A tail).
  • The genetic code is triplet, degenerate and nearly universal.

Examples

Semi-conservative replication. Meselson and Stahl's experiment with isotope-labelled DNA proved each daughter molecule has one old and one new strand.

Why the genetic code is degenerate. Most amino acids are specified by more than one codon, which buffers against mutations.

Practice

  1. Q. DNA replication is: Ans. Semi-conservative. Each new DNA molecule keeps one parental strand.
  2. Q. A codon consists of: Ans. Three nucleotides. Codons are triplets of bases.
  3. Q. Why is DNA a better genetic material than RNA? Ans. Its double-stranded structure allows accurate replication and repair, and its stability protects information.

5.2 Translation, Regulation and the Human Genome

Genes are expressed through translation. Gene regulation controls when proteins are made.

Key points

  • Translation uses ribosomes, tRNA and mRNA to assemble amino acids.
  • The lac operon is a classic model of gene regulation in bacteria.
  • Regulation can occur at transcription, processing, translation or protein level.
  • The Human Genome Project mapped the sequence of human DNA.
  • Mutations change DNA sequence and may alter protein function.

Examples

Lac operon. Lactose binds the repressor, allowing RNA polymerase to express genes for lactose metabolism only when lactose is present.

Human Genome Project finding. Only about 1.5–2% of the human genome codes for proteins, revealing many regulatory sequences.

Practice

  1. Q. Translation occurs on the: Ans. Ribosome. Ribosomes translate mRNA into protein.
  2. Q. The lac operon is induced by: Ans. Lactose. Lactose inactivates the repressor.
  3. Q. What is a mutation? Ans. A change in the DNA sequence that may alter the protein produced.

6.1 Evidence and Mechanisms of Evolution

Evolution is change in populations over time. Evidence comes from fossils, comparative anatomy and molecular data.

Key points

  • Fossils document extinct forms and transitional species.
  • Homologous structures indicate common ancestry; analogous structures indicate convergent evolution.
  • Natural selection is the main mechanism of adaptive evolution.
  • Mutation, recombination and migration generate genetic variation.
  • Genetic drift changes allele frequencies in small populations.

Examples

Darwin's finches. Different beak shapes on the Galápagos Islands reflect adaptation to different food sources.

Antibiotic resistance. Bacteria with resistance survive treatment and reproduce, so resistance spreads rapidly.

Practice

  1. Q. Homologous organs indicate: Ans. Common ancestry. Similar structure in different species suggests shared origin.
  2. Q. The main mechanism of adaptive evolution is: Ans. Natural selection. Selection favours traits that improve survival and reproduction.
  3. Q. How does antibiotic resistance illustrate natural selection? Ans. Resistant bacteria survive treatment and reproduce, so the resistant trait increases in the population.

6.2 Speciation and Human Evolution

Speciation occurs when populations become reproductively isolated. Human evolution is traced through fossil hominids.

Key points

  • Allopatric speciation occurs when populations are geographically separated.
  • Reproductive isolation prevents gene flow between diverging populations.
  • Adaptive radiation produces many species from a common ancestor.
  • Hominid evolution includes Australopithecus, Homo habilis and Homo sapiens.
  • Molecular clocks estimate divergence times.

Examples

Marsupial radiation. In Australia, marsupials diversified into many forms filling niches occupied elsewhere by placental mammals.

Homo sapiens origins. Fossil and genetic evidence places the origin of modern humans in Africa, with later migration across the world.

Practice

  1. Q. Reproductive isolation leads to: Ans. Speciation. Isolation stops gene flow and allows divergence.
  2. Q. Adaptive radiation produces: Ans. Many species from one ancestor. Many species diverge to fill different niches.
  3. Q. What is allopatric speciation? Ans. Speciation caused by geographic separation of populations, leading to reproductive isolation.

7.1 Pathogens, Immunity and Vaccines

Pathogens cause disease. The immune system defends the body through innate and acquired immunity.

Key points

  • Innate immunity is non-specific and present from birth.
  • Acquired immunity is specific and develops after exposure.
  • Antibodies are produced by B cells; T cells kill infected cells.
  • Active immunity develops after infection or vaccination; passive immunity is transferred.
  • Vaccines train the immune system without causing disease.

Examples

How a vaccine works. A weakened or inactivated pathogen triggers antibody production and memory cells, giving rapid protection on future exposure.

Why colostrum matters. Breast milk contains antibodies that provide passive immunity to newborns.

Practice

  1. Q. Antibodies are produced by: Ans. B cells. B lymphocytes produce antibodies.
  2. Q. Vaccination provides: Ans. Active acquired immunity. The immune system actively responds to the vaccine.
  3. Q. Differentiate active and passive immunity. Ans. Active immunity is produced by the body after exposure; passive immunity is received ready-made from another source.

7.2 Major Diseases and Their Prevention

Diseases differ in transmission and control. HIV, malaria, cancer and drug abuse are major health concerns.

Key points

  • HIV attacks helper T cells, weakening immunity and leading to AIDS.
  • Malaria is transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes.
  • Cancer is uncontrolled cell division caused by genetic and environmental factors.
  • Drug and alcohol abuse damages health and society; awareness and support are important.
  • Prevention includes vaccination, sanitation, safe sex and vector control.

Examples

Why HIV leads to AIDS. HIV destroys helper T cells, so the immune system loses coordination and opportunistic infections appear.

Malaria control. Using mosquito nets, draining stagnant water and prompt treatment reduce malaria transmission.

Practice

  1. Q. Malaria is transmitted by: Ans. Female Anopheles mosquito. Anopheles mosquitoes transmit the malaria parasite.
  2. Q. HIV primarily attacks: Ans. Helper T cells. Loss of helper T cells cripples immunity.
  3. Q. Why is cancer called a disease of uncontrolled cell division? Ans. Control mechanisms fail, so cells divide continuously and can spread to other tissues.

8.1 Microbes in Food, Industry and Medicine

Microorganisms are used to make food, medicines and industrial products and to treat waste.

Key points

  • Fermentation by yeast makes bread, beer and wine; Lactobacillus makes curd and cheese.
  • Antibiotics such as penicillin come from microorganisms.
  • Microbes produce enzymes, organic acids, vitamins and biofuels.
  • Biogas is produced by anaerobic digestion of waste.
  • Vaccines and recombinant proteins are produced using microbes.

Examples

Curd and cheese. Lactobacillus converts milk sugar to lactic acid, curdling the protein and giving curd its tang.

Biogas plant. Methanogens digest cattle dung and produce methane-rich biogas used for cooking.

Practice

  1. Q. Which microorganism is used in bread making? Ans. Yeast. Yeast ferments sugars and releases CO2, raising the dough.
  2. Q. Biogas is produced by: Ans. Methanogens. Methanogenic bacteria generate methane in anaerobic conditions.
  3. Q. Name two food products made using microorganisms. Ans. Curd (Lactobacillus) and bread (yeast).

8.2 Microbes in Agriculture, Waste Treatment and Biocontrol

Microbes improve soil fertility, break down waste and control pests.

Key points

  • Rhizobium fixes nitrogen in legume roots.
  • Mycorrhiza improves phosphorus uptake in plants.
  • Sewage is treated by aerobic and anaerobic bacteria.
  • Biocontrol agents such as Bacillus thuringiensis and Trichoderma control pests.
  • Biofertilisers reduce dependence on chemical fertilisers.

Examples

Biofertiliser. Rhizobium inoculants added to legume seeds increase nitrogen fixation and yield.

Bt cotton. The bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis provides a gene that protects cotton from bollworms.

Practice

  1. Q. Rhizobium helps plants by: Ans. Fixing nitrogen. It fixes atmospheric nitrogen in nodules.
  2. Q. Which is a biocontrol agent? Ans. Trichoderma. Trichoderma is a fungus used against plant pathogens.
  3. Q. How does sewage treatment use microorganisms? Ans. Aerobic and anaerobic bacteria decompose organic matter, reducing BOD and producing cleaner water.

9.1 Recombinant DNA Technology

Biotechnology uses living systems to make useful products. Recombinant DNA technology joins DNA from different sources.

Key points

  • Restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific sequences.
  • DNA ligase joins DNA fragments.
  • Plasmids act as vectors to carry genes into host cells.
  • The polymerase chain reaction amplifies DNA exponentially.
  • Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size.

Examples

Making insulin. The human insulin gene is inserted into a plasmid, put into bacteria, and the bacteria produce human insulin.

PCR in diagnostics. PCR amplifies tiny amounts of DNA, so infections and genetic conditions can be detected early.

Practice

  1. Q. Restriction enzymes cut DNA at: Ans. Specific recognition sequences. They recognise specific base sequences.
  2. Q. PCR is used to: Ans. Amplify DNA. Polymerase chain reaction multiplies DNA copies.
  3. Q. What is a plasmid? Ans. A small circular DNA molecule used as a vector to carry foreign genes into bacteria.

9.2 Cloning, Bioreactors and Safety

Genetic engineering raises opportunities and ethical questions. Safety and regulation are essential.

Key points

  • A clone is genetically identical to its parent organism.
  • Bioreactors provide controlled conditions for large-scale culture.
  • Downstream processing purifies the product.
  • Genetic engineering requires biosafety and ethical guidelines.
  • GM organisms must be assessed for environmental and health effects.

Examples

Dolly the sheep. Dolly was produced by somatic cell nuclear transfer, showing that differentiated nuclei can be reprogrammed.

Regulation of GM crops. GM crops are evaluated for safety before release, because gene flow and ecological effects need careful assessment.

Practice

  1. Q. A clone is: Ans. Genetically identical to the parent. Clones carry the same genetic material.
  2. Q. Downstream processing refers to: Ans. Purifying the product after reaction. It covers separation and purification of the product.
  3. Q. Why are ethical guidelines important in biotechnology? Ans. They protect safety, privacy and fairness while enabling beneficial applications.

10.1 Agriculture and Medicine

Biotechnology improves crops, animals and human health.

Key points

  • GM crops have traits such as pest resistance, herbicide tolerance and improved nutrition.
  • Bt crops carry a bacterial gene for pest resistance.
  • Golden rice was developed to increase vitamin A content.
  • Recombinant insulin, vaccines and gene therapy have medical applications.
  • Molecular diagnosis detects diseases at an early stage.

Examples

Recombinant insulin. Human insulin produced in bacteria is purer and more consistent than insulin extracted from animals.

Gene therapy. A functional gene is delivered to cells to compensate for a defective one in certain genetic disorders.

Practice

  1. Q. Bt crops are resistant to: Ans. Insect pests. They carry a gene from Bacillus thuringiensis that kills specific insects.
  2. Q. Golden rice is enriched in: Ans. Vitamin A. It was developed to combat vitamin A deficiency.
  3. Q. Give one medical application of biotechnology. Ans. Production of human insulin by recombinant bacteria for treating diabetes.

10.2 Transgenic Animals and Ethical Issues

Transgenic animals are used for research, medicine and food, raising ethical concerns.

Key points

  • Transgenic animals carry foreign genes in their genome.
  • They are used to study diseases, produce medicines and improve livestock.
  • Cloning and transgenics require strict ethical review.
  • Biosafety and informed consent are important principles.
  • Benefits must be balanced against animal welfare and environmental risks.

Examples

Transgenic sheep. Sheep engineered to produce therapeutic proteins in their milk are used to make medicines.

Ethical review boards. Research involving animals must be approved by committees that weigh scientific value and welfare.

Practice

  1. Q. A transgenic animal: Ans. Carries a foreign gene. A foreign gene has been inserted into its genome.
  2. Q. Biosafety guidelines protect: Ans. Human health and the environment. They reduce risks from GM organisms.
  3. Q. Why is animal welfare a concern in biotechnology? Ans. Genetic modification and cloning can affect animal health and involve ethical trade-offs.

11.1 Population Characteristics and Growth

Populations have size, density, age structure and growth patterns that ecologists model.

Key points

  • Population density = number ÷ area or volume.
  • Exponential growth occurs under unlimited resources: dN/dt = rN.
  • Logistic growth includes carrying capacity K: dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K).
  • Age pyramids show the proportion of young, reproductive and old individuals.
  • Population interactions include competition, predation, parasitism and mutualism.

Examples

Bacteria in a culture. Under ideal conditions, bacteria double every generation, showing exponential growth until resources run out.

Predator-prey cycles. Lynx and hare populations oscillate, with predator numbers lagging behind prey numbers.

Practice

  1. Q. Logistic growth accounts for: Ans. Carrying capacity. Growth slows as N approaches K.
  2. Q. Population density is: Ans. Individuals per unit area. Density is numbers per unit area or volume.
  3. Q. Differentiate exponential and logistic growth. Ans. Exponential growth assumes unlimited resources; logistic growth includes a carrying capacity that slows growth.

11.2 Species Interactions and Adaptations

Organisms interact with each other and their environment, shaping distributions and adaptations.

Key points

  • Competition occurs when resources are limited.
  • Predation controls prey populations and drives adaptations.
  • Parasitism benefits one organism at the expense of another.
  • Mutualism benefits both partners, as in lichens and mycorrhizae.
  • Adaptations allow organisms to survive extremes of temperature and water.

Examples

Mycorrhiza. Fungi extend the root's reach for water and phosphorus while receiving sugars from the plant.

Desert adaptations. Nocturnal habits, water storage and reduced leaves help desert plants and animals survive.

Practice

  1. Q. Mycorrhiza is an example of: Ans. Mutualism. Both fungus and plant benefit.
  2. Q. Predation typically: Ans. Controls prey population size. Predators reduce prey numbers and shape adaptations.
  3. Q. Why are mutualistic relationships important in ecosystems? Ans. They improve survival, nutrition and reproduction for both partners, supporting ecosystem stability.

12.1 Energy Flow and Nutrient Cycling

Ecosystems have producers, consumers and decomposers. Energy flows one way; nutrients cycle.

Key points

  • Solar energy is captured by producers through photosynthesis.
  • Only about 10% of energy passes to the next trophic level.
  • Ecological pyramids show numbers, biomass or energy at each level.
  • Nutrients cycle through biogeochemical cycles (carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus).
  • Decomposers return nutrients to the soil.

Examples

Energy pyramid. A pyramid of energy is always upright because energy decreases at each level.

Carbon cycle. Photosynthesis removes CO2 and respiration and combustion return it, cycling carbon through living and non-living reservoirs.

Practice

  1. Q. Energy transfer between trophic levels is about: Ans. 10%. The ten per cent law governs energy flow.
  2. Q. A pyramid of energy is always: Ans. Upright. Energy always decreases upward.
  3. Q. Why is the number of trophic levels limited? Ans. So little energy remains after several transfers that further levels cannot be supported.

12.2 Ecological Succession and Ecosystem Services

Ecosystems change over time through succession and provide services essential for humans.

Key points

  • Primary succession begins on bare rock; secondary succession follows disturbance.
  • Pioneer species modify the habitat, enabling later species.
  • Climax communities are relatively stable end stages.
  • Ecosystem services include pollination, water purification and climate regulation.
  • Biodiversity increases ecosystem resilience.

Examples

Lichens on bare rock. Lichens are pioneer species that break down rock and add organic matter, preparing the way for mosses and grasses.

Wetland services. Wetlands filter pollutants, store floodwater and recharge groundwater — free services to society.

Practice

  1. Q. Pioneer species in primary succession on rock are: Ans. Lichens. Lichens can colonise bare rock.
  2. Q. Ecosystem services include: Ans. Pollination and water purification. These are benefits ecosystems provide to humans.
  3. Q. What is ecological succession? Ans. The gradual, predictable change in community composition over time toward a stable climax.

13.1 Levels and Patterns of Biodiversity

Biodiversity includes genetic, species and ecosystem diversity. It is unevenly distributed and threatened.

Key points

  • Species diversity is highest in the tropics.
  • The species-area relationship shows more species in larger areas.
  • Biodiversity hotspots are regions of high endemism and high threat.
  • Losses are driven by habitat destruction, overexploitation, invasive species and pollution.
  • Extinction is a natural process, but human activity accelerates it.

Examples

Species-area curve. Larger islands and habitat patches usually hold more species, following a log relationship.

Invasive species. Water hyacinth spreads rapidly in Indian water bodies, crowding out native plants and harming aquatic life.

Practice

  1. Q. Which region has the greatest species diversity? Ans. Tropics. Tropical regions hold the highest species richness.
  2. Q. A biodiversity hotspot has: Ans. High endemism and high threat. Hotspots combine richness with vulnerability.
  3. Q. Name three causes of biodiversity loss. Ans. Habitat destruction, overexploitation and invasive species (also pollution and climate change).

13.2 Conservation Strategies

Conservation protects biodiversity in place (in situ) and outside habitats (ex situ).

Key points

  • In situ conservation: national parks, sanctuaries and biosphere reserves.
  • Ex situ conservation: zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks and cryopreservation.
  • Community participation makes conservation sustainable.
  • International agreements and national laws protect species and habitats.
  • Sustainable use balances conservation with human needs.

Examples

Seed banks. Seeds stored at low temperature preserve genetic diversity of crops and wild plants for future generations.

Community reserves. Local communities protect sacred groves and community forests, conserving biodiversity with traditional knowledge.

Practice

  1. Q. A national park is an example of: Ans. In situ conservation. Protected areas conserve species in their natural habitat.
  2. Q. A seed bank is an example of: Ans. Ex situ conservation. Seeds are stored outside the natural habitat.
  3. Q. Why is community participation important for conservation? Ans. Local people depend on and manage ecosystems, so their involvement makes protection practical and lasting.
