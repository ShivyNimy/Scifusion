1.1 Concentration Terms and Raoult's Law

Solutions are homogeneous mixtures. Concentration can be expressed as molarity, molality or mole fraction, and vapour pressure depends on composition.

Key points

  • Molarity M = moles of solute per litre of solution; molality m = moles per kg of solvent.
  • Mole fraction xi = ni/Σn; the sum of mole fractions is 1.
  • Raoult's law: partial vapour pressure pi = xi pi°.
  • Ideal solutions obey Raoult's law and have zero enthalpy of mixing.
  • Positive deviation gives higher vapour pressure; negative gives lower.

Examples

Molality calculation. 10 g of NaCl (M = 58.5) in 100 g of water gives 0.171 mol in 0.1 kg, so m = 1.71 mol/kg.

Raoult's law. A mixture with xbenzene = 0.4 and pbenzene° = 75 mmHg has pbenzene = 30 mmHg.

Practice

  1. Q. Which concentration term is independent of temperature? Ans. Molality. Molality uses mass of solvent, which does not change with temperature.
  2. Q. An ideal solution has enthalpy of mixing: Ans. Zero. No heat is evolved or absorbed on mixing ideal solutions.
  3. Q. State Raoult's law. Ans. The partial vapour pressure of each component is proportional to its mole fraction in the solution.

1.2 Colligative Properties

Colligative properties depend on the number of solute particles: vapour pressure lowering, boiling point elevation, freezing point depression and osmotic pressure.

Key points

  • ΔTb = Kbm; ΔTf = Kfm.
  • Osmotic pressure π = CRT for dilute solutions.
  • Van't Hoff factor i accounts for association or dissociation.
  • Colligative properties are used to determine molar mass.
  • Winter salt spreading lowers the freezing point of water.

Examples

Freezing point depression. Adding 1 mol of a non-electrolyte to 1 kg water gives ΔTf = 1.86 °C, so the freezing point falls to −1.86 °C.

Van't Hoff factor. NaCl dissociates into two ions, so i ≈ 2 and the colligative effect nearly doubles.

Practice

  1. Q. Osmotic pressure is given by: Ans. π = CRT. π = CRT for dilute solutions.
  2. Q. Which property is NOT colligative? Ans. Viscosity. Viscosity depends on the nature of the solute, not just the number of particles.
  3. Q. Why does adding salt lower the freezing point of water? Ans. The solute particles disrupt the crystal lattice, so more energy must be removed before ice forms.

2.1 Electrochemical Cells and EMF

Electrochemistry connects chemical reactions and electrical energy. Galvanic cells produce electricity; electrolytic cells consume it.

Key points

  • In a galvanic cell, oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode.
  • E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode.
  • A positive E°cell means the reaction is spontaneous.
  • The Nernst equation gives cell potential at non-standard conditions.
  • A salt bridge maintains electrical neutrality.

Examples

Daniel cell. Zn|Zn2+ || Cu2+|Cu has E° = 0.34 − (−0.76) = 1.10 V, so it is spontaneous.

Nernst equation. E = E° − (0.059/n) log Q at 25 °C; increasing product concentration lowers E.

Practice

  1. Q. In a galvanic cell, oxidation occurs at the: Ans. Anode. Oxidation always happens at the anode.
  2. Q. A positive standard cell potential means the reaction is: Ans. Spontaneous. Positive E° corresponds to negative ΔG.
  3. Q. Why is a salt bridge used in a galvanic cell? Ans. It completes the circuit and maintains electrical neutrality in the two half-cells.

2.2 Conductance, Kohlrausch's Law and Faraday's Laws

Ionic conductivity depends on concentration and ion mobility. Faraday's laws quantify electrolysis.

Key points

  • Molar conductivity increases with dilution for weak electrolytes.
  • Kohlrausch's law: limiting molar conductivity of an electrolyte is the sum of ion contributions.
  • Faraday's first law: mass deposited is proportional to charge passed.
  • Faraday's second law: masses deposited by the same charge are proportional to equivalent weights.
  • 1 Faraday = 96,500 C/mol.

Examples

Mass deposited. Depositing copper from Cu2+ with 2 × 96,500 C gives 1 mol of copper (63.5 g).

Why conductivity falls with concentration. Ion-ion interactions reduce mobility at higher concentrations.

Practice

  1. Q. One Faraday equals: Ans. 96,500 C. One mole of electrons carries 96,500 C.
  2. Q. Molar conductivity of a weak electrolyte increases on dilution because: Ans. Ionisation increases. More ions form as the solution is diluted.
  3. Q. State Faraday's first law of electrolysis. Ans. The mass of substance deposited at an electrode is proportional to the quantity of charge passed.

3.1 Rate of Reaction and Rate Laws

Kinetics studies reaction rates. Rate laws relate rate to reactant concentrations and reveal reaction order.

Key points

  • Average rate = change in concentration ÷ time.
  • Order is the sum of concentration exponents in the experimental rate law.
  • First-order rate constant k = (2.303/t) log(a/(a − x)).
  • Half-life of a first-order reaction is independent of initial concentration.
  • Zero-order reactions have a constant rate.

Examples

First-order half-life. A first-order reaction with k = 0.0693 min−1 has t1/2 = 0.693/0.0693 = 10 min.

Determining order. Doubling one reactant doubles the rate means first order in that reactant.

Practice

  1. Q. For a first-order reaction, half-life is: Ans. Independent of concentration. t1/2 = 0.693/k for first order.
  2. Q. The unit of a zero-order rate constant is: Ans. mol L−1 s−1. A zero-order rate is constant in concentration units per time.
  3. Q. A first-order reaction has k = 0.693 s−1. Find its half-life. Ans. t1/2 = 0.693/k = 1 s.

3.2 Factors Affecting Rate and Arrhenius Equation

Temperature, catalysts, surface area and concentration affect rate. The Arrhenius equation links rate constant and activation energy.

Key points

  • Arrhenius equation: k = A e−Ea/(RT).
  • A catalyst lowers activation energy without being consumed.
  • A 10 °C rise in temperature roughly doubles many reaction rates.
  • Collision theory: only molecules with energy above Ea react.
  • Increasing surface area increases the frequency of collisions.

Examples

Temperature effect. If Ea = 50 kJ/mol, raising temperature from 300 K to 310 K increases k by roughly a factor of 2.

Catalyst in industry. Iron catalyses ammonia synthesis by lowering activation energy, so the process works at more moderate conditions.

Practice

  1. Q. A catalyst works by: Ans. Lowering activation energy. Lower Ea gives faster reaction at the same temperature.
  2. Q. The Arrhenius equation relates k to: Ans. Temperature and activation energy. k depends exponentially on temperature and Ea.
  3. Q. Why does a catalyst not change the equilibrium constant? Ans. It speeds up forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached sooner but its position is unchanged.

4.1 Transition Metal Properties

Transition metals show variable oxidation states, form coloured compounds and act as catalysts.

Key points

  • Variable oxidation states arise from similar 4s and 3d energies.
  • Coloured ions come from d-d electronic transitions.
  • Many transition metals and their compounds catalyse reactions.
  • They form complex compounds with ligands.
  • Trends across the series reflect increasing nuclear charge and 3d filling.

Examples

Why Mn shows many oxidation states. Manganese has five 3d electrons and two 4s electrons, so it can lose different numbers, giving states from +2 to +7.

Coloured Cu2+ solutions. Hydrated Cu2+ absorbs red-orange light from d-d transitions, so the solution appears blue.

Practice

  1. Q. Transition metals show variable oxidation states because: Ans. 3d and 4s energies are close. Electrons from both 3d and 4s can be used.
  2. Q. Colour of transition metal ions is due to: Ans. d-d transitions. Absorption of visible light excites d electrons.
  3. Q. Why is Zn not considered a typical transition metal? Ans. Its 3d subshell is full, so it does not show variable oxidation states or coloured compounds.

4.2 Lanthanoids and Actinoids

The f-block elements show lanthanoid and actinoid contraction and characteristic chemistry.

Key points

  • Lanthanoids fill the 4f subshell and show mostly +3 oxidation state.
  • Lanthanoid contraction causes a gradual decrease in size across the series.
  • Actinoids fill 5f orbitals and show more variable oxidation states.
  • Actinoids are mostly radioactive, with uranium and plutonium important in nuclear energy.
  • Lanthanoids are used in magnets, lasers, screens and alloys.

Examples

Lanthanoid contraction. Poor shielding by 4f electrons pulls the nuclei closer, so atomic radii shrink across the lanthanoid series.

Neodymium magnets. Lanthanoid-based magnets are extremely strong and are used in motors, headphones and wind turbines.

Practice

  1. Q. Lanthanoids are characterised by filling of: Ans. 4f orbitals. Lanthanoids fill 4f orbitals.
  2. Q. Actinoids are mostly: Ans. Radioactive. All actinoids are radioactive.
  3. Q. What is lanthanoid contraction? Ans. The gradual decrease in atomic and ionic radii across the lanthanoid series due to poor shielding of 4f electrons.

5.1 Adsorption and Catalysis

Surface chemistry studies phenomena at interfaces. Adsorption concentrates molecules on a surface; catalysis speeds reactions at surfaces.

Key points

  • Adsorption may be physisorption (weak van der Waals forces) or chemisorption (chemical bonds).
  • Physisorption is reversible and has low enthalpy; chemisorption is often irreversible with high enthalpy.
  • Freundlich isotherm: x/m = kp1/n describes adsorption at a given temperature.
  • Catalysts may be homogeneous or heterogeneous; heterogeneous catalysis occurs at surfaces.
  • Catalyst promoters increase activity; poisons reduce it.

Examples

Activated charcoal. Charcoal has a huge surface area and adsorbs gases and colouring matter, which is why it is used in gas masks and water filters.

Iron in ammonia synthesis. The solid iron catalyst provides a surface where nitrogen and hydrogen adsorb and react more easily, lowering activation energy.

Practice

  1. Q. Physisorption is characterised by: Ans. Weak van der Waals forces. Physical adsorption involves weak forces and is reversible.
  2. Q. A promoter in catalysis: Ans. Increases catalyst activity. Promoters enhance catalytic efficiency.
  3. Q. Differentiate physisorption and chemisorption. Ans. Physisorption uses weak forces and is reversible; chemisorption involves chemical bonds and is usually irreversible.

5.2 Colloids and Emulsions

Colloidal particles are larger than molecules but too small to settle. Their surfaces give them special properties.

Key points

  • Colloids are classified as lyophilic or lyophobic depending on affinity for the medium.
  • The Tyndall effect, Brownian motion and electrophoresis reveal colloidal nature.
  • Coagulation destroys a colloid; electrolytes cause coagulation.
  • Emulsions are colloids of one liquid in another, stabilised by emulsifiers.
  • Colloids are used in medicine, food, paints and water purification.

Examples

Milk as an emulsion. Fat droplets are dispersed in water and stabilised by proteins, making milk an oil-in-water emulsion.

River delta formation. River water carrying clay colloids meets salty sea water; ions coagulate the clay, which settles as a delta.

Practice

  1. Q. Which effect confirms the colloidal nature of a mixture? Ans. Tyndall effect. Colloidal particles scatter light.
  2. Q. An emulsion is a colloid of: Ans. One liquid in another. Emulsions involve two immiscible liquids.
  3. Q. Why is milk called an emulsion? Ans. Fat droplets are dispersed throughout water and stabilised by emulsifying proteins.

6.1 Nomenclature and Isomerism

Coordination compounds contain a central metal bonded to ligands. Naming follows IUPAC rules and several isomerism types are possible.

Key points

  • A coordination entity has a central atom and ligands in a definite ratio.
  • Ligand names end with -o (chlorido, cyanido); anionic complexes end with -ate.
  • The coordination number is the number of donor atoms bonded to the metal.
  • Isomers include geometrical (cis/trans), optical and structural forms.
  • Chelate ligands bind through more than one atom and increase stability.

Examples

Naming [Co(NH3)6]Cl3. The octahedral complex of cobalt(III) with six ammine ligands is hexaamminecobalt(III) chloride.

Cis-platin. cis-[PtCl2(NH3)2] binds DNA and is used in cancer chemotherapy; the trans isomer is inactive.

Practice

  1. Q. In [Fe(CN)6]4−, the coordination number of iron is: Ans. 6. Six cyanide ligands bind to iron.
  2. Q. A chelating ligand: Ans. Binds through more than one donor atom. Chelates form stable ring structures.
  3. Q. Why is cis-platin an effective anticancer drug? Ans. It binds to DNA and prevents replication of cancer cells.

6.2 Crystal Field Theory and Colour

Crystal field theory explains colour and magnetism by splitting d orbitals in a ligand field.

Key points

  • Ligands split d orbitals into lower-energy t2g and higher-energy eg sets.
  • The splitting energy Δo depends on the metal and ligand.
  • Strong-field ligands produce large splitting and low-spin complexes.
  • Colour arises when electrons absorb visible light to jump between the sets.
  • The spectrochemical series orders ligands by field strength: I− < Br− < Cl− < F− < H2O < NH3 < CN−.

Examples

Why [Ni(H2O)6]2+ is green. Absorption of light corresponding to Δo promotes d electrons, and the transmitted colour is green.

High spin versus low spin. Weak-field ligands like Cl− give high-spin complexes with unpaired electrons; strong-field CN− gives low-spin.

Practice

  1. Q. The colour of coordination compounds is due to: Ans. d-d transitions. Electron transitions between split d orbitals absorb visible light.
  2. Q. Which ligand produces the largest crystal field splitting? Ans. CN−. CN− is high in the spectrochemical series.
  3. Q. Why is [Cu(H2O)6]2+ blue? Ans. It absorbs red-orange light in d-d transitions and transmits blue light.

7.1 Group 15 and 16 Elements in Detail

The p-block groups show trends in oxidation states, hydrides and oxides as we move down each group.

Key points

  • Group 15: N, P, As, Sb, Bi; oxidation states −3 to +5, with +3 becoming more stable down the group (inert pair effect).
  • Nitrogen forms stable N2 with a triple bond, making it inert.
  • Phosphorus exists as white (reactive, toxic) and red (stable) allotropes.
  • Group 16: O, S, Se, Te, Po; oxygen shows only −2 and −1, while sulphur can show +4 and +6.
  • Oxides of these groups are acidic; nitrogen oxides contribute to pollution.

Examples

Why nitrogen is inert. N≡N has a very high bond dissociation energy, so nitrogen gas reacts only under extreme conditions or with catalysts.

Allotropes of phosphorus. White phosphorus burns spontaneously in air and is stored under water; red phosphorus is stable and used in matchboxes.

Practice

  1. Q. Which allotrope of phosphorus is stored under water? Ans. White phosphorus. White phosphorus is reactive and is kept under water.
  2. Q. The inert pair effect makes which oxidation state more stable down group 15? Ans. +3. Lower oxidation states become more stable down the group.
  3. Q. Why is nitrogen gas chemically inert? Ans. Its triple bond has very high dissociation energy, so breaking it requires extreme conditions.

7.2 Halogens and Noble Gases

Group 17 elements are reactive non-metals with important industrial uses; group 18 elements are notably inert.

Key points

  • Halogens: F2, Cl2, Br2, I2; reactivity decreases down the group.
  • Chlorine is used for water disinfection and in making PVC.
  • The bleaching action of chlorine comes from oxidation (hypochlorous acid).
  • Noble gases have filled outer shells; xenon forms compounds such as XeF2 and XeF4.
  • Where the group 17 and 18 elements are located matters for periodic trends.

Examples

Why fluorine is the most reactive halogen. Its small size and high electronegativity make it eager to gain an electron, so it reacts with almost every element.

Xenon fluorides. Xenon reacts with fluorine under controlled conditions to form compounds such as XeF4, showing that noble gases are not completely inert.

Practice

  1. Q. Which halogen is most reactive? Ans. Fluorine. Reactivity decreases down group 17.
  2. Q. Chlorine bleaches by: Ans. Oxidation. Hypochlorous acid oxidises colouring matter.
  3. Q. Why are noble gases largely inert? Ans. Their filled outer shells give very high ionisation energies and little tendency to gain electrons.

8.1 Nucleophilic Substitution

Haloalkanes undergo substitution. SN1 and SN2 mechanisms differ in rate law and stereochemistry.

Key points

  • SN2^ is a one-step reaction with a rate depending on both substrate and nucleophile.
  • SN1^ goes through a carbocation intermediate and shows first-order kinetics.
  • Tertiary substrates favour SN1; primary favour SN2.
  • SN2^ causes inversion of configuration (Walden inversion).
  • Haloarenes are unreactive toward SN2^ because of partial double-bond character of the C–X bond.

Examples

SN2^ inversion. Hydrolysis of an optically active halide by SN2^ gives the product with inverted configuration.

Why chlorobenzene is inert. Resonance shortens and strengthens the C–Cl bond, so nucleophiles cannot easily displace chloride.

Practice

  1. Q. SN1^ reactions are favoured by: Ans. Tertiary halides. Tertiary carbocations are more stable, favouring SN1^.
  2. Q. SN2^ substitution produces: Ans. Inversion of configuration. The nucleophile attacks the opposite side of the leaving group.
  3. Q. Why are haloarenes less reactive than haloalkanes? Ans. Partial double-bond character of the C–X bond makes it stronger and harder to break.

8.2 Reactions and Uses of Halo Compounds

Haloalkanes are versatile intermediates and also include important environmental pollutants.

Key points

  • Grignard reagents (RMgX) are formed from haloalkanes and magnesium and are powerful synthetic tools.
  • Elimination reactions give alkenes (dehydrohalogenation).
  • Haloalkanes react with metals to form organometallics.
  • CFCs deplete the ozone layer and are widely restricted.
  • DDT is a persistent pesticide that accumulates in food chains.

Examples

Grignard reaction. CH3MgBr reacts with HCHO to give ethanol after hydrolysis, showing carbon–carbon bond formation.

CFC ban. The Montreal Protocol phased out CFCs because chlorine radicals destroy stratospheric ozone.

Practice

  1. Q. Grignard reagents contain: Ans. Mg. RMgX contains magnesium.
  2. Q. CFCs harm the: Ans. Ozone layer. Chlorine radicals break down ozone.
  3. Q. Why are CFCs dangerous for the environment? Ans. They release chlorine radicals in the stratosphere that catalytically destroy ozone molecules.

9.1 Alcohols and Phenols

Alcohols contain −OH on saturated carbon; phenols have −OH on an aromatic ring. Their reactivity differs sharply.

Key points

  • Alcohols hydrogen-bond, giving high boiling points.
  • Phenols are acidic because the phenoxide ion is resonance-stabilised.
  • Alcohols are acidic but weaker than water; phenols are much more acidic.
  • Primary alcohols oxidise to aldehydes then carboxylic acids.
  • Phenols give characteristic colour with FeCl3 and are easily brominated.

Examples

Why phenol is acidic. The negative charge on the phenoxide ion is delocalised into the ring, stabilising the conjugate base.

Lucas test. Tertiary alcohols react immediately with Lucas reagent; primary alcohols react only on heating, distinguishing the classes.

Practice

  1. Q. Phenol is more acidic than ethanol because: Ans. Phenoxide is resonance-stabilised. Resonance stabilises the phenoxide anion.
  2. Q. Oxidation of a primary alcohol gives first: Ans. An aldehyde. Controlled oxidation stops at the aldehyde stage.
  3. Q. Why do alcohols have higher boiling points than ethers of similar mass? Ans. Alcohols form intermolecular hydrogen bonds; ethers do not.

9.2 Ethers and Important Alcohols

Ethers are relatively unreactive solvents; some alcohols are vital industrial and biological compounds.

Key points

  • Ethers are less soluble in water but dissolve many organic compounds.
  • Williamson synthesis makes ethers from an alkoxide and a haloalkane.
  • Methanol is toxic and causes blindness; it is used as a solvent and fuel additive.
  • Ethanol is produced by fermentation and is used as a fuel and solvent.
  • Phenol and its derivatives are used in disinfectants and pharmaceuticals.

Examples

Williamson ether synthesis. Sodium ethoxide plus bromoethane gives diethyl ether, a classic ether preparation.

Denatured alcohol. Adding methanol to ethanol makes it unfit to drink while keeping it usable as a solvent.

Practice

  1. Q. Williamson synthesis produces: Ans. Ethers. An alkoxide and a haloalkane give an ether.
  2. Q. Which alcohol is highly toxic? Ans. Methanol. Methanol is toxic and can cause blindness.
  3. Q. Why is ethanol used as a fuel? Ans. It burns cleanly and can be produced from renewable crops by fermentation.

10.1 Structure, Nomenclature and Reactions

Aldehydes and ketones contain the carbonyl group. Nucleophilic addition and oxidation distinguish them.

Key points

  • Aldehydes are easily oxidised; ketones are not.
  • Tollens' and Fehling's tests detect aldehydes.
  • Nucleophilic addition is the key reaction of carbonyls.
  • Aldol condensation and Cannizzaro reactions are important syntheses.
  • Carboxylic acids are weakly acidic and give esters with alcohols.

Examples

Tollens' silver mirror. An aldehyde reduces [Ag(NH3)2]+ to silver metal, forming a mirror on the test tube.

Esterification. Ethanoic acid and ethanol with acid catalyst give ethyl ethanoate, an ester with fruity smell.

Practice

  1. Q. Tollens' reagent gives a silver mirror with: Ans. Aldehydes. Aldehydes are oxidised while silver ions are reduced.
  2. Q. Carboxylic acids are: Ans. Weakly acidic. They ionise partially to give a carboxylate ion.
  3. Q. Why are carboxylic acids more acidic than phenols? Ans. The carboxylate ion is stabilised by resonance over two equivalent oxygens.

10.2 Preparations and Uses

Carbonyl compounds are prepared by oxidation of alcohols and are used in many industries.

Key points

  • Primary alcohols oxidise to aldehydes; secondary alcohols to ketones.
  • Formaldehyde is used in plastics and as a preservative.
  • Acetone is an important solvent.
  • Acetic acid is used in food and manufacturing.
  • Grignard reagents react with CO2 to give carboxylic acids.

Examples

Acetone from propan-2-ol. Oxidising a secondary alcohol gives a ketone; propan-2-ol gives propanone (acetone).

Formaldehyde in resins. Formaldehyde polymerises with phenol to make bakelite, a thermosetting plastic.

Practice

  1. Q. Oxidation of a secondary alcohol gives a: Ans. Ketone. Secondary alcohols give ketones.
  2. Q. Formaldehyde is used in: Ans. Plastics and preservation. It is a key synthetic intermediate.
  3. Q. How would you distinguish an aldehyde from a ketone? Ans. Tollens' or Fehling's test: the aldehyde gives a positive result, the ketone does not.

11.1 Structure, Basicity and Preparation

Amines are derivatives of ammonia. Basicity depends on electron density on nitrogen and substitution.

Key points

  • Amines are basic due to a lone pair on nitrogen.
  • Aliphatic amines are more basic than ammonia; aromatic amines are less basic.
  • Aniline is less basic because the lone pair is delocalised into the ring.
  • Amines are prepared by reduction of nitro compounds or by Gabriel phthalimide synthesis.
  • Amines react with acids to form salts.

Examples

Why aniline is less basic. Delocalisation of the nitrogen lone pair into the benzene ring reduces availability for protonation.

Amine salt. Methylamine reacts with HCl to give methylammonium chloride, a water-soluble salt.

Practice

  1. Q. Which amine is least basic? Ans. Aniline. Delocalisation makes aniline's lone pair less available.
  2. Q. Gabriel phthalimide synthesis is used to prepare: Ans. Primary amines. The Gabriel synthesis yields primary amines.
  3. Q. Why are amines basic? Ans. They have a lone pair of electrons on nitrogen that can accept a proton.

11.2 Reactions and Diazonium Salts

Amines undergo acylation, alkylation and coupling reactions. Diazonium salts are versatile intermediates.

Key points

  • Acylation gives amides; alkylation adds alkyl groups.
  • The Hinsberg test distinguishes primary, secondary and tertiary amines.
  • Diazonium salts form from aniline with nitrous acid at low temperature.
  • Diazonium salts undergo coupling to give azo dyes.
  • Amines are found in medicines, dyes and proteins.

Examples

Azo dye formation. Benzenediazonium chloride couples with phenol to give an orange azo dye.

Hinsberg test. Primary amines form a soluble product with benzenesulphonyl chloride; tertiary amines do not react.

Practice

  1. Q. Diazonium salts are prepared from aniline at: Ans. Low temperature. Low temperature (0–5 °C) stabilises the diazonium ion.
  2. Q. Azo dyes are formed by: Ans. Coupling of diazonium salts with phenols. Coupling produces the coloured azo group.
  3. Q. What is the Hinsberg test used for? Ans. It distinguishes primary, secondary and tertiary amines by their reaction with benzenesulphonyl chloride.

12.1 Carbohydrates and Proteins

Biomolecules include carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids and vitamins. Their structures determine their roles in life.

Key points

  • Monosaccharides (glucose, fructose) cannot be hydrolysed further.
  • Sucrose is a disaccharide of glucose and fructose; starch and cellulose are polysaccharides.
  • Proteins are polymers of amino acids joined by peptide bonds.
  • Enzymes are biological catalysts, mostly proteins.
  • Denaturation destroys protein structure and function.

Examples

Reducing sugars. Glucose reduces Tollens' and Fehling's reagents, while sucrose does not until hydrolysed.

Why boiling an egg is irreversible. Heat denatures proteins; the folded structure cannot refold after cooling.

Practice

  1. Q. Proteins are polymers of: Ans. Amino acids. Peptide bonds link amino acids.
  2. Q. Which sugar is a monosaccharide? Ans. Glucose. Glucose is a single sugar unit.
  3. Q. What is denaturation of proteins? Ans. Loss of the protein's three-dimensional structure and function due to heat, pH or chemicals.

12.2 Nucleic Acids, Vitamins and Hormones

Nucleic acids store genetic information, vitamins and hormones regulate metabolism.

Key points

  • DNA has a double helix with base pairing A–T and G–C.
  • RNA carries and translates genetic information.
  • Vitamins are essential in small amounts; deficiency causes specific diseases.
  • Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K; water-soluble B and C.
  • Hormones are chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands.

Examples

Base pairing. Adenine pairs with thymine by two hydrogen bonds, guanine with cytosine by three.

Vitamin C deficiency. Lack of ascorbic acid causes scurvy, with bleeding gums and poor wound healing.

Practice

  1. Q. DNA bases pair as: Ans. A–T and G–C. Adenine pairs with thymine and guanine with cytosine.
  2. Q. Which vitamin is fat-soluble? Ans. Vitamin A. Vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fats.
  3. Q. Why are vitamins required in small amounts? Ans. They act as coenzymes and regulators rather than as energy sources.

13.1 Classification and Polymerisation

Polymers are large molecules formed by joining monomers. Addition and condensation polymerisation produce different types.

Key points

  • Addition polymers form from unsaturated monomers, e.g. polythene from ethene.
  • Condensation polymers form with loss of a small molecule, e.g. nylon from a diamine and diacid.
  • Homopolymers have one monomer type; copolymers have more than one.
  • Thermoplastics soften on heating; thermosetting plastics do not.
  • Natural polymers include starch, cellulose and proteins.

Examples

Nylon formation. Hexamethylenediamine plus adipic acid condense to nylon-6,6, losing water and forming strong amide bonds.

Polythene. Ethene molecules add to each other to form long chains; the polymer is used for bags and bottles.

Practice

  1. Q. Polythene is formed by: Ans. Addition polymerisation. Ethene monomers add without losing atoms.
  2. Q. Nylon is a: Ans. Condensation polymer. It forms with loss of water.
  3. Q. Differentiate addition and condensation polymerisation. Ans. Addition joins unsaturated monomers without loss of molecules; condensation joins monomers with loss of a small molecule such as water.

13.2 Important Polymers and Their Uses

Many polymers are used in daily life, but their persistence raises environmental issues.

Key points

  • PVC, polystyrene, bakelite and Teflon have distinct properties and uses.
  • Bakelite is a thermosetting plastic used in electrical fittings.
  • Teflon is non-stick and chemically inert.
  • Bakelite, melamine and epoxy are thermosetting.
  • Biodegradable polymers and recycling reduce plastic pollution.

Examples

Teflon in cookware. PTFE's inertness and low friction make it ideal for non-stick surfaces.

Recycling PET. PET bottles can be recycled into fibres, saving raw material and energy.

Practice

  1. Q. Which polymer is used for non-stick cookware? Ans. Teflon. PTFE (Teflon) resists sticking and chemicals.
  2. Q. Bakelite is a: Ans. Thermosetting plastic. It cannot be remoulded after setting.
  3. Q. Why is plastic pollution a concern? Ans. Most plastics are non-biodegradable and persist for a very long time, harming wildlife and ecosystems.

14.1 Drugs, Antibiotics and Their Action

Drugs are chemicals that interact with biological systems. Their specificity depends on molecular structure.

Key points

  • Receptors are proteins that bind specific molecules and trigger responses.
  • Antacids neutralise stomach acid; antihistamines block histamine receptors.
  • Antibiotics kill bacteria through specific targets such as cell-wall synthesis.
  • Broad-spectrum antibiotics act on many bacteria; narrow-spectrum on few.
  • Drug misuse and overuse can lead to resistance.

Examples

Why antibiotics do not cure colds. Colds are viral, and antibiotics target bacterial structures that viruses lack.

Antacid action. A base such as magnesium hydroxide neutralises excess HCl, relieving acid indigestion.

Practice

  1. Q. Antacids are: Ans. Bases that neutralise stomach acid. They reduce acidity in the stomach.
  2. Q. Antibiotic resistance develops because: Ans. Bacteria evolve resistance under selection. Surviving bacteria multiply and pass on resistance genes.
  3. Q. Why is antibiotic overuse a public health problem? Ans. It selects resistant bacteria, making infections harder to treat.

14.2 Food Additives, Cleansing Agents and Environmental Impact

Chemicals improve food quality and hygiene but can have environmental consequences.

Key points

  • Preservatives prevent microbial spoilage; antioxidants prevent oxidation.
  • Artificial sweeteners add sweetness without much energy.
  • Soaps are salts of fatty acids; detergents are synthetic surfactants.
  • Detergents work in hard water; soaps do not lather well in hard water.
  • Non-biodegradable detergents and fertilisers pollute water bodies.

Examples

Soap in hard water. Calcium ions form an insoluble scum with soap, wasting soap and reducing cleaning.

Eutrophication from detergents. Phosphate-rich detergents enrich lakes with nutrients, causing algal blooms and oxygen depletion.

Practice

  1. Q. Soaps are salts of: Ans. Fatty acids. Soap is a sodium or potassium salt of a fatty acid.
  2. Q. Detergents are better than soaps in hard water because: Ans. They do not form scum. Synthetic surfactants remain soluble in hard water.
  3. Q. Why are phosphates in detergents harmful? Ans. They promote eutrophication, leading to algal blooms and oxygen depletion in water bodies.
