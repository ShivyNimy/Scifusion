Stress-Strain Curves and Elasticity in Practice

A stress-strain curve shows the elastic limit, yield point and breaking point of a material. These determine safe design.

2 worked examples · 3 practice questions

Key points

  • Hooke's law holds in the linear region: stress ∝ strain.
  • Beyond the elastic limit, deformation is permanent (plastic).
  • Ductile materials stretch a lot before breaking (copper); brittle materials break early (glass, cast iron).
  • Elastic hysteresis is energy lost in a loading-unloading cycle, useful in shock absorbers.
  • Safety factors ensure structures stay well below breaking stress.

Worked examples

Example 1 Why bridges bend but don't break

Design keeps stress far below the elastic limit so deformation is reversible; the safety factor covers unexpected loads.

Example 2 Elastomers

Rubber can stretch several times its length and return, because its polymer chains uncoil and recoil. Its stress-strain curve is not linear.

Practice questions

  1. MCQ Beyond the elastic limit, a material:
    A. Deforms permanentlyB. Returns to original shapeC. Breaks immediatelyD. Becomes weightless
    Show answer

    Answer: Deforms permanently

    Plastic deformation is permanent.

  2. MCQ Which material is brittle?
    A. GlassB. CopperC. RubberD. Steel wire
    Show answer

    Answer: Glass

    Glass fractures with little plastic deformation.

  3. Short answer Why are steel rods used in concrete?
    Show answer

    Steel has high tensile strength and Young's modulus, complementing concrete which is strong in compression.
