1.1 SI Units, Dimensions and Errors

Physical quantities are measured in standard units. Dimensions express how a quantity depends on the base quantities, and every measurement has uncertainty.

Key points

  • SI base units: metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole and candela.
  • Derived units combine base units, such as N = kg·m/s2 and J = kg·m2/s2.
  • Dimensional formula of velocity is [M0L1T−1]; force is [M L T−2].
  • Significant figures reflect the precision of a measurement.
  • Errors are absolute, relative or percentage; errors add in quadrature for independent measurements.

Examples

Dimensional check. For s = ut + ½at2, each term must have dimensions of length: [L] = [L T−1][T] = [L T−2][T2]. The equation is dimensionally consistent.

Percentage error. A length of 25.0 cm measured with ±0.1 cm uncertainty has percentage error (0.1/25.0) × 100 = 0.4%.

Practice

  1. Q. The SI unit of force is the: Ans. Newton. 1 N = 1 kg·m/s2.
  2. Q. The dimensional formula of energy is: Ans. [ML2T−2]. Energy = force × distance, so [ML2T−2].
  3. Q. Convert 72 km/h to m/s. Ans. 72 × 5/18 = 20 m/s.

1.2 Dimensional Analysis and Its Uses

Dimensional analysis checks equations, derives relations and converts units, but cannot determine dimensionless constants.

Key points

  • Only quantities with the same dimensions can be added or equated.
  • Dimensions of both sides of a valid equation must match.
  • Dimensional analysis can predict the form of a physical relation up to a dimensionless constant.
  • It cannot handle trigonometric, exponential or logarithmic functions directly.
  • Unit conversion uses the ratio of old to new units.

Examples

Period of a pendulum. Assuming T depends on length l and g, dimensional analysis gives T ∝ √(l/g). The missing factor 2π cannot be found this way.

Checking the wave equation. v = fλ: [L T−1] = [T−1][L], which is consistent.

Practice

  1. Q. Dimensional analysis cannot determine: Ans. Dimensionless constants. Dimensionless numerical factors are invisible to it.
  2. Q. Which of these is dimensionless? Ans. Strain. Strain is a ratio of lengths, so it has no units.
  3. Q. Use dimensional analysis to find the dimensions of pressure. Ans. Pressure = force/area = [MLT−2]/[L2] = [M L−1 T−2].

2.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration

Kinematics describes motion without asking about its cause. Position, displacement, velocity and acceleration are the basic variables.

Key points

  • Displacement is the change in position; distance is the path length travelled.
  • Average velocity = displacement ÷ time; instantaneous velocity is the limit as time tends to zero.
  • Acceleration = rate of change of velocity.
  • For uniform acceleration: v = u + at, s = ut + ½at2 and v2 = u2 + 2as.
  • The slope of a position-time graph gives velocity; the area under a velocity-time graph gives displacement.

Examples

Free fall. A ball dropped from rest falls ½gt2. In 2 s, with g = 9.8 m/s2, it falls 19.6 m and reaches 19.6 m/s.

Relative motion. Two trains moving at 60 km/h in opposite directions approach each other at 120 km/h relative to each other.

Practice

  1. Q. The area under a velocity-time graph gives: Ans. Displacement. Integrating velocity over time gives displacement.
  2. Q. A body thrown upward reaches its highest point when its velocity is: Ans. Zero. At the top the vertical velocity is momentarily zero.
  3. Q. A car accelerates from rest at 2 m/s2 for 5 s. Find its final velocity and distance. Ans. v = at = 10 m/s; s = ½at2 = ½ × 2 × 25 = 25 m.

2.2 Graphs and Relative Velocity

Graphical analysis turns motion into geometry. Relative velocity lets us describe motion from different frames.

Key points

  • A straight position-time line means uniform velocity; a curve means acceleration.
  • Velocity-time graphs: horizontal line = constant velocity; sloped line = constant acceleration.
  • The relative velocity of A with respect to B is vAB = vA − vB.
  • Deceleration appears as a negative slope on a velocity-time graph.
  • The area between the velocity-time curve and the time axis (with sign) gives displacement.

Examples

Graph of a ball thrown up. Velocity decreases linearly with time, becomes zero at the top, then becomes negative as the ball falls back.

River crossing. A boat heading straight across a flowing river drifts downstream; its resultant velocity is the vector sum of boat and current velocities.

Practice

  1. Q. A negative acceleration with positive velocity means the body is: Ans. Slowing down. Velocity and acceleration in opposite directions reduce speed.
  2. Q. Relative velocity of A with respect to B is: Ans. vA − vB. It is the velocity of A measured in B's frame.
  3. Q. A stone is dropped from a 45 m cliff. How long does it take to land (g = 10 m/s2)? Ans. s = ½gt2 gives 45 = 5t2, so t = 3 s.

3.1 Vectors and Projectile Motion

Motion in two dimensions uses vectors. A projectile is an object moving under gravity with a constant horizontal velocity.

Key points

  • Vectors add by the triangle or parallelogram law; components resolve a vector along axes.
  • Projectile motion: horizontal motion is uniform, vertical motion is uniformly accelerated.
  • Time of flight T = 2u sin θ / g; range R = u2 sin 2θ / g; maximum height H = u2 sin2θ / 2g.
  • Maximum range occurs at 45° for a given speed.
  • The horizontal velocity stays constant if air resistance is ignored.

Examples

Football kick. A ball kicked at 20 m/s at 30° has range R = 400 × sin 60° / 9.8 ≈ 35.3 m and time of flight T = 2 × 20 × 0.5 / 9.8 ≈ 2.04 s.

Why 45° gives maximum range. sin 2θ is maximum when 2θ = 90°, that is θ = 45°.

Practice

  1. Q. The horizontal acceleration of a projectile (ignoring air resistance) is: Ans. Zero. Only gravity acts, vertically.
  2. Q. Maximum range for a given launch speed occurs at: Ans. 45°. R ∝ sin 2θ, maximum at θ = 45°.
  3. Q. A ball is thrown at 10 m/s at 45°. Find its range (g = 10 m/s2). Ans. R = u2 sin 2θ / g = 100 × 1 / 10 = 10 m.

3.2 Uniform Circular Motion

In uniform circular motion the speed is constant but velocity changes because direction changes, producing centripetal acceleration.

Key points

  • Angular velocity ω = 2π/T = v/r.
  • Centripetal acceleration ac = v2/r = ω2r, directed toward the centre.
  • Centripetal force F = mv2/r.
  • Linear speed v = ωr relates the linear and angular descriptions.
  • Centrifugal force is a pseudo-force experienced in the rotating frame.

Examples

Car on a curve. A 1000 kg car at 20 m/s on a 50 m radius curve needs F = mv2/r = 1000 × 400 / 50 = 8000 N of friction toward the centre.

Why cyclists lean inward. Leaning aligns the resultant of gravity and friction through the centre, preventing skidding.

Practice

  1. Q. Centripetal acceleration is directed: Ans. Toward the centre. It always points to the centre of the circle.
  2. Q. If speed doubles in the same circle, centripetal force becomes: Ans. Four times. F ∝ v2.
  3. Q. Find the angular velocity of a point on the equator (radius 6400 km) due to Earth's rotation. Ans. T = 86400 s, ω = 2π/T ≈ 7.27 × 10−5 rad/s.

4.1 Newton's Laws and Common Forces

Newton's three laws explain motion in terms of forces. Friction, tension, normal force and spring force are the forces that appear most often.

Key points

  • First law: no net force means no change in velocity (inertia).
  • Second law: Fnet = ma, or F = dp/dt.
  • Third law: action and reaction are equal, opposite and act on different bodies.
  • Friction f ≤ μN; static friction adjusts up to a maximum.
  • Tension is the same throughout a light, frictionless string.

Examples

Elevator physics. A person of mass m in an elevator accelerating upward experiences N = m(g + a); downward acceleration gives N = m(g − a).

Block on an incline. On a frictionless incline at angle θ, the acceleration is g sin θ regardless of mass.

Practice

  1. Q. The normal force on a body on a horizontal floor is: Ans. mg. When not accelerating vertically, N balances weight.
  2. Q. A body on a circular path at constant speed has: Ans. Changing velocity. Direction of velocity changes, so velocity is not constant.
  3. Q. Why do we require seat belts even when a car moves at constant speed? Ans. If the car stops suddenly, inertia carries the passenger forward; the belt provides the stopping force.

4.2 Circular Motion and Banking

Turning requires a centripetal force. Roads are banked to help vehicles turn without relying entirely on friction.

Key points

  • Friction supplies centripetal force on a flat road: μmg ≥ mv2/r, so v ≤ √(μrg).
  • On a banked road, the horizontal component of the normal force supplies centripetal force.
  • Optimum banking: tan θ = v2/(rg).
  • Banking reduces the friction needed, which is why racing tracks and highways are banked.
  • A conical pendulum and a body in a vertical circle are classic circular-motion systems.

Examples

Safe speed on a curve. With μ = 0.4 and r = 40 m, the maximum safe speed is v = √(0.4 × 40 × 9.8) ≈ 12.5 m/s ≈ 45 km/h.

Banking angle. For v = 20 m/s and r = 100 m, tan θ = 400/(100 × 9.8) ≈ 0.41, so θ ≈ 22°.

Practice

  1. Q. Banking of roads helps to: Ans. Provide centripetal force with less friction. The normal force component acts toward the centre.
  2. Q. Maximum safe speed on a flat road varies as: Ans. √r. v ∝ √(μrg).
  3. Q. Why is a cyclist able to negotiate a curve by leaning? Ans. Leaning shifts the reaction force so its horizontal component provides the centripetal force.

5.1 Work and Energy Theorem

Work is the transfer of energy by a force. The work-energy theorem links net work with the change in kinetic energy.

Key points

  • W = F·s = Fs cos θ; work is a scalar.
  • Work is positive when force and displacement are in the same direction, negative when opposite.
  • Work-energy theorem: Wnet = ΔKE.
  • Kinetic energy KE = ½mv2; potential energy for a spring is ½kx2.
  • Gravity is a conservative force; friction is non-conservative.

Examples

Work by friction. A 5 kg box sliding 2 m against 10 N friction loses 20 J of mechanical energy, which becomes heat.

Spring compression. Compressing a spring of k = 200 N/m by 0.1 m stores ½ × 200 × 0.01 = 1 J of energy.

Practice

  1. Q. Work done is zero when: Ans. Force is perpendicular to displacement. cos 90° = 0, so W = 0.
  2. Q. The unit of energy is: Ans. Joule. Energy is measured in joules.
  3. Q. A force of 20 N moves a box 4 m in the direction of the force. Find the work done. Ans. W = Fs cos 0° = 20 × 4 = 80 J.

5.2 Power and Conservation of Energy

Power is the rate of doing work. Energy is conserved but may be converted into less useful forms.

Key points

  • Power P = W/t = Fv.
  • 1 horsepower ≈ 746 W; 1 kWh = 3.6 × 106 J.
  • Conservation of mechanical energy holds when only conservative forces act.
  • Efficiency = useful output energy ÷ total input energy × 100%.
  • Collisions conserve momentum; elastic collisions also conserve kinetic energy.

Examples

Climbing stairs. A 60 kg person climbing 5 m in 10 s does 60 × 9.8 × 5 = 2940 J, giving a power of 294 W.

Elastic collision. In an elastic collision between equal masses, one at rest, the moving ball stops and the other moves off with the original velocity.

Practice

  1. Q. Power is: Ans. Rate of doing work. Power = work ÷ time.
  2. Q. In a perfectly inelastic collision: Ans. Kinetic energy is not conserved. The bodies stick together and some KE becomes heat or deformation.
  3. Q. A pump lifts 500 kg of water 10 m in 20 s. Find its power (g = 10 m/s2). Ans. Work = mgh = 500 × 10 × 10 = 50,000 J; P = 50,000/20 = 2500 W.

6.1 Centre of Mass and Moment of Inertia

Extended bodies are treated as systems of particles. The centre of mass moves as if all forces acted there, and rotational inertia depends on mass distribution.

Key points

  • The centre of mass is the mass-weighted average position of a system.
  • For two masses, the centre of mass divides the distance in inverse ratio to masses.
  • Torque τ = r × F; it produces angular acceleration.
  • Moment of inertia I = Σmr2; larger I means more resistance to rotation.
  • Angular momentum L = Iω is conserved when net torque is zero.

Examples

Balancing on a finger. A ruler balances at its centre of mass. Adding a heavy mass at one end shifts the centre of mass toward that end.

Figure skater spin. Pulling arms in reduces I, so ω increases to conserve angular momentum, and the skater spins faster.

Practice

  1. Q. Torque depends on: Ans. Force and perpendicular distance. τ = rF sin θ.
  2. Q. When a skater pulls her arms in, her angular velocity: Ans. Increases. Reducing I increases ω to keep L constant.
  3. Q. Why is it easier to open a door by pushing near the handle than near the hinge? Ans. The handle is farther from the hinge, so the same force produces a larger torque.

6.2 Rotational Kinematics and Rolling

Rotational motion has analogues of every linear quantity. Rolling combines translation and rotation.

Key points

  • ω = ω0 + αt; θ = ω0t + ½αt2; ω2 = ω02 + 2αθ.
  • Rotational KE = ½Iω2.
  • Rolling without slipping: v = ωR.
  • Total kinetic energy of a rolling body = ½mv2 + ½Iω2.
  • The rolling body with the smaller I/mR2 reaches the bottom of an incline first.

Examples

Solid sphere versus hoop. A solid sphere (I = 2/5 mR2) rolls down faster than a hoop (I = mR2) because a larger fraction of its energy goes into translation.

Rotational energy. A disc with I = 0.5 kg·m2 spinning at 10 rad/s has KE = ½ × 0.5 × 100 = 25 J.

Practice

  1. Q. Rolling without slipping means: Ans. v = ωR. The contact point is instantaneously at rest.
  2. Q. Angular momentum is conserved when: Ans. Net torque is zero. Zero torque means constant L.
  3. Q. Calculate the rotational KE of a wheel with I = 2 kg·m2 spinning at 5 rad/s. Ans. KE = ½Iω2 = ½ × 2 × 25 = 25 J.

7.1 Gravitational Field, Potential and Orbits

Newton's law of gravitation explains falling bodies, orbits and the motion of planets. Field strength and potential describe gravity at a point.

Key points

  • F = Gm1m2/r2; gravitational field g = GM/r2.
  • Gravitational potential energy U = −GMm/r; gravitational potential V = −GM/r.
  • Orbital velocity vo = √(GM/r); escape velocity ve = √(2GM/R).
  • Kepler's third law: T2 ∝ a3 for orbits around the same central body.
  • Inside a uniform spherical shell the gravitational field is zero.

Examples

Satellite speed. For a satellite 400 km above Earth (r ≈ 6.77 × 106 m) with GM = 3.986 × 1014 m3/s2, v = √(GM/r) ≈ 7.67 km/s.

Escape velocity. ve = √(2gR) = √(2 × 9.8 × 6.4 × 106) ≈ 11.2 km/s.

Practice

  1. Q. The escape velocity from Earth is: Ans. ≈ 11.2 km/s. It is about 11.2 km/s.
  2. Q. Gravitational potential energy of a satellite in orbit is: Ans. Negative. It is negative because the force is attractive with zero at infinity.
  3. Q. Why is g smaller at the equator than at the poles? Ans. Earth bulges at the equator (larger radius) and rotation reduces effective gravity there.

7.2 Satellites and Weightlessness

A satellite is in continual free fall around Earth. Its horizontal speed keeps it from hitting the ground.

Key points

  • Orbital period T = 2π√(r3/GM).
  • Geostationary satellites orbit at about 36,000 km with a period of 24 h.
  • Inside an orbiting satellite everything is in free fall, giving apparent weightlessness.
  • Total energy of a satellite in orbit is E = −GMm/(2r).
  • Rockets need large fuel mass; the rocket equation relates speed gain to exhaust speed and mass ratio.

Examples

Geostationary orbit. A satellite at about 36,000 km above the equator takes one day to orbit, so it stays over the same point — ideal for communication and weather.

Weightlessness in orbit. Astronauts float not because gravity is absent but because they and the spacecraft fall together.

Practice

  1. Q. A geostationary satellite has an orbital period of: Ans. 24 hours. It matches Earth's rotation period.
  2. Q. Weightlessness in orbit is due to: Ans. Free fall. Gravity provides the centripetal force; the astronaut and cabin fall together.
  3. Q. State Kepler's third law. Ans. The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis.

8.1 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli

Solids deform under force. Stress is force per area and strain is fractional deformation; their ratio is a modulus.

Key points

  • Stress = force ÷ area; strain = change in dimension ÷ original dimension.
  • Young's modulus Y = longitudinal stress ÷ longitudinal strain.
  • Bulk modulus B = pressure ÷ fractional volume change.
  • Shear modulus G = shear stress ÷ shear strain.
  • Steel has a much larger Young's modulus than rubber, so it is stiffer.

Examples

Stretching a wire. A steel wire of length 2 m and area 10−4 m2 under 100 N stretches by ΔL = FL/(AY). With Y = 2 × 1011 N/m2, ΔL = 100 × 2/(10−4 × 2 × 1011) = 10−5 m.

Bulk compression. Water has a high bulk modulus, so it resists compression strongly — which is why it is used in hydraulic systems.

Practice

  1. Q. Young's modulus is the ratio of: Ans. Longitudinal stress to longitudinal strain. It measures stiffness along the length.
  2. Q. A material with a larger Young's modulus is: Ans. Stiffer. Higher Y means less strain for the same stress.
  3. Q. Define strain and give its unit. Ans. Strain is the ratio of change in dimension to original dimension; it has no unit.

8.2 Stress-Strain Curves and Elasticity in Practice

A stress-strain curve shows the elastic limit, yield point and breaking point of a material. These determine safe design.

Key points

  • Hooke's law holds in the linear region: stress ∝ strain.
  • Beyond the elastic limit, deformation is permanent (plastic).
  • Ductile materials stretch a lot before breaking (copper); brittle materials break early (glass, cast iron).
  • Elastic hysteresis is energy lost in a loading-unloading cycle, useful in shock absorbers.
  • Safety factors ensure structures stay well below breaking stress.

Examples

Why bridges bend but don't break. Design keeps stress far below the elastic limit so deformation is reversible; the safety factor covers unexpected loads.

Elastomers. Rubber can stretch several times its length and return, because its polymer chains uncoil and recoil. Its stress-strain curve is not linear.

Practice

  1. Q. Beyond the elastic limit, a material: Ans. Deforms permanently. Plastic deformation is permanent.
  2. Q. Which material is brittle? Ans. Glass. Glass fractures with little plastic deformation.
  3. Q. Why are steel rods used in concrete? Ans. Steel has high tensile strength and Young's modulus, complementing concrete which is strong in compression.

9.1 Pressure, Pascal's Law and Buoyancy

Fluids exert pressure in all directions. Pascal's law and Archimedes' principle underlie hydraulics and floating.

Key points

  • Pressure at depth h: P = P0 + ρgh.
  • Pascal's law: pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally in all directions.
  • Buoyant force equals the weight of fluid displaced (Archimedes' principle).
  • A body floats if its average density is less than the fluid's.
  • Hydraulic machines use Pascal's law to multiply force.

Examples

Hydraulic lift. A small force on a small piston creates pressure transmitted to a larger piston, producing a larger force: F2 = F1 A2/A1.

Iceberg floating. Ice density is about 0.92 of seawater, so roughly 92% of an iceberg lies below the surface.

Practice

  1. Q. Pressure at depth in a liquid depends on: Ans. Depth and density. P = ρgh, so depth and density matter.
  2. Q. Archimedes' principle gives the buoyant force as: Ans. Weight of displaced fluid. The upthrust equals the weight of the displaced fluid.
  3. Q. Why does a ship made of iron float? Ans. Its hollow shape displaces water whose weight equals the ship's weight, so it floats.

9.2 Fluid Dynamics: Bernoulli and Viscosity

Moving fluids obey continuity and Bernoulli's equation. Real fluids have viscosity that resists flow.

Key points

  • Equation of continuity: A1v1 = A2v2 for incompressible flow.
  • Bernoulli's theorem: P + ½ρv2 + ρgh = constant along a streamline.
  • Faster flow means lower pressure, which explains lift and the working of sprayers.
  • Viscous force F = ηA dv/dx; η is the coefficient of viscosity.
  • Terminal velocity is reached when viscous drag plus buoyancy balances weight.

Examples

Why aeroplanes fly. Air moves faster over the curved upper wing, lowering pressure there, so the higher pressure below produces lift.

Terminal velocity of a raindrop. Drag increases with speed until it balances weight; the drop then falls at a constant terminal speed instead of accelerating.

Practice

  1. Q. Bernoulli's theorem is based on: Ans. Conservation of energy. It follows from energy conservation in an ideal fluid.
  2. Q. At a narrower section of a pipe, the fluid speed: Ans. Increases. By continuity, smaller area means higher speed.
  3. Q. State the equation of continuity and explain it. Ans. A1v1 = A2v2; the mass flow rate of an incompressible fluid is constant, so speed rises where the pipe narrows.

10.1 Temperature, Heat and Expansion

Temperature measures hotness; heat is energy transferred due to temperature difference. Materials expand with temperature.

Key points

  • Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin scales are related; K = °C + 273.15.
  • Linear expansion ΔL = LαΔT; area expansion uses 2α; volume expansion uses 3α.
  • Specific heat capacity c: Q = mcΔT.
  • Latent heat L: Q = mL for a change of state at constant temperature.
  • Water has a very high specific heat, which stabilises climate.

Examples

Heating water. To heat 2 kg of water by 30 °C, with c = 4200 J/kg·K: Q = 2 × 4200 × 30 = 252,000 J.

Melting ice. Melting 1 kg of ice at 0 °C takes Q = mL = 1 × 3.34 × 105 J = 334 kJ.

Practice

  1. Q. The SI unit of specific heat capacity is: Ans. J/kg·K. It is the energy per kilogram per kelvin.
  2. Q. During melting, the temperature: Ans. Remains constant. Latent heat changes state without changing temperature.
  3. Q. How much heat is needed to raise 0.5 kg of water by 10 °C (c = 4200 J/kg·K)? Ans. Q = mcΔT = 0.5 × 4200 × 10 = 21,000 J.

10.2 Heat Transfer and Newton's Law of Cooling

Heat moves by conduction, convection and radiation. Cooling rates depend on temperature difference and surface.

Key points

  • Conduction rate H = kA(T1 − T2)/L.
  • Convection transfers heat through fluid motion; radiation needs no medium.
  • Stefan's law: power radiated ∝ T4; Wien's law: λmax ∝ 1/T.
  • Newton's law of cooling: rate of cooling ∝ temperature difference for small differences.
  • Black surfaces absorb and emit better than shiny ones.

Examples

Why a thermos flask works. Double walls with a vacuum prevent conduction and convection, and silvered surfaces reduce radiation, so drinks stay hot or cold for hours.

Cooling of coffee. The coffee cools fast when much hotter than the room and slows as it approaches room temperature, in line with Newton's law of cooling.

Practice

  1. Q. Heat transfer that needs no medium is: Ans. Radiation. Radiation travels through a vacuum, like sunlight.
  2. Q. A good absorber is also a good: Ans. Emitter. Kirchhoff's law links absorptivity and emissivity.
  3. Q. Why do we feel hot near a fire even in a cold room? Ans. The fire radiates infrared energy that reaches us without needing a medium.

11.1 Zeroth and First Law

Thermodynamics describes energy changes in systems. The first law is the conservation of energy applied to heat and work.

Key points

  • Zeroth law: two bodies in thermal equilibrium with a third are in equilibrium with each other — basis of temperature.
  • First law: ΔU = Q − W, where W is work done by the system.
  • Work done by a gas at constant pressure: W = PΔV.
  • Internal energy is a state function; heat and work are path-dependent.
  • Isothermal, adiabatic, isochoric and isobaric processes are the basic ones.

Examples

Gas expansion. A gas expanding at constant 105 Pa by 2 × 10−3 m3 does W = PΔV = 200 J of work.

Isothermal compression. At constant temperature, compressing a gas requires work, which is given out as heat, so ΔU = 0.

Practice

  1. Q. The first law of thermodynamics is: Ans. ΔU = Q − W. Q is heat added and W is work done by the system.
  2. Q. In an adiabatic process: Ans. Q = 0. No heat is exchanged with the surroundings.
  3. Q. A gas absorbs 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work. Find ΔU. Ans. ΔU = Q − W = 500 − 200 = 300 J.

11.2 Second Law, Heat Engines and Refrigerators

The second law limits the conversion of heat into work. Engines have a maximum possible efficiency.

Key points

  • Kelvin-Planck statement: no engine converts all heat into work.
  • Clausius statement: heat cannot flow from cold to hot without work.
  • Carnot efficiency η = 1 − Tc/Th (temperatures in kelvin).
  • A heat engine takes Qh from the hot reservoir, does work W and rejects Qc to the cold reservoir.
  • A refrigerator is a reversed heat engine with COP = Qc/W.

Examples

Carnot engine. Between 500 K and 300 K the Carnot efficiency is 1 − 300/500 = 40%. No real engine can exceed this.

Why engines are inefficient. Friction, incomplete combustion and heat losses all reduce efficiency below the Carnot limit.

Practice

  1. Q. Carnot efficiency depends on: Ans. Source and sink temperatures. η = 1 − Tc/Th.
  2. Q. The second law forbids: Ans. Converting all heat into work in a cycle. Some heat must be rejected to a colder reservoir.
  3. Q. An engine works between 600 K and 300 K. Find its maximum efficiency. Ans. η = 1 − 300/600 = 0.5, so 50%.

12.1 Molecular Picture of Gases

Kinetic theory explains gas behaviour by treating molecules as tiny particles in random motion.

Key points

  • Pressure arises from molecular collisions with container walls.
  • PV = (1/3) Nm(vrms)2 links macroscopic and microscopic variables.
  • Average kinetic energy per molecule = (3/2)kT.
  • RMS speed vrms = √(3RT/M).
  • Temperature is proportional to average molecular kinetic energy.

Examples

RMS speed of nitrogen. At 300 K, vrms = √(3 × 8.314 × 300/0.028) ≈ 517 m/s.

Why balloons expand in heat. Higher temperature increases molecular speed and pressure; at constant pressure the gas expands.

Practice

  1. Q. Average kinetic energy of a molecule depends only on: Ans. Temperature. KE = (3/2)kT per molecule.
  2. Q. As temperature increases, RMS speed: Ans. Increases. vrms ∝ √T.
  3. Q. Why does a gas exert pressure on the walls of its container? Ans. Molecules collide with the walls and transfer momentum, producing a force per unit area.

12.2 Specific Heat of Gases and Mean Free Path

Gases have two specific heats, Cp and Cv. Molecules travel only short distances between collisions.

Key points

  • Cp − Cv = R for an ideal gas; Cp > Cv.
  • Monatomic gas: Cv = (3/2)R, γ = 5/3; diatomic: Cv = (5/2)R, γ = 7/5.
  • Mean free path λ = 1/(√2 πnd2).
  • Degrees of freedom determine the energy distribution — the equipartition theorem.
  • Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour at high pressure and low temperature.

Examples

Value of γ. For a monatomic gas γ = 1.67, for a diatomic gas γ = 1.4. These values affect the speed of sound.

Mean free path. At standard conditions λ for air molecules is about 10−7 m — tiny compared with the size of the container.

Practice

  1. Q. For an ideal gas, Cp − Cv equals: Ans. R. This is Mayer's relation.
  2. Q. γ for a monatomic gas is: Ans. 5/3. Cp/Cv = (5/2)/(3/2) = 5/3.
  3. Q. Why does temperature stay constant during an isothermal process? Ans. Heat added equals work done by the gas, so internal energy and hence temperature do not change.

13.1 Simple Harmonic Motion

In SHM the restoring force is proportional to displacement and directed toward the mean position. Displacement, velocity and acceleration vary sinusoidally.

Key points

  • x = A sin(ωt + φ); v = Aω cos(ωt + φ); a = −ω2x.
  • Angular frequency ω = 2π/T; maximum speed = Aω; maximum acceleration = Aω2.
  • Force F = −kx, with ω = √(k/m).
  • Total energy E = ½kA2 is constant, exchanging between kinetic and potential.
  • The period is independent of amplitude for small oscillations.

Examples

Spring-mass system. A 0.5 kg mass on a spring of k = 50 N/m oscillates with T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√0.01 ≈ 0.63 s.

Pendulum. A simple pendulum has T = 2π√(l/g); a 1 m pendulum has T ≈ 2.0 s.

Practice

  1. Q. The period of a simple pendulum depends on: Ans. Length and g. T = 2π√(l/g).
  2. Q. At the mean position of SHM, the acceleration is: Ans. Zero. Restoring force is zero at the mean position.
  3. Q. A spring with k = 100 N/m carries a 1 kg mass. Find its period. Ans. T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√(1/100) = 0.628 s.

13.2 Damped and Forced Oscillations, Resonance

Real oscillations lose energy (damping). Forced oscillations can build up dramatically at resonance.

Key points

  • Damping force is proportional to velocity; amplitude decays exponentially.
  • Forced oscillations occur when an external periodic force drives the system.
  • Resonance occurs when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency.
  • At resonance the amplitude is maximum; it can damage structures.
  • Examples: musical instruments, tuning radios, soldiers breaking step on bridges.

Examples

Soldiers on a bridge. If marching frequency matches the bridge's natural frequency, resonant swaying can build up, so soldiers break step on bridges.

Tuning a radio. Adjusting the tuning circuit changes its natural frequency until it matches the station, giving resonant maximum response.

Practice

  1. Q. Resonance occurs when: Ans. Driving frequency equals natural frequency. At matching frequencies the amplitude peaks.
  2. Q. Damping ______ the amplitude of oscillation. Ans. Reduces. Energy is dissipated, so amplitude decays.
  3. Q. Why do soldiers break step while crossing a bridge? Ans. To avoid resonance with the bridge's natural frequency, which could cause dangerous oscillations.

14.1 Wave Characteristics and Speed

Waves transfer energy without transferring matter. A wave has wavelength, frequency, amplitude and speed.

Key points

  • v = fλ; the speed depends on the medium.
  • Transverse waves have perpendicular particle displacement; longitudinal waves have parallel.
  • Sound is longitudinal; light is transverse electromagnetic.
  • Wave speed on a stretched string v = √(T/μ).
  • The principle of superposition: displacements add algebraically.

Examples

Speed of a wave on a string. A string with tension 100 N and linear density 0.01 kg/m supports waves at v = √(100/0.01) = 100 m/s.

Sound in air. A 440 Hz note in air (v = 340 m/s) has wavelength λ = v/f ≈ 0.77 m.

Practice

  1. Q. The relation between speed, frequency and wavelength is: Ans. v = fλ. Wave speed equals frequency times wavelength.
  2. Q. Sound waves in air are: Ans. Longitudinal. Air particles vibrate along the direction of propagation.
  3. Q. A wave has frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 4 m. Find its speed. Ans. v = fλ = 50 × 4 = 200 m/s.

14.2 Standing Waves, Beats and Doppler Effect

Superposition produces standing waves, beats and the Doppler shift. These explain musical notes and moving-source effects.

Key points

  • Standing waves form nodes (no displacement) and antinodes (maximum displacement).
  • On a string fixed at both ends, λ = 2L/n for the nth harmonic.
  • Beats: beat frequency = |f1 − f2|.
  • Doppler effect: observed frequency rises when source and observer approach.
  • An overtone is any resonant frequency above the fundamental.

Examples

Guitar string. A 0.65 m string fixed at both ends has fundamental wavelength 1.3 m; with v = 300 m/s the frequency is f = 300/1.3 ≈ 231 Hz.

Ambulance siren. The siren sounds higher in pitch as the ambulance approaches and lower as it passes, because of the Doppler shift.

Practice

  1. Q. A node in a standing wave has: Ans. Zero displacement. Nodes are points of no motion.
  2. Q. Beat frequency equals: Ans. Difference of frequencies. Beats arise from the difference between two close frequencies.
  3. Q. Two tuning forks of 256 Hz and 260 Hz are sounded together. Find the beat frequency. Ans. Beat frequency = |260 − 256| = 4 Hz.
