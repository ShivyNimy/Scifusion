1.1 Mole Concept and Stoichiometry

Chemistry counts particles in moles. The mole links mass, number of particles and volumes of gases, making quantitative reactions possible.

Key points

  • 1 mole = 6.022 × 1023 particles (Avogadro's number).
  • Molar mass is the mass in grams of one mole of a substance.
  • Moles n = mass ÷ molar mass.
  • At STP, 1 mole of an ideal gas occupies 22.4 L.
  • Percentage composition = (mass of element ÷ molar mass) × 100.

Examples

Moles of water. 36 g of water contains 36/18 = 2 mol, i.e. 1.204 × 1024 molecules.

Empirical formula. A compound with 40% C, 6.7% H and 53.3% O gives a mole ratio 1 : 2 : 1, so the empirical formula is CH2O.

Practice

  1. Q. Avogadro's number is: Ans. 6.022 × 1023. One mole contains 6.022 × 1023 particles.
  2. Q. Number of moles in 4 g of helium (M = 4 g/mol) is: Ans. 1. n = 4/4 = 1 mol.
  3. Q. Calculate the number of molecules in 9 g of water. Ans. Moles = 9/18 = 0.5; molecules = 0.5 × 6.022 × 1023 = 3.011 × 1023.

1.2 Stoichiometry and Limiting Reagents

Balanced equations give mole ratios. The limiting reagent determines how much product can form.

Key points

  • A balanced equation gives the ratio in which reactants combine and products form.
  • The limiting reagent is completely consumed first and controls the yield.
  • Theoretical yield is calculated from stoichiometry; actual yield is measured.
  • Percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100.
  • Excess reagent remains after the reaction completes.

Examples

Hydrogen and oxygen. 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O. If 4 mol H2 reacts with 1 mol O2, hydrogen is in excess and only 2 mol of water can form from the oxygen available.

Percentage yield. If a reaction should give 20 g but gives 16 g, the yield is (16/20) × 100 = 80%.

Practice

  1. Q. The limiting reagent is the one that: Ans. Is completely consumed first. It limits the amount of product formed.
  2. Q. Percentage yield is calculated as: Ans. Actual/theoretical × 100. It compares actual and theoretical yields.
  3. Q. In 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O, how many moles of water form from 2 mol H2 and 2 mol O2? Ans. H2 is limiting; 2 mol H2 gives 2 mol H2O, leaving 1 mol O2 unused.

2.1 Quantum Numbers and Orbitals

Quantum mechanics describes electrons in orbitals defined by four quantum numbers. Orbitals have shapes and energies.

Key points

  • Principal quantum number n gives the shell and energy level.
  • Azimuthal quantum number l gives the subshell (s, p, d, f).
  • Magnetic quantum number ml gives orbital orientation; spin ms is ±½.
  • Number of orbitals in a subshell = 2l + 1; each orbital holds 2 electrons.
  • Aufbau principle: electrons fill lower-energy orbitals first.

Examples

Quantum numbers of a 2p electron. n = 2, l = 1, ml can be −1, 0 or +1, and ms is ±½. The 2p subshell has 3 orbitals and holds 6 electrons.

Filling chromium. Chromium is [Ar]3d54s1 rather than 3d44s2 because half-filled d orbitals are extra stable.

Practice

  1. Q. The maximum number of electrons in a d subshell is: Ans. 10. 5 orbitals × 2 electrons = 10.
  2. Q. Which quantum number describes orbital shape? Ans. Azimuthal. l determines the subshell type: s, p, d, f.
  3. Q. State the Pauli exclusion principle. Ans. No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers.

2.2 Electronic Configuration and Periodic Trends

Electron configurations explain the periodic table and trends such as atomic radius, ionisation energy and electronegativity.

Key points

  • Electrons fill 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d … following the (n + l) rule.
  • Atomic radius decreases across a period and increases down a group.
  • Ionisation energy increases across a period and decreases down a group.
  • Electronegativity increases across a period and decreases down a group.
  • Hund's rule: degenerate orbitals fill singly first with parallel spins.

Examples

Why sodium is more reactive than magnesium. Sodium loses its single 3s electron easily, while magnesium needs to lose two. Lower ionisation energy makes sodium more reactive.

Why noble gases are inert. Their filled outer shells give high ionisation energies and no tendency to gain or lose electrons.

Practice

  1. Q. Across a period, atomic radius generally: Ans. Decreases. Increasing nuclear charge pulls electrons closer.
  2. Q. Which has the largest atomic radius? Ans. Potassium. Radius increases down the group.
  3. Q. Why does ionisation energy decrease down a group? Ans. The outer electron is farther from the nucleus and shielded by more inner shells, so it is easier to remove.

3.1 The Modern Periodic Table and Its Trends

Elements are arranged by atomic number. Position determines properties, and trends recur periodically.

Key points

  • Periods are horizontal rows; groups are vertical columns.
  • Elements in a group have the same valence electron configuration.
  • Metallic character decreases across a period and increases down a group.
  • Anomalies exist, such as the similar chemistry of lithium and magnesium (diagonal relationship).
  • The table separates s, p, d and f blocks by the subshell being filled.

Examples

Diagonal relationship. Lithium and magnesium show similar chemistry because of comparable charge/radius ratios, even though they are in different groups.

Noble gas placement. Group 18 elements have complete octets and very high ionisation energies, giving them their characteristic inertness.

Practice

  1. Q. Elements in the same group have the same: Ans. Number of valence electrons. Group number reflects valence configuration.
  2. Q. Metallic character ______ down a group. Ans. Increases. Atoms lose electrons more easily as size increases.
  3. Q. Why does electronegativity increase across a period? Ans. Effective nuclear charge increases while atomic size decreases, so atoms attract bonding electrons more strongly.

3.2 Periodic Properties in Detail

Ionisation enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy and valence describe reactivity and bonding.

Key points

  • First ionisation enthalpy is the energy to remove the most loosely bound electron.
  • Electron gain enthalpy is the energy change when an electron is added.
  • Group 17 elements have very negative electron gain enthalpies, making them reactive non-metals.
  • Valence is the number of electrons an atom gains, loses or shares to complete its octet.
  • Atomic and ionic radii vary with effective nuclear charge and shielding.

Examples

Fluorine versus chlorine electron gain. Fluorine's small size causes electron-electron repulsion, so chlorine has a more negative electron gain enthalpy than fluorine.

Radius of cation versus atom. Na+ is smaller than Na because losing an electron reduces repulsion and the same nuclear charge pulls the remaining electrons closer.

Practice

  1. Q. A cation is ______ than its parent atom. Ans. Smaller. Loss of electrons and reduced repulsion contract the ion.
  2. Q. Which element has the highest first ionisation enthalpy? Ans. Helium. Helium's tightly held 1s electrons need the most energy to remove.
  3. Q. Why is the second ionisation energy of sodium much greater than the first? Ans. Removing a second electron from a stable noble-gas configuration requires far more energy.

4.1 Ionic and Covalent Bonding, VSEPR

Atoms bond to achieve stable electron configurations. VSEPR theory predicts molecular shapes from electron-pair repulsion.

Key points

  • Ionic bonds form by complete transfer of electrons between metals and non-metals.
  • Covalent bonds form by sharing electrons, giving single, double or triple bonds.
  • VSEPR: electron pairs repel and arrange to minimise repulsion.
  • Lone pairs repel more strongly than bond pairs, compressing bond angles.
  • Common shapes: linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal and bent.

Examples

Water's bent shape. Oxygen has two lone pairs in water, which push the O–H bonds closer, making the bond angle about 104.5° instead of the tetrahedral 109.5°.

Methane versus ammonia. Methane (CH4) is tetrahedral at 109.5°; ammonia (NH3) has one lone pair, so it is trigonal pyramidal with an angle near 107°.

Practice

  1. Q. The shape of a methane molecule is: Ans. Tetrahedral. Four bonding pairs arrange tetrahedrally.
  2. Q. Lone pair–lone pair repulsion is: Ans. Stronger than bond pair–bond pair. Lone pairs occupy more space near the central atom.
  3. Q. Predict the shape of BF3. Ans. Boron has three bonding pairs and no lone pairs, so they spread 120° apart.

4.2 Hybridisation, Molecular Orbital Theory and Bond Parameters

Hybridisation explains geometry while molecular orbital theory explains magnetism and bond order. Bond length, order and energy describe bond strength.

Key points

  • sp: linear (180°); sp2: trigonal planar (120°); sp3: tetrahedral (109.5°).
  • Bond order = (bonding electrons − antibonding electrons)/2.
  • Higher bond order means shorter, stronger bonds.
  • O2 is paramagnetic because of unpaired electrons in π* orbitals.
  • Dipole moment measures bond polarity and molecular polarity.

Examples

Bond order of O2. MO theory gives bond order 2 and two unpaired electrons, correctly predicting that oxygen is paramagnetic.

Why CO2 is nonpolar. Each C=O bond is polar, but the linear shape makes the two dipoles cancel, so the molecule has zero net dipole moment.

Practice

  1. Q. Hybridisation in ethene is: Ans. sp2. The C=C double bond uses sp2 hybrid orbitals with a π bond.
  2. Q. Bond order of N2 is: Ans. 3. The triple bond gives bond order 3.
  3. Q. Why is the bond angle in water less than in ammonia? Ans. Water has two lone pairs versus one in ammonia, so lone-pair repulsion compresses the angle more.

5.1 Gas Laws and the Ideal Gas Equation

Gas behaviour is described by Boyle's, Charles' and Avogadro's laws, combined in the ideal gas equation.

Key points

  • Boyle's law: PV = constant at constant T.
  • Charles' law: V/T = constant at constant P.
  • Avogadro's law: V ∝ n at constant T and P.
  • Ideal gas equation: PV = nRT, with R = 8.314 J/mol·K = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K.
  • Dalton's law: total pressure is the sum of partial pressures.

Examples

Gas volume change. 1 mol of gas at 1 atm occupies 22.4 L at 273 K. At 546 K and the same pressure it occupies 44.8 L.

Using the ideal gas law. 2 mol at 300 K in 10 L: P = nRT/V = 2 × 0.0821 × 300/10 ≈ 4.93 atm.

Practice

  1. Q. Boyle's law states that at constant temperature: Ans. PV is constant. Pressure and volume are inversely proportional.
  2. Q. The value of R in SI units is: Ans. 8.314 J/mol·K. R = 8.314 J/mol·K in SI units.
  3. Q. A gas occupies 2 L at 1 atm. What volume at 2 atm, temperature unchanged? Ans. PV = constant, so V = 2 × 1/2 = 1 L.

5.2 Kinetic Molecular Theory and Real Gases

Kinetic theory links molecular motion to pressure and temperature; real gases deviate from ideal behaviour and are described by the van der Waals equation.

Key points

  • Gas pressure arises from molecular collisions with walls.
  • Average kinetic energy is proportional to absolute temperature.
  • Real gases deviate at high pressure and low temperature due to molecular volume and attractions.
  • van der Waals equation: (P + an2/V2)(V − nb) = nRT.
  • Critical temperature is the temperature above which a gas cannot be liquefied by pressure.

Examples

Why helium liquefies last. Helium has very weak intermolecular forces, so its critical temperature is extremely low (about 5.2 K).

Deviation of a real gas. Attractions reduce pressure and molecular volume reduces free space, so real gases deviate most when molecules are close together.

Practice

  1. Q. Real gases behave most ideally: Ans. At low pressure and high temperature. Molecules are far apart and interact little.
  2. Q. The van der Waals constant 'b' accounts for: Ans. Molecular volume. b corrects for the finite volume of molecules.
  3. Q. Why does a real gas deviate from ideal behaviour at high pressure? Ans. Molecules are forced close together, so their finite volume and intermolecular attractions become significant.

6.1 First Law and Enthalpy

Thermodynamics studies energy changes. Enthalpy measures heat exchanged at constant pressure.

Key points

  • First law: ΔU = q + w, where w is work done on the system.
  • Work done on a gas: w = −PΔV.
  • Enthalpy H = U + PV; ΔH = qp at constant pressure.
  • Exothermic reactions have ΔH < 0; endothermic have ΔH > 0.
  • Hess's law: enthalpy change is independent of the route.

Examples

Hess's law calculation. If the enthalpy of formation of CO2 is −394 kJ/mol and of CO is −111 kJ/mol, the combustion of CO releases 283 kJ/mol.

Enthalpy of a reaction. ΔH = ΣΔHf(products) − ΣΔHf(reactants) gives the heat of reaction from formation enthalpies.

Practice

  1. Q. For an exothermic reaction, ΔH is: Ans. Negative. Heat is released, so enthalpy decreases.
  2. Q. Hess's law follows from: Ans. Conservation of energy. Enthalpy is a state function, so the route does not matter.
  3. Q. State the first law of thermodynamics. Ans. Energy can be exchanged as heat or work, but the total energy of the universe is conserved: ΔU = q + w.

6.2 Entropy, Gibbs Energy and Spontaneity

The second law introduces entropy. Gibbs energy combines enthalpy and entropy to predict spontaneity.

Key points

  • Entropy S is a measure of disorder; ΔSuniverse > 0 for a spontaneous process.
  • Gibbs energy G = H − TS.
  • ΔG < 0 means spontaneous; ΔG = 0 means equilibrium; ΔG > 0 means non-spontaneous.
  • ΔG° = −RT ln K relates free energy to the equilibrium constant.
  • Exothermic reactions with increasing entropy are always spontaneous.

Examples

Melting ice. Above 0 °C, ΔG for melting is negative because the entropy gain outweighs the enthalpy cost; below 0 °C the reverse is true.

Thermodynamic versus kinetic control. A reaction may be thermodynamically favourable (ΔG < 0) but very slow; a catalyst affects only the rate.

Practice

  1. Q. A spontaneous reaction has: Ans. ΔG < 0. Negative Gibbs energy means spontaneity.
  2. Q. Entropy is a measure of: Ans. Disorder. Higher disorder means higher entropy.
  3. Q. Why is the combustion of fuels spontaneous even though it releases heat? Ans. It is strongly exothermic and increases entropy of the surroundings, giving ΔG < 0.

7.1 Chemical Equilibrium and Le Chatelier's Principle

Reversible reactions reach equilibrium when forward and backward rates are equal. Le Chatelier's principle predicts the response to change.

Key points

  • At equilibrium, concentrations are constant but reactions continue in both directions.
  • Kc = [products]n/[reactants]m for a balanced reaction.
  • Kp = Kc(RT)Δn for gases.
  • Le Chatelier: a system shifts to oppose the change imposed.
  • A catalyst speeds up both directions equally and does not change K.

Examples

Haber process. N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 is exothermic and reduces gas moles, so high pressure and moderate temperature favour ammonia.

Effect of removing product. Removing a product shifts the equilibrium to the right, increasing the forward reaction until equilibrium is re-established.

Practice

  1. Q. Adding a catalyst to an equilibrium: Ans. Does not change the equilibrium constant. It speeds both forward and reverse reactions equally.
  2. Q. Increasing pressure favours the side with: Ans. Fewer moles of gas. Le Chatelier's principle favours the smaller volume.
  3. Q. How does increasing temperature affect an exothermic equilibrium? Ans. It shifts the equilibrium to the left, decreasing the product concentration.

7.2 Ionic Equilibrium: Acids, Bases and Buffers

Acids and bases ionise in water. pH, buffers and solubility product describe ionic equilibria.

Key points

  • Kw = [H+][OH−] = 10−14 at 25 °C.
  • pH = −log[H+]; pOH = −log[OH−]; pH + pOH = 14.
  • Strong acids dissociate completely; weak acids partially (Ka small).
  • A buffer resists pH change and works best near pKa.
  • Henderson-Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]).

Examples

pH of a strong acid. 0.01 M HCl gives [H+] = 10−2 M and pH = 2.

Buffer in blood. The carbonic acid–bicarbonate buffer keeps blood pH near 7.4, protecting enzymes from pH swings.

Practice

  1. Q. The pH of a neutral solution at 25 °C is: Ans. 7. [H+] = [OH−] = 10−7 M at 25 °C.
  2. Q. A buffer works best when: Ans. pH ≈ pKa. Maximum buffering capacity is near the pKa.
  3. Q. Calculate the pH of 0.001 M HCl. Ans. pH = −log(10−3) = 3.

8.1 Oxidation Numbers and Redox

Redox reactions involve changes in oxidation state. Oxidation numbers track electron transfer.

Key points

  • Oxidation is loss of electrons (increase in oxidation number); reduction is gain.
  • Oxidation numbers follow rules: pure elements 0, oxygen usually −2, hydrogen usually +1.
  • Disproportionation: one element is simultaneously oxidised and reduced.
  • Oxidising agents are reduced; reducing agents are oxidised.
  • Balancing redox reactions uses the ion-electron method.

Examples

Oxidation number of Mn in KMnO4. +1 + x + 4(−2) = 0 gives x = +7.

Chlorine in bleach. Chlorine (0) in Cl2 disproportionates to Cl− (−1) and ClO− (+1) in alkaline solution.

Practice

  1. Q. Oxidation involves: Ans. Loss of electrons. Oxidation number increases as electrons are lost.
  2. Q. The oxidation number of oxygen in most compounds is: Ans. −2. Oxygen is usually −2 except in peroxides.
  3. Q. Find the oxidation number of sulphur in H2SO4. Ans. 2(+1) + x + 4(−2) = 0 gives x = +6.

8.2 Redox Titrations and Electrode Potentials

Redox reactions power titrations and electrochemical cells. Electrode potentials rank oxidising and reducing strength.

Key points

  • In a redox titration, a standard solution of known concentration oxidises or reduces the analyte.
  • Standard electrode potentials E° rank half-reactions by tendency to gain electrons.
  • A species with a higher E° is a stronger oxidising agent.
  • Cell potential E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode.
  • Redox reactions are central to corrosion and biological respiration.

Examples

Permanganate titration. KMnO4 in acidic medium oxidises Fe2+ to Fe3+, and the purple colour fades at the end-point.

Why zinc displaces copper. Zinc has a more negative reduction potential, so it is oxidised while copper ions are reduced and deposited.

Practice

  1. Q. In a galvanic cell, oxidation occurs at the: Ans. Anode. Anodes are where oxidation happens in all electrochemical cells.
  2. Q. A stronger oxidising agent has: Ans. Higher reduction potential. Higher E° means greater tendency to be reduced.
  3. Q. Define a redox reaction and give one example. Ans. A reaction in which oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously, e.g. Zn + CuSO4 -> ZnSO4 + Cu.

9.1 Hydrogen and Its Compounds

Hydrogen has unique chemistry with three isotopes. It forms hydrides and is important in energy and industry.

Key points

  • Isotopes: protium, deuterium and tritium.
  • Hydrides: ionic (NaH), covalent (HCl) and interstitial (with transition metals).
  • Hydrogen resembles alkali metals and halogens in some reactions, so its position is unique.
  • Heavy water (D2O) is used as a moderator in nuclear reactors.
  • Hydrogen is studied as a clean fuel, producing only water on combustion.

Examples

Why hydrogen has two positions. It loses one electron like alkali metals and gains one like halogens, so it can be placed with either group.

Hydrogen as fuel. Combustion gives 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O, releasing energy with no carbon dioxide, but storage and production costs are challenges.

Practice

  1. Q. Heavy water contains: Ans. Deuterium. D2O contains deuterium instead of protium.
  2. Q. Hydrogen can act as: Ans. Both an oxidising and reducing agent. It can gain or lose one electron.
  3. Q. Why is hydrogen called a clean fuel? Ans. It burns to give only water, with no carbon dioxide or soot.

9.2 Water, Hydrogen Peroxide and Hard Water

Water has unusual properties due to hydrogen bonding. Hydrogen peroxide is a useful oxidant, and hard water contains calcium and magnesium ions.

Key points

  • Water is a polar molecule with strong hydrogen bonds, giving high boiling point and surface tension.
  • Hardness is temporary (bicarbonates) or permanent (sulphates and chlorides of Ca and Mg).
  • Water hardness is determined by EDTA titration.
  • H2O2 is a strong oxidant and decomposes to water and oxygen.
  • Water pollution and purification involve filtration, chlorination and aeration.

Examples

Why ice floats. Hydrogen-bonded water molecules form an open lattice in ice, making it less dense than liquid water.

Bleaching with H2O2. Hydrogen peroxide bleaches by oxidation and breaks down into water and oxygen, making it an environment-friendly bleach.

Practice

  1. Q. Temporary hardness of water is due to: Ans. Calcium and magnesium bicarbonates. Bicarbonates can be removed by boiling.
  2. Q. Hydrogen peroxide decomposes into: Ans. Water and oxygen. 2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2.
  3. Q. Why does water have a high boiling point? Ans. Extensive hydrogen bonding requires a lot of energy to break before boiling.

10.1 Alkali and Alkaline Earth Metals

Group 1 and 2 metals are highly reactive, with trends in atomic size, ionisation energy and solubility.

Key points

  • Alkali metals (Li–Cs) react vigorously with water to give hydroxides and hydrogen.
  • Alkaline earth metals (Be–Ba) are less reactive but still reactive.
  • Reactivity increases down both groups as ionisation energy decreases.
  • Hydroxide solubility increases down group 2 for most compounds; sulphates decrease.
  • Flame colours help identify these metals.

Examples

Sodium in water. 2Na + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2 with vigorous fizzing and a yellow flame; potassium is even more violent.

Solubility trend. Beryllium hydroxide is nearly insoluble while barium hydroxide is quite soluble, due to lattice and hydration enthalpy changes.

Practice

  1. Q. Which group 1 metal reacts most vigorously with water? Ans. Caesium. Reactivity increases down the group.
  2. Q. Magnesium burns in air with a: Ans. Brilliant white flame. Magnesium gives a bright white light.
  3. Q. Why are alkali metals stored in kerosene? Ans. They react rapidly with air and moisture, so kerosene keeps them dry and unreactive.

10.2 Important Compounds and Biological Roles

Compounds of s-block metals are central to industry and life: sodium chloride, limestone, sodium hydroxide and calcium phosphate.

Key points

  • Sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) is made by electrolysis of brine and used in soap making.
  • Sodium carbonate is used in glass and detergents.
  • Calcium oxide (quicklime) and calcium hydroxide are used in mortar and whitewash.
  • Calcium and potassium ions are essential for nerves, muscles and bones.
  • Excess sodium in the diet is linked to high blood pressure.

Examples

Limestone in construction. Calcium carbonate decomposes to CaO and CO2 on heating; CaO with water gives slaked lime, used in mortar.

Potassium in nerves. Potassium ions maintain the resting potential of nerve cells; imbalance disturbs heartbeat and nerve signals.

Practice

  1. Q. Caustic soda is: Ans. Sodium hydroxide. NaOH is known as caustic soda.
  2. Q. Which compound is used to whitewash walls? Ans. Calcium hydroxide. Slaked lime forms a white protective layer.
  3. Q. Why is sodium important in the human body? Ans. Sodium maintains fluid balance, nerve impulses and muscle contraction.

11.1 Group 13 and 14 Elements

Boron and carbon groups show varied bonding and oxidation states, from covalent to metallic characters.

Key points

  • Group 13: B, Al, Ga, In, Tl; aluminium is the most important industrial metal.
  • Group 14: C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb; carbon shows catenation and silicon is the backbone of semiconductors.
  • Aluminium oxide is amphoteric; it reacts with acids and bases.
  • Carbon exists as diamond, graphite and fullerenes with very different properties.
  • Inert pair effect reduces the stability of the highest oxidation state down the group.

Examples

Diamond versus graphite. Diamond is hard because each carbon is tetrahedrally bonded; graphite is slippery because layers are weakly held and conduct electricity along the layers.

Silicon semiconductors. Silicon's four valence electrons form a stable crystal lattice whose conductivity can be controlled by doping.

Practice

  1. Q. Which is the hardest natural substance? Ans. Diamond. Diamond's rigid covalent network makes it hardest.
  2. Q. Aluminium oxide is: Ans. Amphoteric. It reacts with both acids and bases.
  3. Q. Why does graphite conduct electricity? Ans. Each carbon has a delocalised electron that is free to move along the layers.

11.2 Group 15 to 18 Elements

Nitrogen, oxygen, halogens and noble gases show trends in bonding, reactivity and physical state.

Key points

  • Group 15: N, P, As, Sb, Bi — nitrogen is a gas and phosphorus forms P4 molecules.
  • Group 16: O, S, Se, Te, Po — oxygen is a gas and sulphur forms S8 rings.
  • Halogens (17) are coloured, reactive non-metals; reactivity decreases down the group.
  • Noble gases are monatomic with very low reactivity; xenon forms some compounds.
  • Allotropes are important: oxygen/ozone, phosphorus white/red, sulphur rhombic/monoclinic.

Examples

Ozone versus oxygen. Ozone (O3) is a pale blue, reactive gas that absorbs ultraviolet light; oxygen (O2) is essential for respiration.

Halogen displacement. Chlorine displaces bromide from bromide solutions because chlorine is more reactive, showing the trend down group 17.

Practice

  1. Q. Which halogen is the most reactive? Ans. Fluorine. Reactivity decreases down group 17.
  2. Q. Phosphorus is stored in: Ans. Water. White phosphorus reacts with air and is kept under water.
  3. Q. Why are noble gases chemically inert? Ans. They have filled outer shells with very high ionisation energies and no tendency to gain electrons.

12.1 Representation and Isomerism

Organic compounds are based on carbon skeletons. Formulas can be condensed, expanded or shown as structures, and isomers share a formula but differ in arrangement.

Key points

  • Structural isomers differ in connectivity; stereoisomers differ in spatial arrangement.
  • Functional groups determine chemical properties.
  • Skeletal structures show the carbon backbone with hydrogens implied.
  • IUPAC names are systematic: prefix + root + suffix.
  • Degree of unsaturation indicates rings and double/triple bonds.

Examples

Naming a compound. CH3–CH2–OH is ethanol: a two-carbon root with an alcohol (−OH) functional group.

Structural isomers of butane. Butane (straight chain) and 2-methylpropane (branched) share C4H10 but have different structures and boiling points.

Practice

  1. Q. Isomers have the same: Ans. Molecular formula. Isomers differ in Arrangement but share the formula.
  2. Q. The IUPAC suffix for an alcohol is: Ans. −ol. Alcohols are named with the suffix −ol.
  3. Q. Write the IUPAC name of CH3COOH. Ans. Two carbons with a −COOH group gives ethanoic acid.

12.2 Electronic Effects and Reaction Types

Organic reactions are classified by how bonds break and form. Inductive, resonance and hyperconjugation effects explain stability and reactivity.

Key points

  • Homolytic cleavage gives radicals; heterolytic gives ions.
  • Inductive effect: electron withdrawal or donation along sigma bonds.
  • Resonance delocalises π electrons over several atoms.
  • Electrophiles are electron-poor; nucleophiles are electron-rich.
  • Reactions: substitution, addition, elimination and rearrangement.

Examples

Why benzene is stable. Delocalised π electrons around the ring (resonance) give benzene extra stability, so it undergoes substitution rather than addition.

Markovnikov's rule. In addition of HX to propene, hydrogen adds to the carbon with more hydrogens, giving the more stable carbocation intermediate.

Practice

  1. Q. A nucleophile is: Ans. Electron-rich. Nucleophiles donate electron pairs to electron-poor centres.
  2. Q. Benzene undergoes mainly: Ans. Substitution. Aromatic stability favours substitution over addition.
  3. Q. Why does benzene not decolourise bromine water? Ans. Its delocalised π system is stable, so it does not undergo addition like alkenes.

13.1 Alkanes, Alkenes and Alkynes

Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen. Saturation determines their reactions and properties.

Key points

  • Alkanes are saturated and undergo substitution by free radicals.
  • Alkenes have C=C double bonds and undergo addition reactions.
  • Alkynes have C≡C triple bonds and also undergo addition; they are acidic at terminal carbons.
  • Markovnikov's rule predicts the major product in addition to alkenes.
  • Boiling points increase with chain length and branching lowers them.

Examples

Bromine water test. Bromine water decolourises with an alkene or alkyne (addition) but not with an alkane, so it distinguishes unsaturated from saturated hydrocarbons.

Combustion and soot. Alkanes burn with a clean flame when oxygen is plentiful; alkynes with more carbon content tend to produce smoky flames.

Practice

  1. Q. Alkanes react with halogens by: Ans. Substitution. Free-radical substitution replaces a hydrogen.
  2. Q. Which hydrocarbon decolourises bromine water? Ans. Ethene. Unsaturated hydrocarbons add bromine and decolourise the solution.
  3. Q. Why do alkanes make good fuels? Ans. They are highly reduced and release a lot of heat on complete combustion.

13.2 Aromatic Hydrocarbons

Benzene and its derivatives are aromatic: planar rings with delocalised π electrons conferring extra stability.

Key points

  • Aromatic compounds follow Hückel's rule: 4n + 2 delocalised π electrons.
  • Benzene has six delocalised π electrons over a planar ring.
  • Electrophilic aromatic substitution is the main reaction type.
  • Substituents direct incoming groups ortho/para or meta.
  • Fossil fuels and incomplete combustion release aromatic pollutants such as benzene.

Examples

Nitration of benzene. Benzene reacts with mixed acid (HNO3 + H2SO4) to give nitrobenzene, a classic electrophilic aromatic substitution.

Methylbenzene and nitration. A methyl group directs incoming groups to ortho and para positions because it donates electron density by hyperconjugation.

Practice

  1. Q. Benzene has: Ans. 6 delocalised π electrons. Six π electrons satisfy Hückel's rule for n = 1.
  2. Q. A methyl group on benzene is: Ans. Ortho-para directing. Electron-donating groups direct ortho and para.
  3. Q. Why is benzene unusually stable? Ans. Its delocalised π electrons are spread over the ring, lowering the energy of the molecule.

14.1 Air Pollution and Its Effects

Pollutants from combustion and industry damage health, ecosystems and buildings.

Key points

  • Primary pollutants are emitted directly; secondary pollutants form in the atmosphere.
  • CO binds to haemoglobin more strongly than oxygen, causing suffocation.
  • SO2 and NOx cause acid rain, damaging soil, water and monuments.
  • Photochemical smog forms when nitrogen oxides and hydrocarbons react in sunlight.
  • Particulates cause respiratory disease and reduce visibility.

Examples

Photochemical smog. In sunny cities, NOx and volatile organic compounds react to form ozone and other oxidants, creating brown smog that irritates lungs.

Taj Mahal corrosion. Sulphur dioxide from industry forms acid rain that attacks the marble, turning it yellow and pitted.

Practice

  1. Q. Carbon monoxide is dangerous because it: Ans. Binds haemoglobin more strongly than oxygen. It reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood.
  2. Q. Acid rain is caused mainly by: Ans. SO2 and NOx. These oxides form acids with water.
  3. Q. Why is photochemical smog worse on sunny days? Ans. Sunlight drives the reactions that produce ozone and other oxidants.

14.2 Water and Soil Pollution

Contaminants in water and soil harm ecosystems and human health. Treatment and responsible practice reduce pollution.

Key points

  • BOD measures the oxygen demand of organic matter in water; high BOD means pollution.
  • Eutrophication is nutrient enrichment that causes algal blooms and oxygen depletion.
  • Pesticides and heavy metals accumulate in soils and food chains.
  • Industrial effluent must be treated before discharge.
  • Green chemistry aims to reduce hazardous materials from the start.

Examples

BOD and sewage. Untreated sewage has a high BOD because bacteria consume oxygen to decompose organic matter, leaving less oxygen for fish.

Reducing pollution at source. Using biodegradable detergents avoids persistent foams and reduces harm to aquatic life.

Practice

  1. Q. High BOD indicates: Ans. Organic pollution. More organic matter means more oxygen is consumed by bacteria.
  2. Q. Eutrophication leads to: Ans. Oxygen depletion. Algal blooms die and decomposition uses up oxygen.
  3. Q. What is green chemistry? Ans. Designing chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate hazardous substances.
