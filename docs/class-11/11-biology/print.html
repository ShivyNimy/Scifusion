1.1 Taxonomy and Taxonomic Hierarchy

Taxonomy identifies, names and classifies organisms. The hierarchical system groups species into increasingly broad categories.

Key points

  • Taxonomic categories: species, genus, family, order, class, phylum/division, kingdom.
  • Binomial nomenclature: genus + specific epithet, written in italics (Homo sapiens).
  • Taxonomical aids include herbaria, botanical gardens, museums, zoological parks and keys.
  • A species is the fundamental unit of taxonomy.
  • Systematics studies the diversity and evolutionary relationships of organisms.

Examples

Naming a mango. Mangifera indica: Mangifera is the genus, indica the species. The name is universal and avoids confusion across languages.

Herbarium sheet. A pressed, dried and labelled plant specimen preserves the plant's identity for reference and future study.

Practice

  1. Q. The correct way to write a scientific name is: Ans. Genus capitalised, species lowercase, italicised. Binomial names follow strict capitalisation and italicisation rules.
  2. Q. The basic unit of classification is the: Ans. Species. Species is the basic taxonomic unit.
  3. Q. Arrange in increasing order: order, species, family, genus, class. Ans. Species -> genus -> family -> order -> class.

1.2 Diversity of Life and Its Measurement

Biodiversity is unevenly distributed; some regions are exceptionally rich and are called hotspots. Classification keeps the variety manageable.

Key points

  • About 1.7–1.8 million species have been described, but many more exist.
  • India is a megadiverse country with several biodiversity hotspots.
  • Hotspots include the Western Ghats, Himalaya, Indo-Burma and Sundaland (parts in India).
  • Taxonomic keys use pairs of contrasting statements (couplets).
  • Museums and zoological parks preserve specimens and living collections.

Examples

Western Ghats hotspot. The Western Ghats are home to thousands of endemic plants and animals, which is why they are protected as a biodiversity hotspot.

Using a key. A dichotomous key asks whether a plant has, for example, net-like or parallel veins, leading step by step to the correct group.

Practice

  1. Q. Which is a biodiversity hotspot in India? Ans. Western Ghats. The Western Ghats are recognised globally for high endemism.
  2. Q. A herbarium stores: Ans. Preserved plant specimens. Herbaria keep dried, pressed and labelled plant specimens.
  3. Q. What is a taxonomic key? Ans. A device of paired contrasting statements used to identify organisms step by step.

2.1 The Five Kingdom System

Organisms are classified into five kingdoms based on cell type, nutrition and body organisation.

Key points

  • Monera: prokaryotic, includes bacteria and cyanobacteria.
  • Protista: eukaryotic unicellular organisms such as Paramecium and diatoms.
  • Fungi: heterotrophic, saprophytic, cell walls of chitin.
  • Plantae: autotrophic eukaryotes with cell walls of cellulose.
  • Animalia: heterotrophic, multicellular eukaryotes without cell walls.

Examples

Why fungi are not plants. Fungi lack chlorophyll, have chitinous walls and absorb nutrients from dead or living matter, which is why they form their own kingdom.

Kingdom Protista. Amoeba and Paramecium are single eukaryotic cells that carry out all life processes, showing the diversity of protists.

Practice

  1. Q. Which kingdom includes cyanobacteria? Ans. Monera. Cyanobacteria are prokaryotes in Monera.
  2. Q. Fungal cell walls contain: Ans. Chitin. Chitin is characteristic of fungi.
  3. Q. Name the five kingdoms and one example of each. Ans. Monera (bacteria), Protista (Amoeba), Fungi (yeast), Plantae (mango), Animalia (human).

2.2 Viruses, Viroids, Prions and Lichens

Viruses are acellular infectious agents. Viroids and prions are simpler, and lichens are obligate partnerships.

Key points

  • Viruses contain nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein capsid, and are obligate parasites.
  • Viroids are naked RNA molecules that infect plants.
  • Prions are infectious proteins that cause neurological diseases.
  • Lichens are symbiotic associations of algae and fungi.
  • Viruses show characteristics of both living and non-living things.

Examples

Tobacco mosaic virus. TMV is an RNA virus causing mosaic patterns on tobacco leaves; its discovery founded virology.

Lichens as pollution indicators. Lichens are sensitive to air pollution, so their presence indicates clean air and their absence signals poor air quality.

Practice

  1. Q. A virus contains: Ans. Nucleic acid and a protein capsid. The capsid encloses the genetic material.
  2. Q. A lichen is an association of: Ans. Alga and fungus. The alga photosynthesises and the fungus provides shelter.
  3. Q. Why are viruses considered both living and non-living? Ans. They have genetic material and can reproduce inside a host, but outside they are inert.

3.1 Algae to Gymnosperms

Plants are grouped by vascular tissue, seeds and flowers: algae, bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms and angiosperms.

Key points

  • Algae are mostly aquatic and lack true roots, stems and leaves.
  • Bryophytes (mosses) show the first true multicellular body but need water for reproduction.
  • Pteridophytes (ferns) have vascular tissue and reproduce by spores.
  • Gymnosperms have naked seeds and no fruit; many are evergreen conifers.
  • Angiosperms produce flowers and fruits and dominate modern plants.

Examples

Fern life cycle. Ferns alternate between a dominant spore-producing plant and a small gametophyte that needs water for fertilisation.

Why bryophytes are amphibious. They live on land but need water for sperm to swim to the egg, like amphibians.

Practice

  1. Q. Which group produces naked seeds? Ans. Gymnosperms. Gymnosperm seeds are not enclosed in fruits.
  2. Q. Ferns reproduce by: Ans. Spores. Pteridophytes produce spores.
  3. Q. List the plant groups in evolutionary order. Ans. Algae -> bryophytes -> pteridophytes -> gymnosperms -> angiosperms.

3.2 Angiosperms and Plant Life Cycles

Angiosperms are flowering plants with enclosed seeds. Their life cycle alternates between sporophyte and gametophyte generations.

Key points

  • Angiosperms are divided into dicots and monocots.
  • Flowers may be unisexual or bisexual; pollination precedes fertilisation.
  • Double fertilisation produces a zygote and a triploid endosperm in angiosperms.
  • Alternation of generations: diploid sporophyte and haploid gametophyte.
  • Fruits protect seeds and aid dispersal.

Examples

Double fertilisation. One male gamete fuses with the egg to form the zygote, while another fuses with two polar nuclei to form the triploid endosperm — unique to angiosperms.

Monocot versus dicot. Monocots have parallel veins and one cotyledon; dicots have net veins and two cotyledons.

Practice

  1. Q. Endosperm in angiosperms is: Ans. Triploid. Double fertilisation produces triploid endosperm.
  2. Q. Which group dominates modern vegetation? Ans. Angiosperms. Flowering plants are the most diverse and widespread.
  3. Q. What is alternation of generations? Ans. The life cycle alternates between a haploid gametophyte and a diploid sporophyte generation.

4.1 Invertebrate Phyla

Animals are grouped by body symmetry, body cavity and development. Invertebrates include sponges through echinoderms.

Key points

  • Porifera (sponges): cellular level, water canal system.
  • Cnidaria: radial symmetry with stinging cells (Hydra, jellyfish).
  • Platyhelminthes: flatworms; Aschelminthes: roundworms.
  • Annelida: segmented worms (earthworm); Arthropoda: jointed legs and exoskeleton (insects).
  • Mollusca: soft bodies with shells; Echinodermata: spiny skinned with water-vascular system.

Examples

Why arthropods are so successful. Jointed appendages, a protective exoskeleton and metamorphosis allow insects to exploit almost every habitat.

Earthworm and soil. Segmented bodies and setae let earthworms burrow, mixing and aerating the soil.

Practice

  1. Q. Which phylum has jointed legs? Ans. Arthropoda. Arthropods have jointed appendages and exoskeletons.
  2. Q. Sponges belong to the phylum: Ans. Porifera. Porifera are sponges.
  3. Q. Name three features of arthropods. Ans. Jointed legs, exoskeleton and segmented body (also compound eyes).

4.2 Vertebrate Classes

Vertebrates have a backbone and are divided into fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

Key points

  • Fish are aquatic with gills and scales; they include cartilaginous and bony fish.
  • Amphibians live in water and on land and have moist skin.
  • Reptiles have dry scaly skin and lay shelled eggs on land.
  • Birds have feathers, wings, beaks and are endothermic.
  • Mammals have hair, mammary glands and most give birth to live young.

Examples

Why amphibians depend on water. Their eggs lack shells and their larvae have gills, so breeding requires water or moisture.

Bird flight adaptations. Hollow bones, feathers, air sacs and powerful muscles reduce weight and increase oxygen supply.

Practice

  1. Q. Which group is warm-blooded? Ans. Birds and mammals. Birds and mammals maintain constant body temperature.
  2. Q. Reptiles lay eggs: Ans. On land with leathery shells. Shelled eggs protect embryos on dry land.
  3. Q. Give two distinguishing features of mammals. Ans. Hair on the body and mammary glands for feeding young.

5.1 Root, Stem and Leaf Modifications

Plant organs are adapted for storage, support and survival. Modifications reveal function.

Key points

  • Tap roots store food (carrot, turnip); prop roots support banyan; pneumatophores aid breathing in mangroves.
  • Stems may be underground (potato, ginger) or modified for climbing (tendrils).
  • Leaves may become spines (cactus) or tendrils (pea) and may store water (aloe).
  • Phyllotaxy is the arrangement of leaves on the stem.
  • Modifications are adaptations to environment and function.

Examples

Mangrove pneumatophores. Roots grow upwards above the mud to take in air in waterlogged soil, an adaptation to saline, oxygen-poor habitats.

Cactus spines. Leaves reduced to spines reduce water loss, while the green stem performs photosynthesis.

Practice

  1. Q. Potato is a modified: Ans. Stem. The potato tuber is an underground stem with eyes (buds).
  2. Q. Tendrils in peas are modified: Ans. Leaves. Pea tendrils are modified leaf parts that help climbing.
  3. Q. Why are cactus leaves reduced to spines? Ans. To reduce transpiration and protect the plant while the green stem carries out photosynthesis.

5.2 Flower, Fruit and Seed Structure

The flower contains the reproductive organs. Fruits and seeds protect and disperse the embryo.

Key points

  • A typical flower has calyx (sepals), corolla (petals), androecium (stamens) and gynoecium (carpels).
  • The ovary contains ovules; after fertilisation it becomes the fruit.
  • Aestivation is the arrangement of sepals or petals in a bud.
  • Placentation is the arrangement of ovules within the ovary.
  • Seeds contain an embryo, cotyledons and a seed coat.

Examples

Reading a flower in the field. Count the sepals, petals, stamens and carpels. The numbers and fusion help identify the family and species.

From ovary to fruit. The ovary wall becomes the pericarp; the ovule becomes the seed. The fruit protects and helps disperse the seed.

Practice

  1. Q. The gynoecium consists of: Ans. Carpels. Carpels are the female reproductive organs.
  2. Q. The fruit develops from the: Ans. Ovary. The ovary enlarges into the fruit after fertilisation.
  3. Q. Name the four whorls of a flower. Ans. Calyx, corolla, androecium and gynoecium.

6.1 Tissues and Tissue Systems

Plant anatomy studies internal structure. Tissues form three systems: epidermal, ground and vascular.

Key points

  • Epidermal tissue covers surfaces; guard cells regulate stomata.
  • Ground tissue includes parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma.
  • Vascular tissue is xylem (water) and phloem (food).
  • Xylem elements: tracheids, vessels, xylem fibres and parenchyma.
  • Phloem elements: sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem fibres and parenchyma.

Examples

Why sclerenchyma is dead at maturity. Thick lignified walls provide strength but leave no room for a living protoplast, so the cells are dead when mature.

Guard cells. Swelling and shrinking of guard cells opens and closes stomata, controlling gas exchange and water loss.

Practice

  1. Q. Which tissue provides mechanical strength to plants? Ans. Sclerenchyma. Lignified sclerenchyma fibres give strength.
  2. Q. Water-conducting elements of xylem are: Ans. Tracheids and vessels. Tracheids and vessels transport water.
  3. Q. Differentiate xylem and phloem on the basis of function. Ans. Xylem transports water and minerals; phloem transports food.

6.2 Monocot and Dicot Anatomy

Monocot and dicot stems and roots differ in vascular bundle arrangement, which helps identify plant groups.

Key points

  • Dicot stems have vascular bundles in a ring; monocot stems have scattered bundles.
  • Dicot roots have a central xylem core; monocot roots have a pith.
  • Secondary growth occurs in dicots through the cambium.
  • Monocots lack secondary growth because they have no cambium ring.
  • Wood is secondary xylem formed by cambial activity.

Examples

Tree rings and age. Each year's secondary xylem forms one ring, so counting rings estimates the age of a tree.

Bamboo has scattered bundles. As a monocot, bamboo has scattered vascular bundles and shows no secondary thickening.

Practice

  1. Q. Vascular bundles in dicot stems are arranged: Ans. In a ring. A ring of bundles is typical of dicots.
  2. Q. Secondary growth is due to the: Ans. Cambium. The cambium produces secondary xylem and phloem.
  3. Q. Why do monocots not show secondary growth? Ans. They lack a vascular cambium, so they cannot form secondary tissues.

7.1 Animal Tissues

Animal tissues are epithelial, connective, muscular and nervous. Their structure matches their function.

Key points

  • Epithelium may be simple or compound; it covers, protects and secretes.
  • Connective tissue includes loose, dense, cartilage, bone and blood.
  • Muscle tissue is skeletal, smooth or cardiac.
  • Nervous tissue conducts impulses through neurons.
  • Tissues combine into organs and organs into systems.

Examples

Tendon versus ligament. Tendons attach muscle to bone and ligaments attach bone to bone; both are dense connective tissue with parallel fibres.

Blood as connective tissue. Blood has cells suspended in plasma and connects organs by transporting substances, so it is a fluid connective tissue.

Practice

  1. Q. Which tissue joins bone to bone? Ans. Ligament. Ligaments connect bones at joints.
  2. Q. Smooth muscle is found in the: Ans. Intestine. Smooth muscle lines internal organs and is involuntary.
  3. Q. Name the four types of animal tissue. Ans. Epithelial, connective, muscular and nervous.

7.2 Frog, Earthworm and Cockroach Anatomy

Studying representative animals shows how organ systems are arranged to support different lifestyles.

Key points

  • The frog has moist skin, lungs and a three-chambered heart.
  • The earthworm has a segmented body, closed circulatory system and nephridia for excretion.
  • The cockroach has an exoskeleton, tracheal respiration, Malpighian tubules and a dorsal heart.
  • Organ systems are integrated with the organism's habitat and feeding habits.
  • Dissection studies reveal comparative anatomy.

Examples

Frog respiration. Frogs breathe through skin on land and in water and use lungs when active, an adaptation to amphibious life.

Cockroach excretion. Malpighian tubules remove nitrogenous wastes into the gut for excretion, conserving water in a dry habitat.

Practice

  1. Q. The heart of a frog has: Ans. Three chambers. Two atria and one ventricle.
  2. Q. Excretory organs of an earthworm are: Ans. Nephridia. Nephridia remove nitrogenous waste.
  3. Q. Why is the earthworm's circulatory system called closed? Ans. Blood flows entirely within vessels and is pumped by hearts.

8.1 Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

Cells are prokaryotic or eukaryotic. Eukaryotic cells contain membrane-bound organelles that compartmentalise functions.

Key points

  • Prokaryotes lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; they have a cell wall and 70S ribosomes.
  • Eukaryotes have a true nucleus and organelles such as mitochondria, ER and Golgi.
  • The plasma membrane is a fluid mosaic of lipids and proteins.
  • Cell walls of plants are cellulose; fungi have chitin; bacteria have peptidoglycan.
  • Cell size and shape reflect function.

Examples

Why prokaryotes are efficient. Their small size gives a high surface-area-to-volume ratio, so diffusion alone can supply nutrients and remove wastes.

Fluid mosaic model. Proteins float in or on a phospholipid bilayer, allowing lateral movement and selective transport.

Practice

  1. Q. Which is found in prokaryotes? Ans. 70S ribosome. Prokaryotes have 70S ribosomes and lack membrane-bound organelles.
  2. Q. The cell wall of plants is made of: Ans. Cellulose. Cellulose is the main structural polysaccharide in plant cell walls.
  3. Q. Why is the plasma membrane called selectively permeable? Ans. It allows some substances to pass while restricting others.

8.2 Cell Organelles

Organelles perform specialised tasks: energy conversion, protein synthesis, transport, digestion and storage.

Key points

  • Mitochondria produce ATP and contain their own DNA.
  • Ribosomes synthesise proteins; ER transports and modifies them.
  • Golgi complex packages and exports materials.
  • Lysosomes digest waste; peroxisomes handle oxidative reactions.
  • Plastids carry out photosynthesis and storage in plants.

Examples

Why mitochondria have folded inner membranes. Cristae increase the surface area for the electron transport chain and ATP synthesis.

Ribosome factory. Ribosomes translate mRNA into polypeptide chains, the first step of protein synthesis.

Practice

  1. Q. Which organelle packages and exports proteins? Ans. Golgi complex. The Golgi modifies, packages and exports materials.
  2. Q. Chloroplasts are found in: Ans. Plant cells. Chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis in plant cells.
  3. Q. Why are lysosomes called suicidal bags? Ans. Under stress they may rupture and release digestive enzymes, damaging the cell.

9.1 Carbohydrates, Proteins, Lipids and Nucleic Acids

Biomolecules are the building blocks of life. Each class has characteristic structure and function.

Key points

  • Carbohydrates are energy sources and structural materials (starch, cellulose, glycogen).
  • Proteins are polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds and perform many functions.
  • Lipids are hydrophobic molecules used for membranes and energy storage.
  • Nucleic acids (DNA, RNA) store and transmit genetic information.
  • Enzymes are proteins that catalyse biological reactions.

Examples

Why cooking changes an egg. Heat denatures egg proteins, breaking weak bonds so the proteins unfold and coagulate irreversibly.

DNA versus RNA. DNA stores genetic information with deoxyribose and thymine; RNA carries information and uses ribose and uracil.

Practice

  1. Q. Proteins are polymers of: Ans. Amino acids. Peptide bonds link amino acids into proteins.
  2. Q. Which biomolecule is hydrophobic? Ans. Lipid. Lipids repel water.
  3. Q. What is the difference between DNA and RNA? Ans. DNA has deoxyribose and thymine and stores information; RNA has ribose and uracil and usually carries information.

9.2 Enzymes and Their Kinetics

Enzymes lower activation energy and are specific. Their activity depends on pH, temperature and concentration.

Key points

  • Enzymes are biological catalysts, mostly proteins.
  • They lower activation energy by stabilising transition states.
  • Optimum temperature and pH maximise enzyme activity.
  • Competitive inhibitors bind the active site; non-competitive inhibitors bind elsewhere.
  • Michaelis-Menten kinetics describes initial rates; Km indicates substrate affinity.

Examples

Why fever makes you feel weak. High temperature denatures enzymes and disturbs metabolism, which is why fever is dangerous.

Enzyme in the stomach. Pepsin works best at acidic pH, while intestinal enzymes prefer alkaline conditions — the environment matches the organ.

Practice

  1. Q. Enzymes increase the rate of reaction by: Ans. Lowering activation energy. Enzymes lower the activation energy barrier.
  2. Q. A competitive inhibitor binds to the: Ans. Active site. It competes with the substrate for the active site.
  3. Q. Why is enzyme activity reduced at very high temperature? Ans. The enzyme's tertiary structure denatures, so the active site loses its shape and function.

10.1 Mitosis and Meiosis

Cells divide for growth and reproduction. Mitosis gives identical cells; meiosis produces gametes with genetic variation.

Key points

  • The cell cycle has interphase (G1, S, G2) and mitosis.
  • In the S phase DNA replicates, doubling chromosome content.
  • Mitosis stages: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase.
  • Meiosis has two divisions and produces four haploid cells.
  • Crossing over in prophase I creates genetic variation.

Examples

Why crossing over matters. Genetic recombination in meiosis produces new combinations of alleles, increasing variation in offspring.

Cancer and the cell cycle. Cancer results from loss of cell-cycle control, causing uncontrolled division.

Practice

  1. Q. DNA replicates during: Ans. S phase. Synthesis of DNA occurs in S phase.
  2. Q. Meiosis produces: Ans. Four haploid cells. Meiosis halves the chromosome number.
  3. Q. Differentiate mitosis and meiosis. Ans. Mitosis gives two identical diploid cells for growth; meiosis gives four haploid gametes with genetic variation.

10.2 Significance of Cell Division

Cell division maintains body size, repairs damage and enables reproduction. Uncontrolled division causes disease.

Key points

  • Mitosis replaces worn-out cells and heals wounds.
  • In plants, meristems divide throughout life to produce new growth.
  • Meiosis maintains the chromosome number across generations.
  • Apoptosis is programmed cell death, balancing division.
  • Checkpoints ensure damage is repaired before division.

Examples

Wound healing. Cells around a cut divide by mitosis, filling the gap and restoring the tissue.

Programmed cell death. Apoptosis removes damaged or unwanted cells; loss of apoptosis contributes to cancer.

Practice

  1. Q. Programmed cell death is called: Ans. Apoptosis. Apoptosis is controlled, programmed cell death.
  2. Q. Which cells divide throughout plant life? Ans. Meristematic cells. Meristems contain actively dividing cells.
  3. Q. Why are checkpoints important in the cell cycle? Ans. They verify that DNA is intact and replicated before division, preventing the propagation of errors.

11.1 Water Potential, Plasmolysis and Absorption

Water moves by osmosis from high to low water potential. Plant cells use water potential and pressure to regulate turgor.

Key points

  • Water potential ψw = ψs + ψp (solute and pressure potentials).
  • Osmosis moves water toward lower water potential.
  • Turgid cells have positive pressure potential; plasmolysed cells lose turgor.
  • Root hairs increase surface area for water absorption.
  • Mineral ions are often actively absorbed using energy.

Examples

Fresh lettuce in salt water. Salt water has lower water potential than the cells, so water leaves and the lettuce wilts — plasmolysis.

Root pressure. Active ion transport into the root draws water in and pushes it up, explaining guttation in some plants.

Practice

  1. Q. Water moves by osmosis from: Ans. Higher water potential to lower. Water moves down the water potential gradient.
  2. Q. Plasmolysis occurs when a cell is placed in: Ans. Hypertonic solution. Water leaves the cell in a hypertonic solution.
  3. Q. Define water potential and give its units. Ans. The potential energy of water per unit volume relative to pure water; measured in bars or pascals.

11.2 Transpiration, Ascent of Sap and Translocation

Water moves up tall trees mainly by transpiration pull; food moves through the phloem by pressure flow.

Key points

  • Transpiration is water loss from leaves through stomata.
  • Cohesion-tension theory explains the ascent of sap: transpiration pull draws a continuous water column.
  • Guttation is the loss of water droplets from leaf margins.
  • Translocation moves sugars in the phloem from source to sink.
  • The pressure-flow hypothesis explains phloem transport.

Examples

Why trees wilt in drought. Transpiration exceeds water uptake, so cells lose turgor and leaves droop.

Sugar transport in spring. Stored sugars move from roots (source) to developing buds (sink) through the phloem.

Practice

  1. Q. Ascent of sap is mainly explained by: Ans. Cohesion-tension theory. Transpiration pull drives water up the xylem.
  2. Q. Food is transported in: Ans. Phloem. Phloem carries sugars and other solutes.
  3. Q. Why does transpiration pull work in tall trees? Ans. Water molecules cohere, forming a continuous column that is pulled up by evaporation at the leaves.

12.1 Macro and Micronutrients

Plants need mineral nutrients in different amounts. Deficiency causes specific symptoms.

Key points

  • Macronutrients: C, H, O, N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S.
  • Micronutrients: Fe, Mn, Cu, Mo, Zn, B, Cl.
  • Nitrogen is needed for proteins and chlorophyll; deficiency causes chlorosis.
  • Nutrients are absorbed from soil solution by roots.
  • Deficiency symptoms appear in specific parts of the plant.

Examples

Nitrogen deficiency. Leaves turn pale yellow because chlorophyll synthesis is limited and growth slows.

Chlorosis versus necrosis. Chlorosis is yellowing from loss of chlorophyll; necrosis is death of tissue from severe deficiency.

Practice

  1. Q. Which is a macronutrient? Ans. Nitrogen. Nitrogen is needed in large amounts.
  2. Q. Deficiency of nitrogen causes: Ans. Chlorosis. Pale yellow leaves indicate nitrogen deficiency.
  3. Q. Why are micronutrients needed in small amounts? Ans. They serve as cofactors and catalysts rather than structural building materials.

12.2 Nitrogen Fixation and Nitrogen Cycle

Plants cannot use atmospheric nitrogen directly. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert it into usable compounds.

Key points

  • Nitrogen fixation converts N2 into ammonia.
  • Rhizobium fixes nitrogen in root nodules of legumes.
  • Azotobacter and cyanobacteria also fix nitrogen.
  • Nitrification converts ammonia to nitrite and nitrate.
  • Denitrification returns nitrogen to the atmosphere.

Examples

Root nodules. Rhizobium in root nodules receives carbon compounds and supplies nitrogen compounds to the plant — a mutualistic symbiosis.

Crop rotation with legumes. Growing legumes restores soil nitrogen, reducing the need for nitrogen fertiliser.

Practice

  1. Q. Which bacterium fixes nitrogen in legume roots? Ans. Rhizobium. Rhizobium forms nitrogen-fixing nodules on legume roots.
  2. Q. Nitrification converts ammonia to: Ans. Nitrate. Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonia to nitrate.
  3. Q. How does adding legumes improve soil fertility? Ans. Symbiotic nitrogen fixation adds usable nitrogen compounds to the soil.

13.1 Light Reactions and Photosystems

Photosynthesis uses light energy to produce ATP and NADPH, which drive sugar synthesis.

Key points

  • Chlorophyll a and b absorb light, mainly in blue and red regions.
  • Photosystem II splits water, releasing oxygen and electrons.
  • The electron transport chain pumps protons, producing a gradient used to make ATP (photophosphorylation).
  • Photosystem I produces NADPH.
  • Light reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes.

Examples

Effect of green light. Chlorophyll reflects green light, so it is least effective for photosynthesis, which is why a leaf appears green.

Where the oxygen comes from. Oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from water, not carbon dioxide — shown by isotope experiments.

Practice

  1. Q. Oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from: Ans. Water. Photolysis of water in PSII releases oxygen.
  2. Q. Light reactions occur in the: Ans. Thylakoid membranes. The thylakoids contain the photosystems.
  3. Q. Why is chlorophyll green? Ans. It absorbs red and blue light strongly and reflects green light.

13.2 Calvin Cycle and Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

The Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugar. Many factors limit the rate.

Key points

  • The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma of the chloroplast.
  • CO2 is fixed by RuBisCO onto RuBP.
  • The cycle uses ATP and NADPH to reduce carbon compounds to sugar.
  • C3 plants fix CO2 once; C4 plants fix it twice and are more efficient in hot climates.
  • Limiting factors include light, CO2 concentration and temperature.

Examples

C4 advantage. C4 plants concentrate CO2 in bundle sheath cells, reducing photorespiration and boosting productivity in heat.

Greenhouse CO2 enrichment. Increasing CO2 can increase photosynthesis, up to the point where another factor becomes limiting.

Practice

  1. Q. The Calvin cycle occurs in the: Ans. Stroma. Carbon fixation happens in the stroma.
  2. Q. The enzyme that fixes CO2 in C3 plants is: Ans. RuBisCO. RuBisCO catalyses the first step of carbon fixation.
  3. Q. Why are C4 plants more efficient in hot climates? Ans. They concentrate CO2 at the site of fixation, minimising photorespiration.

14.1 Glycolysis, Krebs Cycle and Electron Transport

Aerobic respiration releases energy from glucose in three stages: glycolysis, Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation.

Key points

  • Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm and yields 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose.
  • Pyruvate enters mitochondria and is converted to acetyl CoA.
  • Krebs cycle occurs in the mitochondrial matrix and yields CO2, ATP, NADH and FADH2.
  • The electron transport chain and ATP synthase produce most ATP.
  • The overall aerobic yield is about 30–38 ATP per glucose depending on shuttle efficiency.

Examples

Why aerobic respiration yields more ATP. Complete oxidation transfers more electrons to the electron transport chain, producing far more ATP than fermentation.

Respiratory quotient. RQ = CO2 released ÷ O2 consumed; it is 1 for carbohydrates and less than 1 for fats.

Practice

  1. Q. Glycolysis occurs in the: Ans. Cytoplasm. Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm.
  2. Q. Most ATP is produced by: Ans. Oxidative phosphorylation. The electron transport chain produces most ATP.
  3. Q. Write the overall equation for aerobic respiration. Ans. C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy (about 30–38 ATP).

14.2 Anaerobic Respiration and Amphibolic Pathway

Fermentation allows energy release without oxygen, but yields much less ATP. The respiratory pathway is amphibolic.

Key points

  • In yeast, pyruvate is converted to ethanol and CO2.
  • In muscles, pyruvate becomes lactic acid during vigorous exercise.
  • Fermentation yields only 2 ATP per glucose.
  • The respiratory pathway provides intermediates for other biosynthetic reactions (amphibolic).
  • Respiration is controlled by the energy needs of the cell.

Examples

Muscle cramp. Lactic acid from anaerobic respiration accumulates during intense exercise, causing cramps.

Bread rising. Yeast ferments sugars and releases CO2 bubbles, which make dough rise.

Practice

  1. Q. Anaerobic respiration in muscles produces: Ans. Lactic acid. Lactic acid forms when oxygen is limited.
  2. Q. Fermentation yields how many ATP per glucose? Ans. 2. Fermentation yields only 2 ATP.
  3. Q. Why is the respiratory pathway called amphibolic? Ans. It both breaks down (catabolic) and supplies intermediates for synthesis (anabolic) of molecules.

15.1 Growth Hormones and Regulators

Plant growth is regulated by hormones that promote or inhibit growth and development.

Key points

  • Auxins stimulate cell elongation and control phototropism.
  • Gibberellins promote stem elongation, seed germination and flowering.
  • Cytokinins promote cell division and delay senescence.
  • Abscisic acid inhibits growth and closes stomata under stress.
  • Ethylene promotes ripening and abscission.

Examples

Auxin in phototropism. Auxin accumulates on the shaded side and elongates those cells, bending the shoot toward light.

Ripening fruit. Ethylene released by ripe fruit accelerates ripening of nearby fruit; that is why one ripe banana ripens others in a bag.

Practice

  1. Q. Which hormone promotes ripening? Ans. Ethylene. Ethylene promotes fruit ripening.
  2. Q. Abscisic acid is mainly: Ans. Inhibitory. ABA inhibits growth and closes stomata under stress.
  3. Q. Explain phototropism in terms of auxin. Ans. Light causes auxin to accumulate on the shaded side, where it stimulates elongation, bending the shoot toward light.

15.2 Growth Stages and Seed Germination

Seed germination, growth, flowering and senescence are regulated by internal and external factors.

Key points

  • Imbibition is the first step of seed germination, absorbing water.
  • Germination needs water, oxygen and a suitable temperature.
  • Some seeds need light or stratification to germinate.
  • Growth curves show an initial slow phase, rapid growth and a plateau.
  • Senescence and abscission are controlled processes that recycle nutrients.

Examples

Dormancy. A dormant seed waits for favourable conditions; the seed coat may block water or oxygen, or inhibitors may block germination.

Growth curve. An annual crop grows slowly at first, rapidly in the middle season, then levels off as nutrients go into grain formation.

Practice

  1. Q. The first step of seed germination is: Ans. Imbibition. Seeds first absorb water and swell.
  2. Q. Seed dormancy helps the seed to: Ans. Survive unfavourable conditions. Dormancy delays germination until conditions are suitable.
  3. Q. Why do seeds need oxygen to germinate? Ans. Germination requires energy from aerobic respiration, which needs oxygen.

16.1 The Human Digestive System

Digestion breaks food into absorbable molecules. The alimentary canal and associated glands secrete enzymes that act at specific pH values.

Key points

  • The mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine and large intestine form the alimentary canal.
  • Saliva contains amylase, which begins starch digestion.
  • The stomach secretes HCl and pepsin, digesting proteins in an acidic medium.
  • The small intestine receives bile and pancreatic juice and completes digestion; villi absorb nutrients.
  • The large intestine absorbs water and electrolytes and forms faeces.

Examples

Why the stomach does not digest itself. A thick mucus layer and rapid cell replacement protect the stomach lining from acid and pepsin.

Bile and fat digestion. Bile salts emulsify large fat droplets into smaller ones, increasing the surface area for lipase.

Practice

  1. Q. Protein digestion begins in the: Ans. Stomach. Pepsin in the stomach starts protein digestion.
  2. Q. Bile is produced by the: Ans. Liver. The liver produces bile, stored in the gall bladder.
  3. Q. Why is the small intestine the main site of absorption? Ans. Its long length and millions of villi and microvilli give an enormous surface area.

16.2 Absorption and Digestive Disorders

Nutrients are absorbed in different regions of the gut. Disorders can be caused by infection, diet or organ dysfunction.

Key points

  • Glucose and amino acids are absorbed in the small intestine by active transport.
  • Fatty acids and glycerol are absorbed as chylomicrons into lymph.
  • Vitamin B12 absorption needs intrinsic factor from the stomach.
  • Jaundice is associated with liver dysfunction and bilirubin accumulation.
  • Vomiting and diarrhoea cause dehydration and loss of electrolytes.

Examples

Why rapid fluid replacement is needed in diarrhoea. Diarrhoea causes severe water and electrolyte loss, so ORS restores fluids and salts.

Fat-soluble vitamins. Vitamins A, D, E and K need bile for absorption because they are fat-soluble.

Practice

  1. Q. Which vitamin needs intrinsic factor for absorption? Ans. Vitamin B12. Intrinsic factor from the stomach is needed for B12 uptake.
  2. Q. Most water is absorbed in the: Ans. Large intestine. The large intestine absorbs most remaining water.
  3. Q. Why is jaundice linked to liver problems? Ans. The liver processes bilirubin; when it cannot, bilirubin accumulates and yellows the skin and eyes.

17.1 Respiratory Organs and Gas Exchange

Gas exchange surfaces are thin, moist and richly supplied with blood. In humans, alveoli exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide.

Key points

  • Insects use tracheae; fish use gills; humans use lungs with alveoli.
  • Alveoli have a large surface area and thin walls for diffusion.
  • Breathing involves inspiration by the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.
  • Oxygen diffuses into blood and binds haemoglobin; CO2 is transported mainly as bicarbonate.
  • Vital capacity is the maximum volume exhaled after a maximum inhalation.

Examples

Why lungs are spongy. Millions of alveoli give a total surface area of about 70 m2, enough for efficient gas exchange.

CO2 transport. About 70% of CO2 is carried as bicarbonate ions, which also help maintain blood pH.

Practice

  1. Q. Gas exchange in humans occurs in the: Ans. Alveoli. Alveoli are the functional units of exchange.
  2. Q. Most CO2 is transported in blood as: Ans. Bicarbonate. About 70% travels as bicarbonate.
  3. Q. Why must respiratory surfaces be thin and moist? Ans. Thin walls and moisture allow fast diffusion of gases and protect the delicate surface.

17.2 Lung Volumes and Disorders

Respiratory volumes describe lung function. Disorders reduce gas exchange and require care.

Key points

  • Tidal volume is the air moved in one normal breath.
  • Functional residual capacity is the air left after normal expiration.
  • Asthma narrows airways and makes breathing difficult.
  • Emphysema destroys alveolar walls and reduces surface area.
  • Smoking damages cilia and alveoli and increases risk of lung disease.

Examples

Asthma attack. Smooth muscle constricts the bronchioles and mucus increases, so airways narrow and wheezing occurs.

Effect of altitude. Lower oxygen pressure stimulates faster breathing and red blood cell production.

Practice

  1. Q. Which disorder destroys alveolar walls? Ans. Emphysema. Emphysema reduces the surface for gas exchange.
  2. Q. The volume of air breathed in normal rest is called: Ans. Tidal volume. Tidal volume is the resting breath volume.
  3. Q. Why is smoking harmful to the respiratory system? Ans. It damages cilia and alveoli, reduces surface area and increases the risk of cancer and chronic disease.

18.1 Blood and Its Components

Blood transports substances and defends the body. It contains plasma and formed elements.

Key points

  • Plasma is about 55% of blood and carries proteins, nutrients, hormones and wastes.
  • Red blood cells contain haemoglobin and transport oxygen.
  • White blood cells defend the body against infection.
  • Platelets help in blood clotting.
  • The average adult has about 5 L of blood.

Examples

Why blood is red. Haemoglobin containing iron binds oxygen, giving blood its red colour.

Blood clotting. Platelets and clotting factors convert fibrinogen into a fibrin mesh that seals the wound.

Practice

  1. Q. Which blood cells transport oxygen? Ans. Red blood cells. Haemoglobin in RBCs carries oxygen.
  2. Q. The percentage of plasma in blood is about: Ans. 55%. Plasma makes up about 55% of blood volume.
  3. Q. What is the function of platelets? Ans. They help form clots and stop bleeding at injury sites.

18.2 Heart, Cardiac Cycle and Circulatory Disorders

The heart pumps blood through pulmonary and systemic circuits. Its rhythm and output are measurable.

Key points

  • The heart has four chambers: two atria and two ventricles.
  • The cardiac cycle is one heartbeat with systole and diastole phases.
  • Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume.
  • Blood pressure is measured as systolic/diastolic.
  • Hypertension, atherosclerosis and heart attack are common disorders.

Examples

Double circulation. Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit — once to the lungs and once to the body — which improves oxygenation efficiency.

Electrocardiogram. The ECG records electrical activity of the heart and helps detect arrhythmias and heart damage.

Practice

  1. Q. The normal resting heart rate of an adult is about: Ans. 72 beats per minute. Typical resting rate is around 70–75 bpm.
  2. Q. Double circulation means blood passes through the heart: Ans. Twice per complete circuit. Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit.
  3. Q. What is cardiac output? Ans. The volume of blood pumped by the heart per minute, equal to heart rate × stroke volume.

19.1 The Kidney and Urine Formation

Kidneys filter blood and form urine. The nephron is the functional unit, and the process has three stages.

Key points

  • The nephron has a glomerulus, Bowman's capsule, tubules and collecting duct.
  • Ultrafiltration occurs in the glomerulus under pressure.
  • Reabsorption returns water, glucose and ions from the tubules to blood.
  • Tubular secretion moves wastes from blood into the filtrate.
  • The counter-current mechanism in the loop of Henle conserves water.

Examples

Why urine is concentrated. The loop of Henle builds a salt gradient that allows water reabsorption, concentrating the urine and conserving water.

Glucose generally absent from urine. Glucose is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule; its presence in urine may indicate diabetes.

Practice

  1. Q. The functional unit of the kidney is the: Ans. Nephron. Each kidney contains about a million nephrons.
  2. Q. Ultrafiltration occurs in the: Ans. Glomerulus. Pressure forces filtrate into Bowman's capsule.
  3. Q. Name the three processes of urine formation. Ans. Ultrafiltration, selective reabsorption and tubular secretion.

19.2 Osmoregulation and Excretory Disorders

The body regulates water and solute balance through the kidneys and hormones. Dialysis replaces kidney function in failure.

Key points

  • Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) increases water reabsorption.
  • Aldosterone regulates sodium reabsorption.
  • Renal failure requires dialysis or a transplant.
  • Kidney stones are crystallised mineral deposits in the urinary tract.
  • Ammonia, urea and uric acid are nitrogenous wastes; their form depends on water availability.

Examples

Why we urinate less when dehydrated. ADH release increases water reabsorption, producing small amounts of concentrated urine.

Dialysis. A dialysis machine removes nitrogenous wastes from blood when kidneys have failed.

Practice

  1. Q. ADH increases: Ans. Water reabsorption. ADH makes collecting ducts more permeable to water.
  2. Q. A person whose kidneys fail may need: Ans. Dialysis. Dialysis filters waste from blood.
  3. Q. Why do desert animals excrete uric acid? Ans. Uric acid is almost insoluble and requires little water to excrete, conserving water.

20.1 Muscles and the Sliding Filament Model

Muscles contract to produce movement. Skeletal muscle consists of bundles of fibres containing actin and myosin filaments.

Key points

  • A muscle fibre contains myofibrils with sarcomeres.
  • In contraction, actin filaments slide over myosin, shortening the sarcomere.
  • Calcium ions trigger binding of actin and myosin.
  • ATP powers myosin heads and is needed for relaxation too.
  • Red muscle fibres are slow and fatigue-resistant; white fibres are fast and tire quickly.

Examples

Why muscles need calcium. Calcium binds troponin and exposes myosin-binding sites on actin, allowing cross-bridge cycling.

Rigor mortis. After death, ATP is depleted so myosin cross-bridges cannot detach, making muscles stiff.

Practice

  1. Q. Muscle contraction occurs by: Ans. Sliding of actin over myosin. The sliding filament model explains contraction.
  2. Q. Which ion triggers contraction? Ans. Calcium. Calcium release triggers cross-bridge cycling.
  3. Q. Why is ATP needed for muscle relaxation? Ans. ATP is needed to pump calcium back and detach myosin heads so the muscle can relax.

20.2 Skeletal System and Joints

The human skeleton supports the body, protects organs and allows movement through joints.

Key points

  • The adult skeleton has 206 bones.
  • The axial skeleton includes skull, vertebral column and rib cage.
  • The appendicular skeleton includes limbs and girdles.
  • Joints may be fibrous, cartilaginous or synovial.
  • Osteoporosis and arthritis affect bones and joints.

Examples

Why the skull has sutures. Immovable fibrous joints between skull bones lock them tightly to protect the brain.

Knee joint. A synovial hinge joint with cartilage and fluid allows smooth bending and straightening.

Practice

  1. Q. The number of bones in an adult human is: Ans. 206. Adults have 206 bones.
  2. Q. The knee is a: Ans. Synovial hinge joint. The knee allows motion in one plane.
  3. Q. Differentiate the axial and appendicular skeleton. Ans. The axial skeleton forms the central axis (skull, spine, ribs); the appendicular skeleton forms the limbs and girdles.

21.1 Neurons and Nerve Impulses

Neurons transmit electrical impulses. The resting potential and action potential depend on ion movement across the membrane.

Key points

  • A neuron has dendrites, a cell body and an axon.
  • The resting membrane potential is about −70 mV.
  • Depolarisation opens sodium channels, generating an action potential.
  • Repolarisation restores the potential by potassium movement.
  • Impulses jump between nodes of Ranvier in myelinated axons, speeding conduction.

Examples

Why myelinated axons conduct faster. Myelin insulates the axon except at nodes, so the impulse jumps from node to node — saltatory conduction.

Local anaesthetics. They block sodium channels, preventing action potentials and therefore pain signals.

Practice

  1. Q. The resting potential of a neuron is about: Ans. −70 mV. The inside is negative relative to the outside.
  2. Q. Depolarisation is caused by movement of: Ans. Sodium ions inward. Sodium influx raises the membrane potential.
  3. Q. Why are impulses faster in myelinated neurons? Ans. Conduction is saltatory, jumping between nodes of Ranvier rather than travelling continuously.

21.2 Synapses, Brain and Reflexes

Signals cross synapses chemically. The brain and spinal cord coordinate complex and reflex actions.

Key points

  • Synaptic transmission uses neurotransmitters released by calcium-triggered exocytosis.
  • Excitatory synapses depolarise the postsynaptic cell; inhibitory ones hyperpolarise it.
  • The cerebral cortex handles higher functions; the cerebellum coordinates movement.
  • The medulla controls breathing and heartbeat.
  • Reflex arcs provide rapid responses to stimuli.

Examples

Reflex arc. A hammer tap on the knee stretches a tendon, triggering a sensory neuron that synapses in the spinal cord and excites a motor neuron — the knee jerks.

Neurotransmitter balance. Dopamine and serotonin influence mood and movement; imbalances are linked to Parkinson's disease and depression.

Practice

  1. Q. Neurotransmitters are released from the: Ans. Axon terminal. Vesicles in the axon terminal release neurotransmitters.
  2. Q. Which brain region controls balance? Ans. Cerebellum. The cerebellum coordinates posture and balance.
  3. Q. What is a reflex arc? Ans. The neural pathway that mediates a reflex action, from receptor to effector.

22.1 Endocrine Glands and Hormones

Hormones are chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands into the blood, regulating growth, metabolism and reproduction.

Key points

  • The pituitary gland is the master gland, controlling other endocrine glands.
  • The thyroid gland produces thyroxine, regulating metabolism.
  • The pancreas secretes insulin and glucagon to control blood glucose.
  • The adrenal glands produce adrenaline and corticosteroids.
  • The gonads produce sex hormones.

Examples

Goitre. Iodine deficiency reduces thyroxine production; the thyroid enlarges as it tries to compensate.

Insulin and glucose. After a meal, insulin helps cells absorb glucose; in diabetes this fails, so blood glucose remains high.

Practice

  1. Q. The master endocrine gland is the: Ans. Pituitary. The pituitary controls other endocrine glands.
  2. Q. Insulin is secreted by the: Ans. Pancreas. Beta cells of the pancreas secrete insulin.
  3. Q. Why is iodine necessary in the diet? Ans. It is needed to synthesise thyroid hormones; deficiency causes goitre and hypothyroidism.

22.2 Mechanism of Hormone Action

Hormones act on target cells through receptors. Water-soluble hormones use surface receptors; lipid-soluble hormones enter cells.

Key points

  • Protein hormones bind surface receptors and trigger second messengers such as cAMP.
  • Steroid hormones enter cells and bind intracellular receptors, altering gene expression.
  • Hormone levels are regulated by feedback loops.
  • The hypothalamus links the nervous and endocrine systems.
  • Hormonal imbalance causes diseases such as diabetes and hypothyroidism.

Examples

Negative feedback. High thyroxine levels inhibit TSH release from the pituitary, keeping thyroid activity in check.

Steroid hormone action. Cortisol enters cells and binds receptors that act as transcription factors, changing gene expression slowly but durably.

Practice

  1. Q. Steroid hormones act by: Ans. Binding intracellular receptors. They enter cells and affect gene expression.
  2. Q. A negative feedback loop: Ans. Reduces the stimulus that started it. The response counteracts the initial stimulus.
  3. Q. Why does hormone action take longer than nerve action? Ans. Hormones travel in blood, bind receptors and often alter gene expression, which takes time.
