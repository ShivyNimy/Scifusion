## Chapter 1. Matter in Our Surroundings

### 1.1 States of Matter and Interconversion

Matter exists as solid, liquid or gas depending on the arrangement and energy of particles. Heating and cooling convert one state into another.

**Key points**

- Particles of matter have spaces between them and are in constant motion; their kinetic energy increases with temperature.
- Melting (solid -> liquid), boiling/vaporisation (liquid -> gas), condensation (gas -> liquid) and freezing (liquid -> solid) are interconversions.
- Sublimation is a direct solid-to-gas change (camphor, naphthalene, dry ice).
- Latent heat of fusion is the heat needed to change 1 kg of solid to liquid at its melting point.
- Evaporation is a surface phenomenon; it increases with temperature, surface area, wind speed and decreases with humidity.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why we feel cool after sweating.** Sweat absorbs latent heat from the skin as it evaporates, so the body cools. On a humid day evaporation is slow, so we feel sticky and hotter.

2. **Water at 0 °C versus ice at 0 °C.** Ice at 0 °C absorbs latent heat to melt, so it cools a drink more effectively than the same mass of water at 0 °C.

**Practice questions**

1. Which state has the highest kinetic energy of particles? (A) Gas (B) Liquid (C) Solid (D) All are equal
   - *Answer:* Gas. Gas particles move fastest and have the most kinetic energy.
2. The heat required to change 1 kg of ice at 0 °C into water at 0 °C is called: (A) Latent heat of fusion (B) Specific heat (C) Latent heat of vaporisation (D) Thermal energy
   - *Answer:* Latent heat of fusion. Latent heat of fusion melts the ice without changing temperature.
3. Why does evaporation cause cooling?
   - *Answer:* The fastest particles escape as vapour, taking away energy and lowering the average kinetic energy (temperature) of what remains.

### 1.2 The Gaseous State and the SI Units

Gases are highly compressible and diffuse rapidly. They exert pressure and have measurable properties related to temperature, volume and amount.

**Key points**

- Air is a mixture of gases; it can be squeezed into a small cylinder because of large inter-particle spaces.
- Gas pressure is due to collisions of particles with container walls.
- The SI unit of temperature is the kelvin; 0 °C = 273.15 K.
- The SI unit of pressure is the pascal (Pa).
- Diffusion of gases is fast because their particles move quickly and randomly.

**Worked examples**

1. **LPG in a cylinder.** LPG is filled as a liquid under high pressure. On release it vaporises, expanding hugely, which is why a small cylinder supplies a lot of gas.

2. **Smell spreading through a room.** The smelly particles move randomly and collide with air molecules, spreading through the room by diffusion.

**Practice questions**

1. 0 °C equals: (A) 273.15 K (B) 100 K (C) -100 K (D) 373 K
   - *Answer:* 273.15 K. Kelvin temperature = Celsius + 273.15.
2. Gases are highly compressible because: (A) There is a lot of space between particles (B) Particles are heavy (C) Particles are solid (D) Particles are charged
   - *Answer:* There is a lot of space between particles. Large inter-particle spaces allow compression.
3. Convert 27 °C to kelvin.
   - *Answer:* 27 + 273.15 = 300.15 K.

## Chapter 2. Is Matter Around Us Pure?

### 2.1 Mixtures, Solutions and Colloids

Matter can be classified as pure substances (elements and compounds) or mixtures (homogeneous and heterogeneous).

**Key points**

- A homogeneous mixture has a uniform composition throughout, for example salt solution.
- A heterogeneous mixture has visible different parts, for example sand and iron filings.
- A colloid has particles dispersed in a medium that do not settle, such as milk or fog.
- A suspension has particles that settle on standing, such as muddy water.
- The Tyndall effect: colloids scatter a beam of light, solutions do not.

**Worked examples**

1. **Classifying milk.** Milk looks uniform but its fat droplets are dispersed in water and scatter light; it is a colloid. When it sours, protein clumps and settles, forming a suspension.

2. **Separating salt and sand.** Dissolve the mixture, filter off the sand, then evaporate the filtrate to recover salt. The method combines two techniques based on solubility.

**Practice questions**

1. Which is a homogeneous mixture? (A) Salt solution (B) Sand and iron (C) Muddy water (D) Oil and water
   - *Answer:* Salt solution. Salt dissolves to give a uniform composition.
2. Which mixture shows the Tyndall effect? (A) Colloid (B) True solution (C) Element (D) Compound
   - *Answer:* Colloid. Colloidal particles scatter light.
3. Distinguish between a solution and a suspension.
   - *Answer:* In a solution the solute is dissolved and does not settle; a suspension has larger particles that settle on standing.

### 2.2 Elements, Compounds and Separation Techniques

An element contains one kind of atom; a compound contains two or more elements chemically combined. Pure substances have fixed composition.

**Key points**

- Elements are listed in the periodic table and cannot be split into simpler substances by chemical means.
- Compounds have properties different from their elements (water is unlike hydrogen or oxygen).
- Separation techniques: filtration, evaporation, distillation, fractional distillation, sublimation and magnetism.
- Chromatography separates dissolved substances on the basis of their movement through a medium.
- Centrifugation spins a mixture to separate components of different density.

**Worked examples**

1. **Separating a mixture of salt, sand and iron.** Use a magnet to remove iron, add water and filter to remove sand, then evaporate the filtrate to recover salt. Three techniques, three components.

2. **Fractional distillation of crude oil.** Components of crude oil have different boiling points and condense at different heights in a fractionating column, giving petrol, kerosene, diesel and more.

**Practice questions**

1. Which is a compound? (A) Water (B) Oxygen (C) Iron (D) Argon
   - *Answer:* Water. Water contains hydrogen and oxygen chemically combined.
2. A magnet can separate: (A) Iron from sulphur (B) Salt from water (C) Alcohol from water (D) Sand from water
   - *Answer:* Iron from sulphur. Iron is magnetic and can be pulled out.
3. How would you separate a mixture of alcohol and water?
   - *Answer:* By fractional distillation, because the two liquids have different boiling points.

## Chapter 3. Atoms and Molecules

### 3.1 Laws of Chemical Combination and Atomic Mass

Chemical reactions follow the laws of conservation of mass and constant proportions. Atoms are the smallest particles of elements; molecules are groups of atoms bonded together.

**Key points**

- Law of conservation of mass: mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
- Law of constant proportions: a compound always contains its elements in a fixed mass ratio.
- Atomic mass is measured relative to carbon-12.
- 1 atomic mass unit (u) is one-twelfth the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
- The mole is the amount of substance containing Avogadro's number (6.022 × 10<sup>23</sup>) of particles.

**Worked examples**

1. **Conservation of mass.** When 1.2 g of carbon burns in oxygen, it forms 4.4 g of carbon dioxide. The oxygen used is 4.4 − 1.2 = 3.2 g, so total mass is conserved.

2. **Molar mass of water.** 2 × 1 + 16 = 18 g/mol, so 18 g of water contains 6.022 × 10<sup>23</sup> molecules.

**Practice questions**

1. Avogadro's number is: (A) 6.022 × 10<sup>23</sup> (B) 3.14 × 10<sup>8</sup> (C) 9.8 × 10<sup>2</sup> (D) 1.6 × 10<sup>-19</sup>
   - *Answer:* 6.022 × 10<sup>23</sup>. One mole of any substance contains 6.022 × 10<sup>23</sup> particles.
2. The law of conservation of mass was proposed by: (A) Lavoisier (B) Dalton (C) Newton (D) Einstein
   - *Answer:* Lavoisier. Antoine Lavoisier established the law of conservation of mass.
3. Calculate the molar mass of CO<sub>2</sub>.
   - *Answer:* C = 12, O = 16, so 12 + 2 × 16 = 44 g/mol.

### 3.2 Writing Chemical Formulas and Mole Calculations

Chemical formulas show the elements and their ratio. The mole connects mass, number of particles and volume of a gas.

**Key points**

- A molecule of a compound is shown by its formula, such as H<sub>2</sub>O.
- Molecular mass is the sum of atomic masses of the atoms in a formula.
- Number of moles = mass ÷ molar mass.
- Molar volume of a gas at STP is 22.4 L.
- Percentage composition = (mass of element ÷ molar mass) × 100.

**Worked examples**

1. **Moles in 5.6 g of iron.** Atomic mass of Fe is 56, so moles = 5.6 ÷ 56 = 0.1 mol, which is 6.022 × 10<sup>22</sup> atoms.

2. **Mass of one molecule of water.** Molar mass 18 g contains 6.022 × 10<sup>23</sup> molecules, so one molecule has mass ≈ 3 × 10<sup>-23</sup> g.

**Practice questions**

1. Number of moles in 36 g of water (molar mass 18) is: (A) 2 (B) 1 (C) 18 (D) 36
   - *Answer:* 2. Moles = 36 ÷ 18 = 2.
2. Volume of 1 mole of an ideal gas at STP is: (A) 22.4 L (B) 11.2 L (C) 1 L (D) 44.8 L
   - *Answer:* 22.4 L. One mole occupies 22.4 L at STP.
3. Calculate the percentage of oxygen in water.
   - *Answer:* Oxygen mass = 16 out of 18, so (16/18) × 100 ≈ 88.9%.

## Chapter 4. Structure of the Atom

### 4.1 Subatomic Particles and Atomic Models

Atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons. Successive models — Thomson, Rutherford and Bohr — refined our picture of atomic structure.

**Key points**

- Protons are positive, electrons negative and neutrons neutral.
- Atomic number = number of protons; mass number = protons + neutrons.
- Rutherford's gold foil experiment showed that most of the atom is empty space with a tiny dense nucleus.
- Bohr proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed energy shells.
- Isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

**Worked examples**

1. **Reading the symbol of sodium.** Sodium has atomic number 11 and mass number 23, so it has 11 protons, 11 electrons and 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons.

2. **Isotopes of hydrogen.** Protium (1 proton, 0 neutrons), deuterium (1 proton, 1 neutron) and tritium (1 proton, 2 neutrons) are isotopes with the same chemistry but different masses.

**Practice questions**

1. The mass number of an atom is: (A) Protons + neutrons (B) Protons only (C) Neutrons only (D) Electrons only
   - *Answer:* Protons + neutrons. Mass number counts protons plus neutrons.
2. Which experiment led to the discovery of the nucleus? (A) Rutherford's gold foil experiment (B) Thomson's cathode ray experiment (C) Millikan's oil drop (D) Bohr's model
   - *Answer:* Rutherford's gold foil experiment. Alpha particles scattering showed a small dense nucleus.
3. What are isotopes? Give one example.
   - *Answer:* Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons; for example chlorine-35 and chlorine-37.

### 4.2 Electronic Configuration and Valency

Electrons fill shells in a fixed order (2, 8, 8 …). The number of electrons in the outermost shell determines chemical behaviour and valency.

**Key points**

- The maximum number of electrons in a shell is 2n<sup>2</sup>.
- Shells are filled from the inside out (K, L, M, N).
- Atoms gain, lose or share electrons to achieve a stable outer shell (octet).
- Valency is the combining capacity of an element.
- Ions form when atoms lose or gain electrons.

**Worked examples**

1. **Configuration of magnesium.** Magnesium has 12 electrons: 2 in K, 8 in L and 2 in M. Losing 2 electrons gives a stable configuration, so its valency is +2.

2. **Sodium chloride.** Sodium (2, 8, 1) loses one electron to become Na<sup>+</sup>; chlorine (2, 8, 7) gains one to become Cl<sup>−</sup>; the ions attract and form NaCl.

**Practice questions**

1. The maximum number of electrons in the L shell is: (A) 8 (B) 2 (C) 18 (D) 32
   - *Answer:* 8. The L shell holds up to 8 electrons.
2. Valency is: (A) The combining capacity of an element (B) Number of neutrons (C) Atomic mass (D) Number of shells
   - *Answer:* The combining capacity of an element. Valency reflects how many electrons an atom can gain, lose or share.
3. Write the electronic configuration of chlorine and state its valency.
   - *Answer:* 2, 8, 7; valency 1 (it gains one electron).

## Chapter 5. The Fundamental Unit of Life

### 5.1 Cell Structure and Function

The cell is the basic unit of life. Prokaryotic cells lack a nucleus; eukaryotic cells have membrane-bound organelles.

**Key points**

- Plasma membrane is selectively permeable and controls transport.
- Nucleus contains DNA, the genetic material, and controls cell activities.
- Mitochondria are the powerhouse and make ATP through respiration.
- Ribosomes synthesise proteins; the ER and Golgi complex process and transport materials.
- Lysosomes digest waste and worn-out organelles; plastids in plants carry pigments.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why muscle cells have many mitochondria.** Muscle contraction needs large amounts of ATP, so muscle cells contain many mitochondria to produce it.

2. **Plant versus animal cell.** Plant cells have a rigid cell wall, chloroplasts and a large vacuole; animal cells lack a wall and chloroplasts and have small scattered vacuoles.

**Practice questions**

1. Which organelle is called the powerhouse of the cell? (A) Mitochondrion (B) Nucleus (C) Ribosome (D) Golgi body
   - *Answer:* Mitochondrion. Mitochondria release energy as ATP.
2. Prokaryotic cells lack: (A) A membrane-bound nucleus (B) A cell membrane (C) Ribosomes (D) Cytoplasm
   - *Answer:* A membrane-bound nucleus. Their DNA is not enclosed in a nucleus.
3. Why is the plasma membrane called selectively permeable?
   - *Answer:* It allows only certain substances to pass in or out of the cell.

### 5.2 Cell Division and the Cell Theory

The cell theory states that all living things are made of cells and new cells arise from existing cells. Cells divide for growth, repair and reproduction.

**Key points**

- Schleiden and Schwann proposed the cell theory; Virchow added that cells arise from pre-existing cells.
- Mitosis produces two identical daughter cells for growth and repair.
- Meiosis produces four gametes with half the chromosome number.
- Cell division is controlled; uncontrolled division can lead to cancer.
- Growth in multicellular organisms happens by increase in cell number and size.

**Worked examples**

1. **Wound healing.** Skin cells at the edge of a cut divide by mitosis to fill the gap and restore the barrier.

2. **Gamete formation.** Meiosis halves the chromosome number so that fertilisation restores the normal diploid number in the offspring.

**Practice questions**

1. Cells that are genetically identical to the parent are produced by: (A) Mitosis (B) Meiosis (C) Fertilisation (D) Budding
   - *Answer:* Mitosis. Mitosis produces identical daughter cells.
2. Meiosis produces: (A) Four gametes (B) Two identical cells (C) One cell (D) Eight cells
   - *Answer:* Four gametes. Meiosis gives four haploid gametes.
3. State the cell theory.
   - *Answer:* All organisms are made of cells; the cell is the basic unit of life; all cells arise from pre-existing cells.

## Chapter 6. Tissues

### 6.1 Plant Tissues

Plants have meristematic tissues for growth and permanent tissues for support, transport and photosynthesis.

**Key points**

- Meristematic tissue is found at growing tips (apical), in stems (lateral/cambium) and at the base of leaves.
- Parenchyma has thin walls and stores food; collenchyma provides flexibility; sclerenchyma is dead and rigid.
- Xylem carries water and minerals upward; phloem carries food in both directions.
- Epidermis protects and forms root hairs; cork is a dead protective outer layer.
- Simple tissues have one cell type; complex tissues (xylem, phloem) have several.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why a tall tree can stand.** Sclerenchyma fibres and lignified xylem walls give strength and rigidity, while xylem vessels carry water up the trunk.

2. **Wood and bark.** Wood is largely secondary xylem; bark is cork and phloem at the outside, protecting the tree and moving food.

**Practice questions**

1. Which tissue transports food in plants? (A) Phloem (B) Xylem (C) Parenchyma (D) Sclerenchyma
   - *Answer:* Phloem. Phloem carries sugars from leaves to other parts.
2. Meristematic tissue is responsible for: (A) Growth (B) Transport (C) Protection (D) Storage
   - *Answer:* Growth. It contains actively dividing cells.
3. Differentiate xylem and phloem.
   - *Answer:* Xylem carries water and minerals upward and is mostly dead; phloem carries food in both directions and is living.

### 6.2 Animal Tissues

Animals have four main tissue types: epithelial, connective, muscular and nervous.

**Key points**

- Epithelial tissue covers and protects surfaces and may absorb or secrete.
- Connective tissue includes bone, cartilage, blood and adipose tissue.
- Muscular tissue is of three types: skeletal (voluntary), smooth (involuntary) and cardiac (involuntary, striated).
- Nervous tissue is made of neurons that conduct impulses.
- Blood is a fluid connective tissue with plasma, RBCs, WBCs and platelets.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why cardiac muscle never tires.** Cardiac muscle cells are striated but involuntary and have many mitochondria and a rich blood supply, letting them contract continuously without fatigue.

2. **Bone and cartilage.** Bone is hard and rigid due to calcium salts and supports the body; cartilage is flexible and found in the nose and ears.

**Practice questions**

1. Which tissue connects muscle to bone? (A) Tendon (B) Ligament (C) Cartilage (D) Blood
   - *Answer:* Tendon. Tendons attach muscle to bone; ligaments join bone to bone.
2. Voluntary muscles are found in the: (A) Arms and legs (B) Heart (C) Stomach (D) Intestine
   - *Answer:* Arms and legs. Skeletal muscles are under conscious control.
3. Name the four types of animal tissue.
   - *Answer:* Epithelial, connective, muscular and nervous tissue.

## Chapter 7. Diversity in Living Organisms

### 7.1 Classification and the Five Kingdoms

Biodiversity is enormous, so organisms are classified into groups based on shared features. Whittaker proposed five kingdoms.

**Key points**

- Monera (bacteria), Protista (unicellular eukaryotes), Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.
- Classification uses cell type, body organisation, mode of nutrition and reproduction.
- Binomial nomenclature gives each species a genus and species name in Latin, e.g. Homo sapiens.
- Hierarchy: kingdom, phylum (division), class, order, family, genus, species.
- Species is the basic unit of classification.

**Worked examples**

1. **Where does a mushroom belong?.** Mushrooms are fungi: eukaryotic, heterotrophic with cell walls of chitin, and they absorb nutrients from dead matter.

2. **Naming a mango.** Mangifera indica — Mangifera is the genus and indica the species, written in italics with the genus capitalised.

**Practice questions**

1. Which kingdom includes bacteria? (A) Monera (B) Protista (C) Fungi (D) Plantae
   - *Answer:* Monera. Bacteria are prokaryotes in kingdom Monera.
2. Binomial nomenclature uses: (A) Genus and species (B) Kingdom and phylum (C) Class and order (D) Family and genus
   - *Answer:* Genus and species. The scientific name has two parts: genus and species.
3. Write the hierarchy of classification from kingdom to species.
   - *Answer:* Kingdom -> phylum -> class -> order -> family -> genus -> species.

### 7.2 Plant and Animal Groups

Plants are grouped into thallophytes, bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms and angiosperms. Animals are grouped into invertebrates and vertebrates.

**Key points**

- Thallophytes (algae) have no true roots, stems or leaves.
- Bryophytes are amphibians of the plant kingdom and need water for reproduction.
- Pteridophytes have vascular tissue and reproduce by spores.
- Gymnosperms have naked seeds (pine, cycas); angiosperms have flowers and enclosed seeds.
- Vertebrates: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why ferns need moist places.** Ferns (pteridophytes) release sperm that swim through water to reach the egg, so they reproduce only in damp conditions.

2. **Mammals versus birds.** Mammals have hair, mammary glands and give birth to young (mostly); birds have feathers, beaks and lay eggs.

**Practice questions**

1. Which group has naked seeds? (A) Gymnosperms (B) Angiosperms (C) Pteridophytes (D) Bryophytes
   - *Answer:* Gymnosperms. Gymnosperm seeds are not enclosed in a fruit.
2. Which vertebrate group has moist skin and lays eggs in water? (A) Amphibians (B) Reptiles (C) Birds (D) Mammals
   - *Answer:* Amphibians. Amphibians such as frogs depend on water for reproduction.
3. Name the five vertebrate groups.
   - *Answer:* Fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

## Chapter 8. Motion

### 8.1 Describing Motion

Motion is described by displacement, velocity and acceleration. These quantities allow precise predictions of speed and position.

**Key points**

- Distance is the total path length; displacement is the shortest straight-line change in position.
- Speed = total distance ÷ time; velocity = displacement ÷ time.
- Velocity has direction, so it is a vector; speed is a scalar.
- Acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time.
- Uniform motion means constant velocity; non-uniform means changing velocity.

**Worked examples**

1. **A runner on a circular track.** One lap around a 400 m track covers 400 m of distance but zero displacement, because the runner returns to the start.

2. **Car acceleration.** A car goes from 0 to 20 m/s in 5 s: acceleration = (20 − 0) ÷ 5 = 4 m/s<sup>2</sup>.

**Practice questions**

1. Displacement can be: (A) Zero while distance is not zero (B) Always equal to distance (C) Negative only (D) A scalar
   - *Answer:* Zero while distance is not zero. On a closed loop displacement is zero, though distance is not.
2. The SI unit of acceleration is: (A) m/s<sup>2</sup> (B) m/s (C) m (D) s
   - *Answer:* m/s<sup>2</sup>. Acceleration is velocity change per unit time.
3. A cyclist covers 20 km in 1 hour. Find the average speed.
   - *Answer:* Average speed = total distance ÷ total time = 20 ÷ 1 = 20 km/h.

### 8.2 Equations of Motion and Graphs

For uniformly accelerated motion, three equations link velocity, displacement, acceleration and time.

**Key points**

- v = u + at
- s = ut + ½at<sup>2</sup>
- v<sup>2</sup> = u<sup>2</sup> + 2as
- The area under a velocity-time graph gives displacement; the slope gives acceleration.
- In a distance-time graph, the slope gives speed.

**Worked examples**

1. **A stone dropped from rest.** With u = 0 and a = 9.8 m/s<sup>2</sup>, after 2 s the velocity is v = 0 + 9.8 × 2 = 19.6 m/s, and distance s = ½ × 9.8 × 4 = 19.6 m.

2. **Braking distance.** With u = 20 m/s, v = 0 and a = −4 m/s<sup>2</sup>: v<sup>2</sup> = u<sup>2</sup> + 2as gives 0 = 400 − 8s, so s = 50 m.

**Practice questions**

1. The area under a velocity-time graph gives: (A) Displacement (B) Acceleration (C) Force (D) Speed
   - *Answer:* Displacement. Area under v-t graph = displacement.
2. If v = u + at and u = 0, a = 2 m/s<sup>2</sup>, t = 3 s, then v is: (A) 6 m/s (B) 9 m/s (C) 3 m/s (D) 1.5 m/s
   - *Answer:* 6 m/s. v = 0 + 2 × 3 = 6 m/s.
3. A train accelerates from 10 m/s to 30 m/s in 10 s. Find the acceleration.
   - *Answer:* a = (30 − 10) ÷ 10 = 2 m/s<sup>2</sup>.

## Chapter 9. Force and Laws of Motion

### 9.1 Newton's Laws of Motion

Newton's three laws describe how forces change motion. Inertia, momentum and action-reaction are the key ideas.

**Key points**

- First law: an object stays at rest or in uniform motion unless acted on by an external force (law of inertia).
- Second law: F = ma; force equals mass times acceleration.
- Third law: every action has an equal and opposite reaction.
- Momentum p = mv; force is the rate of change of momentum.
- Inertia depends on mass: heavier objects are harder to start or stop.

**Worked examples**

1. **Seat belts and the first law.** When a car stops suddenly, passengers continue moving forward because of inertia. Seat belts provide the restraining force.

2. **Recoil of a gun.** The gun pushes the bullet forward and the bullet pushes the gun backwards with an equal force; the lighter bullet therefore has the greater acceleration.

**Practice questions**

1. Newton's second law is written as: (A) F = ma (B) F = m/a (C) F = a/m (D) F = mv
   - *Answer:* F = ma. Force equals mass times acceleration.
2. A body at rest stays at rest unless: (A) An external force acts (B) It gets hot (C) Its mass changes (D) Time passes
   - *Answer:* An external force acts. This is the law of inertia.
3. Calculate the force needed to accelerate a 5 kg mass at 3 m/s<sup>2</sup>.
   - *Answer:* F = ma = 5 × 3 = 15 N.

### 9.2 Momentum, Impulse and Conservation

Momentum is mass times velocity. In the absence of external forces, total momentum of a system is conserved.

**Key points**

- p = mv, with units kg·m/s.
- Impulse = force × time = change in momentum.
- Momentum is conserved in collisions and explosions.
- A longer contact time reduces the force for the same momentum change — the principle behind airbags.
- Recoil of a gun follows conservation of momentum.

**Worked examples**

1. **Airbag physics.** An airbag increases the time over which a passenger's momentum drops to zero, reducing the force on the body.

2. **A gun and bullet.** A 2 kg gun fires a 0.02 kg bullet at 400 m/s. Recoil velocity = (0.02 × 400) ÷ 2 = 4 m/s backwards, so total momentum stays zero.

**Practice questions**

1. The SI unit of momentum is: (A) kg·m/s (B) N (C) J (D) m/s
   - *Answer:* kg·m/s. Momentum is mass times velocity, kg·m/s.
2. Momentum is conserved when: (A) No external force acts (B) Gravity is strong (C) Friction is large (D) Temperature rises
   - *Answer:* No external force acts. Conservation holds for isolated systems.
3. Why are airbags safer than rigid dashboards?
   - *Answer:* They increase the collision time, reducing the force on the passenger.

## Chapter 10. Gravitation

### 10.1 Universal Law of Gravitation

Every object attracts every other object with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

**Key points**

- F = G m<sub>1</sub>m<sub>2</sub>/r<sup>2</sup>, where G = 6.674 × 10<sup>-11</sup> N·m<sup>2</sup>/kg<sup>2</sup>.
- Gravitational force is always attractive and acts along the line joining the centres.
- The force is weak unless one mass is enormous, which is why we notice gravity only for planets and stars.
- Weight is the gravitational force on an object: W = mg.
- Mass is constant; weight changes with the gravitational field.

**Worked examples**

1. **Weight on the Moon.** g on the Moon is about 1.6 m/s<sup>2</sup>. A 60 kg astronaut has the same mass but a weight of only about 96 N, versus 588 N on Earth.

2. **Doubling the distance.** If the distance between two masses doubles, the gravitational force becomes one-quarter of its original value, since F ∝ 1/r<sup>2</sup>.

**Practice questions**

1. The value of G is: (A) 6.674 × 10<sup>-11</sup> N·m<sup>2</sup>/kg<sup>2</sup> (B) 9.8 m/s<sup>2</sup> (C) 3 × 10<sup>8</sup> m/s (D) 1.6 × 10<sup>-19</sup> C
   - *Answer:* 6.674 × 10<sup>-11</sup> N·m<sup>2</sup>/kg<sup>2</sup>. G is the universal gravitational constant.
2. Weight of a 10 kg object on Earth (g = 9.8) is: (A) 98 N (B) 10 N (C) 9.8 N (D) 980 N
   - *Answer:* 98 N. W = mg = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N.
3. State the universal law of gravitation.
   - *Answer:* Every particle attracts every other with a force proportional to the product of masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance.

### 10.2 Free Fall, Motion of Planets and Satellites

Objects fall with acceleration g. Orbital motion is a balance between gravity and tangential velocity.

**Key points**

- Free fall acceleration near Earth's surface is g ≈ 9.8 m/s<sup>2</sup>.
- Equations of motion apply with a = g for vertical motion.
- Kepler's laws describe planetary orbits: elliptical paths and equal areas in equal times.
- A satellite stays in orbit because its tangential speed keeps it falling around Earth.
- Escape velocity from Earth is about 11.2 km/s.

**Worked examples**

1. **Time to fall.** A stone dropped from a 20 m tower takes t = √(2s/g) = √(40/9.8) ≈ 2.02 s to reach the ground.

2. **Why astronauts float.** The spacecraft and astronauts are in continuous free fall around Earth, so they experience weightlessness relative to the cabin.

**Practice questions**

1. g near Earth's surface is about: (A) 9.8 m/s<sup>2</sup> (B) 1.6 m/s<sup>2</sup> (C) 3 × 10<sup>8</sup> m/s (D) 6.674 × 10<sup>-11</sup>
   - *Answer:* 9.8 m/s<sup>2</sup>. Earth's surface gravity is about 9.8 m/s<sup>2</sup>.
2. Escape velocity from Earth is about: (A) 11.2 km/s (B) 9.8 km/s (C) 3 km/s (D) 100 km/s
   - *Answer:* 11.2 km/s. An object needs about 11.2 km/s to escape Earth's gravity.
3. Why does a satellite not fall to Earth?
   - *Answer:* Its high tangential velocity makes it fall around Earth rather than into it.

## Chapter 11. Work and Energy

### 11.1 Work, Energy and Power

Work is done when a force moves an object. Energy is the capacity to do work; power is the rate of doing work.

**Key points**

- Work W = F × s × cos θ; the SI unit is the joule (J).
- Kinetic energy KE = ½mv<sup>2</sup>; potential energy PE = mgh.
- Work-energy theorem: net work equals change in kinetic energy.
- Power P = W/t; the SI unit is the watt (W).
- 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10<sup>6</sup> J is the commercial unit of energy.

**Worked examples**

1. **Work done in lifting.** Lifting a 2 kg book by 1.5 m: W = mgh = 2 × 9.8 × 1.5 = 29.4 J.

2. **Kinetic energy of a car.** A 1000 kg car at 20 m/s has KE = ½ × 1000 × 400 = 200,000 J = 200 kJ.

**Practice questions**

1. The SI unit of work is the: (A) Joule (B) Watt (C) Newton (D) Pascal
   - *Answer:* Joule. Work and energy are measured in joules.
2. If speed doubles, kinetic energy becomes: (A) Four times (B) Twice (C) Half (D) Same
   - *Answer:* Four times. KE ∝ v<sup>2</sup>, so doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy.
3. A 10 kg object is lifted 2 m. Find the work done (g = 9.8 m/s<sup>2</sup>).
   - *Answer:* W = mgh = 10 × 9.8 × 2 = 196 J.

### 11.2 Conservation of Energy and Sources

Energy can change form but is never created or destroyed. Renewable and non-renewable sources differ in how long they last.

**Key points**

- Potential energy converts to kinetic energy as an object falls, and back again as it rises.
- Friction converts mechanical energy into heat, which is still energy but less useful.
- Renewable sources: solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, biomass.
- Non-renewable sources: coal, petroleum, natural gas, nuclear fuel.
- Energy efficiency means getting more useful work from the same energy input.

**Worked examples**

1. **Pendulum energy.** At the highest point of a swing the bob has maximum potential energy and zero speed; at the lowest point it has maximum kinetic energy and minimum potential energy.

2. **Hydroelectric power.** Water stored in a dam has potential energy; falling water spins turbines, converting it into electricity through a generator.

**Practice questions**

1. Energy is: (A) Conserved, only changed in form (B) Destroyed during work (C) Created by machines (D) Always kinetic
   - *Answer:* Conserved, only changed in form. Total energy stays constant, though its form changes.
2. Which is a renewable source? (A) Wind (B) Coal (C) Petroleum (D) Natural gas
   - *Answer:* Wind. Wind is replenished naturally.
3. Why is a machine never 100% efficient?
   - *Answer:* Some energy is always lost as heat due to friction and other effects.

## Chapter 12. Sound

### 12.1 Nature of Sound and Its Propagation

Sound is a mechanical wave produced by vibrating bodies. It requires a medium and travels at different speeds in different materials.

**Key points**

- Sound waves are longitudinal; particles vibrate along the direction of travel.
- Speed of sound in air at 0 °C is about 331 m/s; it increases with temperature.
- Sound travels faster in solids than in liquids and gases.
- Frequency (Hz) determines pitch; amplitude determines loudness.
- Audible range for humans is about 20 Hz to 20 kHz; ultrasound is above 20 kHz.

**Worked examples**

1. **Counting to find distance.** Sound travels about 340 m/s in air. If thunder arrives 4 s after lightning, the storm is about 4 × 340 = 1360 m away.

2. **Bats and ultrasound.** Bats emit ultrasound and listen for echoes to locate insects — echolocation. Sonar uses the same principle in water.

**Practice questions**

1. Sound waves are: (A) Longitudinal (B) Transverse (C) Electromagnetic (D) Light waves
   - *Answer:* Longitudinal. Sound waves cause compressions and rarefactions along the direction of travel.
2. Ultrasound has frequency: (A) Above 20 kHz (B) Below 20 Hz (C) Between 20 Hz and 20 kHz (D) Exactly 20 Hz
   - *Answer:* Above 20 kHz. Ultrasound lies above the human audible range.
3. Why can't sound travel through a vacuum?
   - *Answer:* Sound needs particles to vibrate and carry energy; a vacuum has no particles.

### 12.2 Reflection of Sound and Applications

Sound reflects like light. Echoes and reverberation are reflection effects; ultrasound is used in medicine and industry.

**Key points**

- An echo is heard when the reflected sound reaches the ear at least 0.1 s after the original.
- Reverberation is the persistence of sound due to multiple reflections in a hall.
- Echo ranging: d = v × t ÷ 2 for a round trip.
- Ultrasound is used in sonography, cleaning and flaw detection.
- Sound-absorbing materials reduce reverberation in auditoriums.

**Worked examples**

1. **Echo distance.** An echo returns after 0.6 s (round trip). With v = 340 m/s, distance = (340 × 0.6) ÷ 2 = 102 m.

2. **Sonography.** High-frequency ultrasound waves reflect differently from tissues of different density, building an image without radiation.

**Practice questions**

1. An echo is due to: (A) Reflection of sound (B) Refraction (C) Diffraction (D) Absorption
   - *Answer:* Reflection of sound. Sound reflects from surfaces.
2. Sonar uses: (A) Ultrasound (B) Infrasound (C) Light (D) X-rays
   - *Answer:* Ultrasound. Sonar sends ultrasound pulses and measures echoes.
3. What is reverberation?
   - *Answer:* The persistence of sound in a hall due to repeated reflections from walls and ceiling.

## Chapter 13. Why Do We Fall Ill?

### 13.1 Health, Disease and Causes

Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being. Disease results from pathogens, deficiencies, genetic defects or environmental factors.

**Key points**

- Infectious diseases are caused by pathogens and can spread.
- Non-infectious diseases are caused by deficiency, genetics or environment and do not spread.
- Pathogens include viruses, bacteria, fungi and protozoa.
- Disease spreads through air, water, food, contact and vectors.
- A balanced diet and clean environment support health.

**Worked examples**

1. **Cholera in a village.** The bacterium Vibrio cholerae spreads through contaminated water. Providing safe water and sanitation breaks the transmission cycle.

2. **Why diabetes is not contagious.** Type 2 diabetes is caused by insulin resistance related to genetics and lifestyle, not by a pathogen, so it does not spread.

**Practice questions**

1. Which is an infectious disease? (A) Malaria (B) Diabetes (C) Hypertension (D) Scurvy
   - *Answer:* Malaria. Malaria is caused by a pathogen and is transmitted by mosquitoes.
2. Non-infectious diseases are caused by: (A) Deficiency, genetics or environment (B) Pathogens only (C) Vectors only (D) Air only
   - *Answer:* Deficiency, genetics or environment. They do not involve a transmissible pathogen.
3. Define health.
   - *Answer:* A state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease.

### 13.2 Prevention and Treatment of Disease

Preventing disease is better than treating it. Vaccination, hygiene, safe water and vector control reduce transmission; medicines target specific pathogens.

**Key points**

- Vaccines train the immune system by exposing it to weakened or killed pathogens.
- Antibiotics work against bacteria, not viruses.
- Clean water, handwashing and safe food prevent waterborne and foodborne diseases.
- Mosquito nets and draining stagnant water reduce malaria and dengue.
- Health infrastructure and awareness are as important as medicines.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why antibiotics do not cure a cold.** The common cold is caused by viruses, and antibiotics act on bacterial structures that viruses do not have.

2. **Two-dose immunisation.** Some vaccines need two doses to build a strong memory of the pathogen, so completing the schedule matters for immunity.

**Practice questions**

1. Antibiotics are effective against: (A) Bacteria (B) Viruses (C) All pathogens (D) Only fungi
   - *Answer:* Bacteria. Antibiotics target bacterial structures and processes.
2. A vaccine works by: (A) Training the immune system (B) Killing all bacteria (C) Replacing blood (D) Curing allergies
   - *Answer:* Training the immune system. It creates memory cells that respond quickly to infection.
3. Name three measures to prevent communicable diseases.
   - *Answer:* Vaccination, safe drinking water and good hygiene (also vector control).

## Chapter 14. Natural Resources

### 14.1 Air, Water and Soil

Natural resources include air, water, soil, minerals and biodiversity. Sustainable use keeps them available for future generations.

**Key points**

- Air is a mixture of gases; the percentage of carbon dioxide is rising due to burning fuels.
- Water is renewed through the water cycle but freshwater is scarce.
- Soil forms slowly and is lost by erosion.
- Ozone in the stratosphere protects life from ultraviolet radiation.
- Air, water and soil pollution damage ecosystems.

**Worked examples**

1. **The ozone hole.** CFCs and halons release chlorine and bromine radicals that destroy ozone over Antarctica. The Montreal Protocol phased out CFCs.

2. **Soil erosion on a hill.** Removing trees exposes the soil to rain, which washes it down. Terracing and tree planting slow the water and hold the soil.

**Practice questions**

1. Which gas forms the ozone layer? (A) Ozone (O<sub>3</sub>) (B) Oxygen (O<sub>2</sub>) (C) Carbon dioxide (D) Nitrogen
   - *Answer:* Ozone (O<sub>3</sub>). Ozone absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation.
2. Rising carbon dioxide mainly contributes to: (A) Global warming (B) Ozone formation (C) Soil formation (D) Rainfall increase
   - *Answer:* Global warming. Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas.
3. Why is the ozone layer important?
   - *Answer:* It absorbs UV radiation that would harm living organisms.

### 14.2 Biodiversity and Conservation

Biodiversity includes genetic, species and ecosystem variety. Its loss threatens ecosystem services and human welfare.

**Key points**

- Hotspots are regions with high species diversity and high threat.
- Conservation includes protected areas, seed banks and captive breeding.
- Ecosystem services include pollination, water purification and climate regulation.
- Sustainable development meets present needs without compromising future generations.
- Reduce, reuse and recycle, and use renewable energy.

**Worked examples**

1. **Pollinators and food.** Without bees and other pollinators, many fruits and vegetables would fail, showing that biodiversity directly supports food production.

2. **Wetlands as filters.** Wetlands trap sediment and pollutants and recharge groundwater, providing free water treatment to cities.

**Practice questions**

1. The variety of life on Earth is called: (A) Biodiversity (B) Biomass (C) Biogeography (D) Biosphere
   - *Answer:* Biodiversity. Biodiversity is the variety of genes, species and ecosystems.
2. Sustainable development means: (A) Meeting present needs without harming future generations (B) Using all resources now (C) Stopping all industry (D) Ignoring pollution
   - *Answer:* Meeting present needs without harming future generations. It balances present and future needs.
3. Give two examples of ecosystem services.
   - *Answer:* Pollination of crops and water purification by wetlands.

## Chapter 15. Improvement in Food Resources

### 15.1 Crop Improvement and Management

Food production can be raised by better varieties, nutrients, water and pest management. Sustainable practices protect soil and water.

**Key points**

- Plant breeding develops high-yield, disease-resistant and short-duration varieties.
- Macronutrients (N, P, K) and micronutrients (B, Zn, Cu, Mn) are needed in small amounts.
- Manure adds organic matter; fertilisers supply concentrated nutrients but can pollute.
- Crop rotation with legumes restores nitrogen.
- Integrated pest management uses biological and chemical control together.

**Worked examples**

1. **Green revolution.** High-yield dwarf wheat and rice varieties combined with irrigation and fertiliser raised production sharply in the 1960s, showing the impact of improved varieties.

2. **Mixed cropping.** Growing a legume with a cereal uses soil nitrogen more efficiently and spreads risk if one crop fails.

**Practice questions**

1. Which nutrient helps in forming proteins in plants? (A) Nitrogen (B) Potassium (C) Calcium (D) Iron
   - *Answer:* Nitrogen. Nitrogen is essential for protein and chlorophyll synthesis.
2. Manure is a: (A) Organic source of nutrients (B) Chemical fertiliser (C) Pesticide (D) Herbicide
   - *Answer:* Organic source of nutrients. Manure is decomposed organic matter.
3. Why is crop rotation with legumes beneficial?
   - *Answer:* Legumes fix atmospheric nitrogen, improving soil fertility and breaking pest cycles.

### 15.2 Animal Husbandry and Fisheries

Animal husbandry provides milk, meat, eggs and fibre through breeding, feeding and disease control, while fisheries and beekeeping add income.

**Key points**

- Cattle breeds: milch (dairy) and draught (work); cross-breeding improves yield.
- Poultry farming produces eggs and meat; housing and vaccination improve survival.
- Fish farming (aquaculture) combines capture and culture fisheries.
- Bee keeping (apiculture) yields honey and wax and supports pollination.
- Disease control and proper nutrition reduce losses.

**Worked examples**

1. **Cross-breeding for milk.** Crossing an indigenous cow with a high-yield breed produces offspring with better milk yield and local adaptability.

2. **Apiculture and crops.** Placing beehives near orchards improves pollination and fruit set while producing honey.

**Practice questions**

1. Rearing of honeybees is called: (A) Apiculture (B) Pisciculture (C) Sericulture (D) Horticulture
   - *Answer:* Apiculture. Apiculture is beekeeping.
2. Which of these improves milk yield in cattle? (A) Cross-breeding (B) Less water (C) More noise (D) Less feed
   - *Answer:* Cross-breeding. Selective breeding improves productivity.
3. Name two products of apiculture.
   - *Answer:* Honey and beeswax (and pollination service).
