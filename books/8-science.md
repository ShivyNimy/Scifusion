## Chapter 1. Crop Production and Management

### 1.1 Agricultural Practices

Growing a crop involves a sequence of practices: preparing the soil, sowing, adding manure and fertilisers, irrigating, protecting from weeds and pests, harvesting and storing.

**Key points**

- Ploughing loosens and aerates the soil so roots can grow and breathe.
- Sowing should place seeds at the right depth and spacing.
- Manure is organic and improves soil texture; fertilisers are chemical nutrient concentrates.
- Irrigation supplies water; excessive irrigation can waterlog the soil and damage crops.
- Weeds compete with crops for water, nutrients and light and must be removed (weeding).

**Worked examples**

1. **Manure versus fertiliser.** Manure adds humus and improves water retention but has low nutrient concentration. Fertilisers deliver nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium quickly but can pollute water if overused.

2. **Why crop rotation works.** Growing a legume such as gram after wheat restores nitrogen through Rhizobium, breaking pest cycles and improving soil fertility without extra fertiliser.

**Practice questions**

1. Which practice loosens and aerates the soil? (A) Ploughing (B) Harvesting (C) Winnowing (D) Storage
   - *Answer:* Ploughing. Ploughing turns and loosens the soil.
2. Fertilisers are mainly: (A) Chemical nutrient salts (B) Decomposed plants (C) Pesticides (D) Seeds
   - *Answer:* Chemical nutrient salts. They are concentrated mineral nutrients, usually N, P and K.
3. Why should weeds be removed from a crop field?
   - *Answer:* Weeds compete with crops for water, nutrients, light and space, reducing yield.

### 1.2 Irrigation, Harvesting and Storage

Water, timing and safe storage decide the final yield and quality of a crop.

**Key points**

- Sources of irrigation include wells, canals, rivers, tanks and rainwater harvesting.
- Drip and sprinkler irrigation save water compared with flood irrigation.
- Harvesting is cutting the mature crop; threshing separates grain from the stalk.
- Grains must be dried before storage to prevent mould and insect attack.
- Grain silos and hermetic bags protect stored grain; neem leaves are a traditional insect deterrent.

**Worked examples**

1. **Moisture and mould.** Storing damp grain in a closed sack causes fungi to grow and produce toxins. Drying grain in the sun before storage prevents spoilage.

2. **Drip irrigation in a dry district.** Drip lines deliver water drop by drop at the roots. In water-scarce areas, this can save more than half the water used by flood irrigation.

**Practice questions**

1. Which method saves the most irrigation water? (A) Drip irrigation (B) Flood irrigation (C) Canal flooding (D) Rain-fed only
   - *Answer:* Drip irrigation. Drip irrigation delivers water directly to the root zone.
2. Grain should be stored after: (A) Drying (B) Wetting (C) Sprouting (D) Threshing in rain
   - *Answer:* Drying. Dry grain resists mould and pests.
3. Name two modern irrigation methods.
   - *Answer:* Drip irrigation and sprinkler irrigation.

## Chapter 2. Microorganisms: Friend and Foe

### 2.1 Microorganisms and Their Uses

Microorganisms are living things too small to see with the naked eye: bacteria, fungi, protozoa and algae. Many are useful in food, medicine and agriculture.

**Key points**

- Lactobacillus turns milk into curd; yeast makes bread and alcohol; moulds ripen cheese.
- Antibiotics such as penicillin come from fungi and bacteria and kill or stop bacteria.
- Vaccines use weakened or killed pathogens to train the immune system.
- Rhizobium fixes nitrogen in legume root nodules; some bacteria decompose waste.
- Microorganisms are used to make vitamins, enzymes, biogas and sewage treatment.

**Worked examples**

1. **Curd from milk.** A spoon of curd added to warm milk provides Lactobacillus bacteria. They convert milk sugar into lactic acid, which coagulates the protein and turns milk into curd.

2. **Penicillin's discovery.** Alexander Fleming noticed that a mould on a petri dish stopped bacteria from growing. That mould, Penicillium, became the source of the first antibiotic, penicillin.

**Practice questions**

1. Which microorganism turns milk into curd? (A) Lactobacillus (B) Yeast (C) Amoeba (D) Rhizobium
   - *Answer:* Lactobacillus. Lactobacillus bacteria produce lactic acid.
2. Antibiotics are used to treat: (A) Bacterial infections (B) Viral infections (C) All diseases (D) Injuries
   - *Answer:* Bacterial infections. Antibiotics act on bacteria, not viruses.
3. Name two useful products made with the help of microorganisms.
   - *Answer:* Curd (Lactobacillus) and bread (yeast); also antibiotics and biogas.

### 2.2 Diseases Caused by Microorganisms

Some microorganisms are pathogens. They spread through air, water, food, contact and vectors, and cause diseases in humans, animals and plants.

**Key points**

- Human diseases: tuberculosis and cholera (bacteria), common cold and COVID-19 (viruses), malaria (protozoan), ringworm (fungus).
- Plant diseases: citrus canker (bacteria), rust of wheat (fungus), mosaic disease (virus).
- Malaria spreads through the bite of a female Anopheles mosquito; dengue through Aedes.
- Prevention: vaccination, clean water, handwashing, covering food, vector control.
- Food preservation (boiling, refrigeration, salting, drying, canning) slows or stops microbial growth.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why boiling kills germs.** High temperature denatures the proteins and enzymes of pathogens, so they cannot survive. Boiling water for a minute makes it much safer to drink.

2. **Vaccination works by training.** A vaccine exposes the immune system to a harmless part of a pathogen. The body makes antibodies and memory cells, so a real infection is fought off quickly.

**Practice questions**

1. Malaria is caused by a: (A) Protozoan (B) Virus (C) Fungus (D) Bacterium
   - *Answer:* Protozoan. Plasmodium, a protozoan, causes malaria, spread by Anopheles mosquitoes.
2. Which food preservation method removes water? (A) Drying (B) Freezing (C) Boiling (D) Refrigeration
   - *Answer:* Drying. Drying removes water that microorganisms need to grow.
3. Name two ways to prevent the spread of communicable diseases.
   - *Answer:* Vaccination and safe drinking water (also handwashing and vector control).

## Chapter 3. Synthetic Fibres and Plastics

### 3.1 Synthetic Fibres

Synthetic fibres are made from chemicals, usually from petroleum. Rayon, nylon, polyester and acrylic are common, each with distinct properties and uses.

**Key points**

- Rayon is semisynthetic, made from wood pulp treated with chemicals; it is cheap and absorbs water.
- Nylon is strong, elastic and light; it is used in ropes, parachutes, toothbrush bristles and clothing.
- Polyester is wrinkle-resistant, strong and quick-drying.
- Acrylic is warm, soft and often used as a wool substitute in blankets.
- Synthetic fibres do not absorb water well, so they dry quickly but can trap sweat.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why nylon ropes are strong.** Long polymer chains in nylon pack closely and resist pulling, making nylon stronger than many natural fibres for a given thickness.

2. **Cotton versus polyester in summer.** Cotton absorbs sweat and allows air through, so it feels cool. Polyester traps moisture, so it feels sticky in hot weather.

**Practice questions**

1. Rayon is made from: (A) Wood pulp (B) Petroleum (C) Coal (D) Cotton seed
   - *Answer:* Wood pulp. Rayon is a semisynthetic fibre derived from cellulose in wood pulp.
2. Which synthetic fibre is strongest and most elastic for ropes? (A) Nylon (B) Acrylic (C) Rayon (D) Wool
   - *Answer:* Nylon. Nylon has high tensile strength and elasticity.
3. Why are synthetic clothes uncomfortable in summer?
   - *Answer:* They do not absorb sweat readily, so moisture stays trapped against the skin.

### 3.2 Plastics and the Environment

Plastics are polymers: long chains of small units. Thermoplastics soften on heating and can be reshaped; thermosetting plastics cannot. Plastic waste is a major environmental problem.

**Key points**

- Thermoplastics: polythene, PVC, polystyrene — soften on heating.
- Thermosetting plastics: bakelite, melamine — hard, heat-resistant, and cannot be remoulded.
- Bakelite is used for electrical switches; melamine for crockery.
- Plastic is non-biodegradable, so it persists for decades or centuries.
- Reduce, reuse and recycle; avoid single-use plastic; use biodegradable alternatives.

**Worked examples**

1. **Bakelite in a switch.** Bakelite is a poor conductor of heat and electricity and does not soften with heat, so it is ideal for switch covers and pan handles.

2. **Why plastic ends up in the ocean.** Light plastic bags blow away, drain into rivers and reach the sea, where animals eat or get entangled in them. That is why single-use plastic is banned in many places.

**Practice questions**

1. Which plastic cannot be softened and remoulded? (A) Bakelite (B) Polythene (C) PVC (D) Polystyrene
   - *Answer:* Bakelite. Thermosetting plastics such as bakelite degrade rather than melt.
2. Why is plastic a pollution problem? (A) It is non-biodegradable (B) It is cheap (C) It is light (D) It is strong
   - *Answer:* It is non-biodegradable. Microorganisms cannot break it down, so it accumulates.
3. Distinguish thermoplastics from thermosetting plastics with one example each.
   - *Answer:* Thermoplastics soften on heating and can be remoulded (polythene); thermosetting plastics cannot (bakelite).

## Chapter 4. Materials: Metals and Non-metals

### 4.1 Physical and Chemical Properties

Metals are typically shiny, hard, malleable, ductile and good conductors. Non-metals are dull, brittle and poor conductors. Their reactions reveal their reactivity.

**Key points**

- Metals: iron, copper, aluminium, zinc, gold, silver.
- Non-metals: oxygen, sulphur, carbon, phosphorus, chlorine, nitrogen.
- Metals react with oxygen to form basic oxides; non-metals form acidic oxides.
- Metals react with acids to give hydrogen; gold and silver do not react with dilute acids.
- Metals are sonorous; non-metals are not.

**Worked examples**

1. **Metal oxides and litmus.** Burn magnesium in air to get magnesium oxide, then dissolve it in water. The solution turns red litmus blue, showing it is basic. Burning sulphur gives an acidic oxide.

2. **Why a metal spoon bends.** Metals are malleable, so their layers can slide over each other without breaking. That is why metal sheets can be hammered into shape.

**Practice questions**

1. Which of these is a non-metal? (A) Sulphur (B) Copper (C) Aluminium (D) Zinc
   - *Answer:* Sulphur. Sulphur is a non-metal, brittle and a poor conductor.
2. Metals react with dilute acids to produce: (A) Hydrogen (B) Oxygen (C) Nitrogen (D) Chlorine
   - *Answer:* Hydrogen. Hydrogen gas is displaced from the acid.
3. Why is sodium kept immersed in kerosene?
   - *Answer:* It is so reactive that it catches fire in air; kerosene keeps out air and moisture.

### 4.2 Uses of Metals and Non-metals

Properties decide uses. Metals make structures, wires and utensils; non-metals are vital in life, fertilisers and industry.

**Key points**

- Iron and steel: construction, machines and vehicles.
- Copper and aluminium: electrical wiring; aluminium is light and corrosion-resistant.
- Gold and silver: jewellery because they are unreactive and lustrous.
- Non-metals: oxygen for breathing, nitrogen in fertilisers, carbon in steel, chlorine in water treatment.
- Alloys combine metals to improve properties, for example stainless steel (iron, chromium, nickel).

**Worked examples**

1. **Aluminium in aircraft.** Aluminium is light and strong enough when alloyed, and resists corrosion by forming an oxide layer, so it is ideal where weight matters.

2. **Noble metals in jewellery.** Gold is unreactive and does not tarnish, so it keeps its shine. Pure gold is soft, so it is alloyed with copper or silver for strength.

**Practice questions**

1. Which metal is used for electrical wiring because of good conductivity? (A) Copper (B) Sulphur (C) Iodine (D) Carbon
   - *Answer:* Copper. Copper conducts electricity and is ductile enough to draw into wire.
2. Stainless steel is an alloy of: (A) Iron, chromium and nickel (B) Copper and zinc (C) Aluminium and lead (D) Gold and silver
   - *Answer:* Iron, chromium and nickel. Chromium and nickel improve strength and rust resistance.
3. Give one use each of oxygen and nitrogen.
   - *Answer:* Oxygen: respiration and medical support; nitrogen: fertilisers and food packaging.

## Chapter 5. Coal and Petroleum

### 5.1 Fossil Fuels and Their Formation

Coal and petroleum are fossil fuels formed from ancient living matter over millions of years. They are exhaustible resources.

**Key points**

- Coal formed from compressed plant remains under heat and pressure.
- Petroleum formed from marine organisms buried in sediments.
- Fractional distillation separates petroleum into petrol, diesel, kerosene, lubricating oil and bitumen.
- Exhaustible resources are limited and will run out; inexhaustible resources such as sunlight are not used up.
- Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas.

**Worked examples**

1. **Refining petroleum.** Crude oil is heated in a fractionating column. Different fractions condense at different temperatures: gas at the top, then petrol, kerosene, diesel and bitumen at the bottom.

2. **Coal tar and coke.** Heating coal in the absence of air gives coke and coal tar. Coke is used in steel making and coal tar in dyes and medicines.

**Practice questions**

1. Which resource is exhaustible? (A) Coal (B) Sunlight (C) Wind (D) Tidal energy
   - *Answer:* Coal. Coal is finite and cannot be replenished on human timescales.
2. Petroleum is refined by: (A) Fractional distillation (B) Filtration (C) Evaporation (D) Condensation
   - *Answer:* Fractional distillation. Fractions separate by boiling point in a fractionating column.
3. Why are fossil fuels called non-renewable?
   - *Answer:* They formed over millions of years and are being used much faster than they form.

### 5.2 Conservation of Resources

Conserving fuels means using less, using efficiently and switching to renewable sources such as solar, wind and hydel power.

**Key points**

- Switch off lights and appliances when not needed.
- Use public transport, cycling and walking; carpool where possible.
- Use renewable energy: solar panels, wind turbines and hydropower.
- Recycle metals, paper and plastics to save energy.
- Plant trees to absorb carbon dioxide.

**Worked examples**

1. **Solar water heater.** Black panels absorb sunlight and heat water without burning fuel, reducing electricity or gas use.

2. **A community cycle lane.** Dedicated cycle lanes encourage short trips by bicycle, reducing fuel use and air pollution.

**Practice questions**

1. Which energy source is renewable? (A) Solar energy (B) Coal (C) Petroleum (D) Natural gas
   - *Answer:* Solar energy. Sunlight is continuously available.
2. Recycling aluminium saves mainly: (A) Energy and raw material (B) Water only (C) Land only (D) Labour only
   - *Answer:* Energy and raw material. Recycling aluminium uses far less energy than extracting it from ore.
3. State two ways to reduce consumption of petrol.
   - *Answer:* Use public transport and maintain properly inflated tyres (or cycle for short trips).

## Chapter 6. Combustion and Flame

### 6.1 Combustion

Combustion is a chemical reaction in which a substance burns in oxygen, releasing heat and light. Combustible substances burn; others do not.

**Key points**

- Conditions for combustion: a combustible substance, oxygen and heat to reach the ignition temperature.
- Burning can produce flame, heat, smoke and ash.
- Incomplete combustion produces carbon monoxide, a poisonous gas.
- Fire extinguishers cut off oxygen, remove heat or stop the fuel supply.
- Water is good for many fires but dangerous for electrical and oil fires.

**Worked examples**

1. **The candle flame.** The part near the wick is dark and cool, the middle is bright yellow and luminous because of burning carbon particles, and the outer zone is the hottest because oxygen is plentiful.

2. **Why water must not be used on electrical fires.** Water conducts electricity and can spread the current, causing shock. Carbon dioxide extinguishers smother the fire without conducting.

**Practice questions**

1. Which is required for combustion? (A) Oxygen (B) Nitrogen (C) Carbon dioxide (D) Argon
   - *Answer:* Oxygen. Oxygen supports combustion.
2. The hottest part of a candle flame is the: (A) Outer zone (B) Middle zone (C) Inner zone (D) Wick
   - *Answer:* Outer zone. The outer zone has the most complete combustion.
3. Name two ways to extinguish a fire.
   - *Answer:* Cut off the oxygen supply and remove the heat (cool it).

### 6.2 Fuels, Calorific Value and Pollution

Fuels release energy when burnt. Calorific value measures the energy per unit mass, and exhaust gases pollute air and cause acid rain.

**Key points**

- Good fuel: high calorific value, low cost, low smoke, easy storage and transport.
- Ideal fuel conditions: high energy per kg, no harmful residue and moderate ignition temperature.
- Burning fuels produces carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides.
- Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides cause acid rain.
- Alternative fuels: LPG, CNG, biogas, hydrogen, ethanol and electricity.

**Worked examples**

1. **Calorific value comparison.** A fuel with a high calorific value releases more energy per kilogram, so less fuel is needed. Hydrogen has the highest calorific value per unit mass but is difficult to store.

2. **CNG in city buses.** CNG burns more cleanly than diesel, reducing soot and particulate pollution in crowded cities.

**Practice questions**

1. The unit of calorific value is: (A) kJ/kg (B) kg/kJ (C) °C (D) m/s
   - *Answer:* kJ/kg. Calorific value is energy released per kilogram.
2. Acid rain is mainly caused by: (A) Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides (B) Oxygen (C) Water vapour (D) Nitrogen
   - *Answer:* Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides. These oxides dissolve in rain water to form acids.
3. What is a good fuel?
   - *Answer:* A fuel that has high calorific value, low pollution, and is cheap and easy to store and transport.

## Chapter 7. Conservation of Plants and Animals

### 7.1 Deforestation and Its Consequences

Deforestation is the clearing of forests, which destroys habitat and disrupts the water cycle, soil and climate.

**Key points**

- Causes: agriculture, housing, industry, roads, mining, fire and logging.
- Consequences: habitat loss, soil erosion, floods, reduced rainfall, carbon dioxide rise and species extinction.
- Deforestation increases the risk of desertification and landslides.
- Reforestation and afforestation restore forests and habitats.
- Endemic species live only in a particular area and are most vulnerable.

**Worked examples**

1. **A landslide after the trees are cut.** Tree roots grip the soil and slow rainwater. Without roots, water saturates the slope and soil slides away — a direct, visible consequence of deforestation.

2. **The red panda.** The red panda lives only in eastern Himalayan forests and is endangered because bamboo habitat is being cleared. It is an endemic species.

**Practice questions**

1. Clearing of forests is called: (A) Deforestation (B) Afforestation (C) Conservation (D) Reforestation
   - *Answer:* Deforestation. Deforestation is the removal of forest cover.
2. Animals found only in a specific area are called: (A) Endemic (B) Extinct (C) Domestic (D) Migratory
   - *Answer:* Endemic. Endemic species are restricted to a specific region.
3. State two consequences of deforestation.
   - *Answer:* Habitat loss and increased soil erosion and floods.

### 7.2 Protected Areas and Wildlife Conservation

India protects biodiversity through national parks, wildlife sanctuaries and biosphere reserves, and through laws that ban hunting and illegal trade.

**Key points**

- A national park conserves the whole ecosystem; a sanctuary protects particular species.
- The Wildlife Protection Act (1972) protects wildlife in India.
- Project Tiger protects tigers and their habitats.
- Biosphere reserves allow conservation together with sustainable human activity.
- Species are classified as endangered, vulnerable or extinct based on risk.

**Worked examples**

1. **Kaziranga and the rhino.** Kaziranga National Park protects the one-horned rhinoceros in Assam. Patrols, grassland management and tourism fees have helped numbers recover.

2. **Why community participation matters.** Bird sanctuaries and community reserves work best when local people see income or pride in protecting wildlife, such as in the Chilika lagoon.

**Practice questions**

1. Which law protects wildlife in India? (A) Wildlife Protection Act 1972 (B) Factories Act (C) Forest Survey Act (D) Environment Act 1986
   - *Answer:* Wildlife Protection Act 1972. The Wildlife Protection Act provides legal protection to wildlife.
2. Project Tiger was launched to: (A) Protect tigers and their habitat (B) Count trees (C) Plant crops (D) Tour forests
   - *Answer:* Protect tigers and their habitat. It conserves tiger populations and their ecosystems.
3. Differentiate a national park from a wildlife sanctuary.
   - *Answer:* A national park conserves the whole ecosystem; a sanctuary mainly protects specific species and allows some regulated activities.

## Chapter 8. Cell: Structure and Functions

### 8.1 The Cell and Its Organelles

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. Organisms are unicellular or multicellular, and plant and animal cells differ in important ways.

**Key points**

- Cell membrane controls entry and exit of substances; cytoplasm holds organelles; nucleus contains chromosomes.
- Mitochondria release energy; ribosomes make proteins; vacuoles store water and waste.
- Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large central vacuole, while animal cells have neither cell wall nor chloroplasts.
- Unicellular organisms such as Amoeba carry out all life processes in one cell.
- Cells specialize by shape and function, for example nerve cells, muscle cells and red blood cells.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why plant cells stay firm.** The rigid cell wall supports the plant and resists water pressure, so leaves and stems stay upright. Animal cells lack a wall and rely on a flexible membrane.

2. **Nerve cell shape.** A nerve cell has long projections to carry electrical signals quickly over distances, showing that cell shape matches function.

**Practice questions**

1. The control centre of the cell is the: (A) Nucleus (B) Mitochondrion (C) Cell membrane (D) Vacuole
   - *Answer:* Nucleus. The nucleus contains the genetic material that directs cell activity.
2. Which structure is present in plant cells but not in animal cells? (A) Cell wall (B) Nucleus (C) Mitochondria (D) Cell membrane
   - *Answer:* Cell wall. The cellulose cell wall gives plants shape and rigidity.
3. Why is the cell called the structural and functional unit of life?
   - *Answer:* It is the smallest unit that can carry out all life processes and makes up all living things.

### 8.2 Cell Division and Organisation

Cells divide to grow and reproduce. In multicellular organisms, cells are organised into tissues, organs, organ systems and the whole organism.

**Key points**

- Mitosis produces identical cells for growth and repair.
- Meiosis produces gametes with half the chromosome number.
- Tissues are groups of similar cells performing a specific function.
- Organs are made of tissues (for example, the stomach), and organ systems of organs (for example, the digestive system).
- In unicellular organisms, cell division is reproduction.

**Worked examples**

1. **From cell to organism.** Muscle cells form muscle tissue, muscle tissue plus other tissues forms the heart, the heart works with blood vessels as the circulatory system, and the systems form the organism.

2. **Why cells must stay small.** A small cell has a large surface area relative to its volume, so exchange of gases and nutrients is fast enough. Very large cells cannot supply their interior efficiently.

**Practice questions**

1. Mitosis is used for: (A) Growth and repair (B) Producing gametes (C) Digestion (D) Respiration
   - *Answer:* Growth and repair. Mitosis produces identical daughter cells.
2. A group of similar cells performing a specific function is a: (A) Tissue (B) Organ (C) Organ system (D) Organism
   - *Answer:* Tissue. Tissues are groups of similar cells.
3. Arrange in order of organisation: organ, cell, organ system, tissue.
   - *Answer:* Cell -> tissue -> organ -> organ system.

## Chapter 9. Reproduction in Animals

### 9.1 Sexual and Asexual Reproduction in Animals

Animals reproduce sexually by the fusion of gametes, or asexually from a single parent. Sexual reproduction creates variation.

**Key points**

- Male gametes are sperm; female gametes are eggs (ova).
- Fertilisation may be external (frogs, fish) or internal (humans, birds).
- Viviparous animals give birth to young; oviparous animals lay eggs.
- Asexual methods include binary fission (Amoeba), budding (Hydra) and regeneration (Planaria).
- In humans, the zygote develops inside the uterus for about nine months.

**Worked examples**

1. **Frog versus hen.** Frogs lay eggs in water and fertilise them externally, while hens lay shelled eggs that are fertilised internally and incubated outside the body.

2. **Hydra budding.** A bud grows on the side of a Hydra, develops tentacles and detaches as a new individual — one parent, no fertilisation.

**Practice questions**

1. Fertilisation in frogs is: (A) External (B) Internal (C) Absent (D) In the uterus
   - *Answer:* External. Eggs and sperm meet in water.
2. Animals that give birth to young are called: (A) Viviparous (B) Oviparous (C) Asexual (D) Budding
   - *Answer:* Viviparous. Viviparous animals develop the embryo inside the mother.
3. Why is sexual reproduction advantageous to a species?
   - *Answer:* It produces genetic variation, helping the population adapt to changing conditions.

### 9.2 Development and Metamorphosis

After fertilisation, the zygote develops into an embryo. Some animals change form dramatically during development.

**Key points**

- The zygote divides repeatedly to form an embryo, which develops organs in the uterus.
- The placenta exchanges oxygen, nutrients and wastes between mother and embryo.
- Metamorphosis transforms a larva into an adult, as in frogs and butterflies.
- Tadpoles have gills and tails; adult frogs have lungs and legs.
- Care of young increases survival in many species.

**Worked examples**

1. **Frog metamorphosis.** Egg -> tadpole (gills, tail) -> froglet -> adult frog. Each stage is adapted to a different way of life.

2. **Placenta in mammals.** The placenta lets nutrients and oxygen reach the embryo from the mother's blood and removes wastes.

**Practice questions**

1. The change from larva to adult is called: (A) Metamorphosis (B) Fertilisation (C) Budding (D) Fragmentation
   - *Answer:* Metamorphosis. Metamorphosis is a dramatic change in body form.
2. A tadpole breathes using: (A) Gills (B) Lungs (C) Spiracles (D) Skin only
   - *Answer:* Gills. Tadpoles have gills for aquatic life.
3. What is the function of the placenta?
   - *Answer:* It allows exchange of oxygen, nutrients and wastes between the mother and the developing embryo.

## Chapter 10. Reaching the Age of Adolescence

### 10.1 Adolescence and Puberty

Adolescence is the period between childhood and adulthood when the body becomes capable of reproduction. Puberty is the set of changes that begin this transition.

**Key points**

- Puberty starts between about 10 and 14 years and is controlled by hormones.
- Secondary sexual characteristics develop: voice change, body hair, breast development, growth spurt.
- Hormones such as testosterone and oestrogen drive these changes.
- Adolescence also brings emotional and social changes.
- A balanced diet, exercise and good hygiene are important during this period.

**Worked examples**

1. **The growth spurt.** The body grows rapidly in height during early puberty, often reaching close to adult height by the mid-teens. Growth depends on heredity and nutrition.

2. **Why adolescents need more protein and iron.** Rapid growth builds new tissue and blood; girls also lose iron during menstruation, so a nutrient-rich diet prevents anaemia.

**Practice questions**

1. The period between childhood and adulthood is called: (A) Adolescence (B) Infancy (C) Old age (D) Puberty
   - *Answer:* Adolescence. Adolescence is the transition to adulthood.
2. Which hormone helps bring about male secondary sexual characteristics? (A) Testosterone (B) Insulin (C) Thyroxine (D) Adrenaline
   - *Answer:* Testosterone. Testosterone is produced in the testes.
3. Define puberty.
   - *Answer:* The stage when the body becomes capable of reproduction, marked by physical changes controlled by hormones.

### 10.2 Reproductive Health and Hormonal Changes

Reproductive health means physical and mental well-being in relation to the reproductive system, including hygiene, safe habits and reliable information.

**Key points**

- Girls need to manage menstruation hygienically with clean pads and proper disposal.
- Adolescents should keep the genital area clean and wear loose cotton clothing.
- Avoid myths and misinformation; seek reliable information from parents, teachers and health workers.
- The pituitary gland controls growth and reproductive hormones.
- Respect and consent are part of healthy relationships.

**Worked examples**

1. **Menstrual hygiene.** Changing pads every 4–6 hours, washing the area and disposing of pads properly prevents infections and discomfort.

2. **Why sleep matters.** During adolescence, growth and hormonal changes are demanding, and 8–9 hours of sleep support growth, memory and mood.

**Practice questions**

1. Reproductive health includes: (A) Physical and mental well-being (B) Only height (C) Only weight (D) Only exercise
   - *Answer:* Physical and mental well-being. It covers overall well-being related to reproduction.
2. During menstruation, it is important to: (A) Practise hygiene with clean pads (B) Avoid all water (C) Stop school (D) Avoid milk
   - *Answer:* Practise hygiene with clean pads. Cleanliness prevents infection and discomfort.
3. Name two habits that support adolescent health.
   - *Answer:* Balanced diet and adequate sleep (also exercise and hygiene).

## Chapter 11. Force and Pressure

### 11.1 Force and Its Effects

A force is a push or pull that can change an object's speed, direction or shape. Forces are described by magnitude and direction.

**Key points**

- Contact forces: muscular force, friction, tension and normal force.
- Non-contact forces: magnetic, electrostatic and gravitational force.
- A force can change the state of motion, the direction of motion or the shape of an object.
- Force is measured in newtons (N).
- Resultant force: balanced forces do not change motion; unbalanced forces do.

**Worked examples**

1. **The hockey hit.** A hockey stick applies a force to the ball, changing both its speed and direction. Because the ball is light, the same force gives it a large acceleration.

2. **Stretching a rubber band.** The band stretches when pulled and returns when released. A force has changed its shape; the amount of stretch shows the size of the force.

**Practice questions**

1. Which is a non-contact force? (A) Magnetic force (B) Friction (C) Muscular force (D) Tension
   - *Answer:* Magnetic force. Magnetic force acts without touching.
2. Force is measured in: (A) Newtons (B) Joules (C) Watts (D) Pascals
   - *Answer:* Newtons. The SI unit of force is the newton.
3. State two effects a force can have on an object.
   - *Answer:* It can change the object's shape and change its speed or direction of motion.

### 11.2 Pressure in Solids, Liquids and Gases

Pressure is force per unit area. Solids exert pressure on surfaces, liquids exert pressure at a depth, and gases exert pressure in all directions.

**Key points**

- Pressure = force ÷ area; the SI unit is the pascal (Pa).
- A sharp knife cuts better because the same force acts on a smaller area, giving higher pressure.
- Liquids exert pressure on the walls and bottom of the container, and pressure increases with depth.
- Gases exert pressure equally in all directions; atmospheric pressure acts on everything.
- A syringe uses pressure differences to draw in or push out liquid.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why snowshoes work.** Snowshoes spread body weight over a large area, reducing pressure, so the wearer does not sink into soft snow.

2. **A leaking dam.** Water pressure increases with depth, so the base of a dam is built thicker and stronger than the top.

**Practice questions**

1. Pressure is: (A) Force ÷ area (B) Force × area (C) Area ÷ force (D) Force × distance
   - *Answer:* Force ÷ area. Pressure is force per unit area.
2. Liquid pressure ______ with depth. (A) Increases (B) Decreases (C) Stays constant (D) Becomes zero
   - *Answer:* Increases. More liquid above means more weight pressing down.
3. Why does a sharp knife cut better than a blunt one?
   - *Answer:* It has a smaller area of contact, so the same force produces greater pressure.

## Chapter 12. Friction

### 12.1 Friction and Its Types

Friction opposes relative motion between surfaces in contact. It can be static, sliding, rolling or fluid friction.

**Key points**

- Static friction prevents a stationary object from starting to move.
- Sliding friction acts when an object slides; rolling friction is much smaller than sliding friction.
- Fluid friction acts on objects moving through liquids or gases.
- Friction produces heat and causes wear, so oil and lubricants reduce it.
- Friction is useful: we could not walk or hold objects without it.

**Worked examples**

1. **Ball bearings.** Ball bearings replace sliding contact with rolling contact, greatly reducing friction. That is why machines with bearings run smoothly and need less energy.

2. **Why shoes wear out.** Repeated contact with rough ground rubs away material, and friction converts motion into heat. Soles are thick to last longer.

**Practice questions**

1. The friction that acts when a body rolls is: (A) Rolling friction (B) Static friction (C) Sliding friction (D) Fluid friction
   - *Answer:* Rolling friction. Rolling friction is much less than sliding friction.
2. Friction can be reduced by: (A) Using lubricants (B) Increasing weight (C) Making surfaces rough (D) Increasing speed
   - *Answer:* Using lubricants. Lubricants create a thin layer that separates surfaces.
3. Give two examples where friction is useful.
   - *Answer:* Walking on the ground and writing with a pen (also braking a bicycle).

### 12.2 Friction in Nature and Technology

Friction shapes the design of tyres, shoes, aircraft and sports equipment, and also causes energy loss in machines.

**Key points**

- Treaded tyres increase friction on wet roads and prevent skidding.
- Streamlining reduces fluid friction for fish, aircraft and high-speed trains.
- Friction between tectonic plates can cause earthquakes when they suddenly slip.
- The heat from friction is used in matches and rubbers.
- Polishing surfaces reduces friction, so more force is needed to grip them.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why racing cars have wide, smooth tyres.** Wide tyres have a larger contact area, and the smooth surface maximises grip on dry tracks; in rain, grooves clear water to keep contact. Design balances friction with speed.

2. **Streamlining a cyclist.** Tucking into an aerodynamic posture cuts air resistance at speed, allowing the cyclist to go faster with the same effort.

**Practice questions**

1. Treaded tyres are used to: (A) Increase friction and grip (B) Reduce friction (C) Reduce weight (D) Increase speed
   - *Answer:* Increase friction and grip. Treads push water away and improve grip on wet roads.
2. Streamlining reduces: (A) Fluid friction (B) Weight (C) Temperature (D) Volume
   - *Answer:* Fluid friction. A streamlined shape lets air or water flow smoothly around an object.
3. Why is friction called a necessary evil?
   - *Answer:* It causes wear and wastes energy, but without it we could not walk, hold objects or stop vehicles.

## Chapter 13. Sound

### 13.1 Sound: Vibration, Medium and Propagation

Sound is produced by vibrating objects and travels as waves through a medium. It cannot travel through a vacuum.

**Key points**

- Vibration is the to-and-fro motion of particles of a source.
- Sound needs a medium: solids, liquids and gases all transmit sound, but a vacuum cannot.
- Sound travels fastest in solids and slowest in gases.
- Compressions and rarefactions make up a sound wave.
- An echo is a reflected sound heard after a delay.

**Worked examples**

1. **The thunder delay.** Light from lightning reaches us almost instantly, but sound takes about 3 seconds per kilometre. Counting seconds until thunder gives a rough distance of the storm.

2. **A bell in a vacuum jar.** As air is pumped out of the jar, the ringing becomes fainter and finally inaudible, showing sound needs a medium.

**Practice questions**

1. Sound cannot travel through: (A) A vacuum (B) Air (C) Water (D) Steel
   - *Answer:* A vacuum. A vacuum has no particles to carry the vibration.
2. Sound travels fastest in: (A) Steel (B) Water (C) Air (D) A vacuum
   - *Answer:* Steel. Particles in solids are closest, so vibrations pass quickly.
3. Why can you hear the sound of a distant train through rails before hearing it in air?
   - *Answer:* Sound travels much faster through steel than through air.

### 13.2 Frequency, Amplitude and Noise Pollution

Frequency determines pitch, amplitude determines loudness, and noise is unwanted sound that can harm health.

**Key points**

- Frequency is measured in hertz (Hz); higher frequency means higher pitch.
- Human hearing ranges roughly from 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.
- Amplitude determines loudness; larger amplitude means louder sound.
- Noise above safe levels can cause hearing loss, stress and headaches.
- Ways to reduce noise: silencers, sound-absorbing materials, tree belts and quiet machinery.

**Worked examples**

1. **Hummingbird versus cow.** A hummingbird's wings beat dozens of times per second, making a high-pitched hum, while a cow's low-frequency moo has a lower pitch.

2. **Why classrooms need curtains.** Soft materials absorb sound and reduce echoes, so speech is clearer and noise levels drop.

**Practice questions**

1. Pitch depends on: (A) Frequency (B) Amplitude (C) Speed (D) Medium
   - *Answer:* Frequency. Higher frequency gives higher pitch.
2. Loudness depends on: (A) Amplitude (B) Frequency (C) Wavelength (D) Pitch
   - *Answer:* Amplitude. Larger amplitude means louder sound.
3. State two ways to reduce noise pollution.
   - *Answer:* Use silencers on vehicles and grow tree belts (also use sound-absorbing material).

## Chapter 14. Chemical Effects of Electric Current

### 14.1 Conductors, Electrolytes and Electrolysis

Electric current can flow through some liquids, called electrolytes, and produce chemical changes. This is electrolysis.

**Key points**

- Distilled water is a poor conductor; adding salt or acid makes it conduct.
- Electrolytes contain ions that carry current.
- Electrolysis of water produces hydrogen at the cathode and oxygen at the anode.
- Electroplating deposits a metal layer on an object using electrolysis.
- Chemical effects have many uses: refining metals, coating jewellery and making chlorine.

**Worked examples**

1. **Testing liquids.** Connect a simple circuit with a bulb through various liquids. Lemon juice, salt water and vinegar make the bulb glow; oil and distilled water do not.

2. **Electroplating a spoon.** The spoon is the cathode, a silver bar is the anode and silver salt solution is the electrolyte. Silver ions deposit on the spoon, giving it a shiny silver coating.

**Practice questions**

1. Which liquid conducts electricity best? (A) Salt water (B) Distilled water (C) Vegetable oil (D) Sugar solution
   - *Answer:* Salt water. Dissolved salts provide ions that carry current.
2. Electroplating uses: (A) Electrolysis (B) Filtration (C) Distillation (D) Evaporation
   - *Answer:* Electrolysis. Metal ions are deposited by electric current.
3. Why does distilled water not conduct electricity while tap water does?
   - *Answer:* Tap water contains dissolved salts that provide ions; distilled water has almost no ions.

### 14.2 Applications and Environmental Effects

Electrolytic processes are used industrially to purify metals and coat surfaces, but they also consume energy and can create waste.

**Key points**

- Electrorefining purifies copper and other metals used in wiring.
- Electroplating protects metals from corrosion and improves appearance.
- The electrolyte can be toxic and must be treated before disposal.
- Electrolysis of brine produces chlorine, hydrogen and sodium hydroxide, all industrially important.
- Efficient processes reduce energy use and pollution.

**Worked examples**

1. **Copper refining.** Impure copper is the anode and pure copper is the cathode. Copper ions move through the solution and deposit as pure copper on the cathode.

2. **Chrome plating on taps.** A thin layer of chromium gives taps a shiny, corrosion-resistant surface that is harder than the metal beneath.

**Practice questions**

1. Pure copper is obtained by: (A) Electrorefining (B) Filtering (C) Smelting (D) Condensing
   - *Answer:* Electrorefining. Ions of copper deposit on the cathode, leaving impurities behind.
2. Electroplating is used to: (A) Prevent corrosion and improve appearance (B) Increase weight (C) Magnetise metals (D) Soften metals
   - *Answer:* Prevent corrosion and improve appearance. A thin metal coating protects and beautifies the object.
3. Name two industries that use electrolysis.
   - *Answer:* Metal refining and electroplating (also chlorine manufacture).

## Chapter 15. Some Natural Phenomena

### 15.1 Lightning and Electric Charge

Lightning is a large electric discharge caused by the separation of charges in clouds. Electric charge can be static or flowing.

**Key points**

- Friction between cloud particles separates positive and negative charges.
- When the potential difference is large enough, charge jumps between cloud and ground or within clouds.
- Lightning produces thunder by rapidly heating air, which expands and produces sound waves.
- The electroscope detects charge; like charges repel and unlike charges attract.
- Lightning conductors (earthing) protect tall buildings.

**Worked examples**

1. **Lightning conductor.** A metal rod fixed on a building runs down to the ground. It provides a safe path for charge to reach the earth, protecting the structure.

2. **Charged balloon.** Rub a balloon on your hair and hold it near small paper pieces; the paper is attracted because the balloon carries a static charge.

**Practice questions**

1. Lightning is caused by: (A) Electric discharge (B) Friction of wind only (C) Sunlight (D) Rain only
   - *Answer:* Electric discharge. Charge separation in clouds leads to a huge discharge.
2. A lightning conductor works by: (A) Earthing the charge safely (B) Attracting rain (C) Reflecting light (D) Insulating the building
   - *Answer:* Earthing the charge safely. It provides a low-resistance path to the ground.
3. Why does thunder follow lightning?
   - *Answer:* Light reaches us almost instantly, while sound takes about 3 s per km, so thunder is heard later.

### 15.2 Earthquakes

An earthquake is the sudden shaking of the Earth's crust due to movement along faults. Its energy travels as seismic waves.

**Key points**

- Earthquakes occur mainly at plate boundaries where tectonic plates collide, separate or slide.
- The Richter scale measures magnitude; each step of 1 is about 32 times more energy.
- Seismographs record earthquakes.
- Safety: drop, cover and hold on; stay away from windows and weak structures.
- Early warning systems use seismic and satellite data.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why India has earthquakes.** The Indian plate pushes north into the Eurasian plate, building stress in the Himalayan region. When the stress releases suddenly, an earthquake occurs.

2. **Building design matters.** Flexible foundations and reinforced structures can sway with the ground shaking instead of collapsing.

**Practice questions**

1. Earthquakes are measured on the: (A) Richter scale (B) Celsius scale (C) Beaufort scale (D) pH scale
   - *Answer:* Richter scale. Magnitude is measured on the Richter scale.
2. Earthquakes are most common at: (A) Plate boundaries (B) City centres (C) Deserts (D) Lakes
   - *Answer:* Plate boundaries. Most earthquakes occur where tectonic plates meet.
3. What should you do during an earthquake?
   - *Answer:* Drop, cover under a sturdy table and hold on; stay away from windows and heavy objects.

## Chapter 16. Light

### 16.1 Reflection and Refraction

Light reflects from surfaces and bends when it passes from one medium to another. These two behaviours explain mirrors, lenses and optical illusions.

**Key points**

- Laws of reflection: angle of incidence equals angle of reflection, in the same plane.
- Refraction occurs because light changes speed in a new medium.
- Light bends towards the normal when entering a denser medium, away when leaving it.
- Plane mirrors produce virtual images; curved mirrors can produce real or virtual images.
- A prism splits white light into a spectrum by refraction.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why a straw looks bent in water.** Light from the submerged part bends at the water surface before reaching the eye, so the straw appears displaced — refraction.

2. **The periscope.** Two plane mirrors at 45° reflect light twice, allowing a submarine to see above the water surface.

**Practice questions**

1. Bending of light when it changes medium is: (A) Refraction (B) Reflection (C) Dispersion (D) Absorption
   - *Answer:* Refraction. Refraction is the change in direction due to a change in speed.
2. The image in a plane mirror is: (A) Virtual and erect (B) Real and inverted (C) Magnified (D) Not formed
   - *Answer:* Virtual and erect. Plane mirrors give upright virtual images.
3. State the two laws of reflection.
   - *Answer:* Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection; incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in the same plane.

### 16.2 Human Eye and Optical Instruments

The eye focuses light with a lens onto the retina, which sends signals to the brain. Lenses and mirrors extend the eye's reach in telescopes, microscopes and cameras.

**Key points**

- Light passes through the cornea; the iris controls pupil size; the lens focuses; the retina senses light.
- Myopia (short-sightedness) is corrected with a concave lens; hypermetropia with a convex lens.
- A convex lens converges light; a concave lens diverges it.
- A microscope magnifies tiny objects; a telescope magnifies distant objects.
- Cameras and eyes both form an inverted image that the brain or film interprets.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why reading glasses magnify.** A convex lens bends light rays so they converge more strongly, helping the eye focus nearby print. That is why reading glasses use convex lenses.

2. **Why a camera image is inverted.** The lens forms a real image on the sensor with rays crossing at the lens, so the image is upside down; software flips it for display.

**Practice questions**

1. Myopia is corrected by a: (A) Concave lens (B) Convex lens (C) Plane mirror (D) Prism
   - *Answer:* Concave lens. A concave lens diverges light so the image falls on the retina.
2. The structure that controls the amount of light entering the eye is the: (A) Iris (B) Retina (C) Cornea (D) Optic nerve
   - *Answer:* Iris. The iris adjusts the size of the pupil.
3. What is the function of the retina?
   - *Answer:* It contains light-sensitive cells that convert light into electrical signals sent to the brain.

## Chapter 17. Stars and the Solar System

### 17.1 The Solar System

The Sun and the objects orbiting it — planets, moons, asteroids, comets and meteors — make up the solar system.

**Key points**

- Eight planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.
- The Sun is a star; planets shine by reflected light.
- Inner planets are rocky; outer planets are giant gas and ice planets.
- Asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter; comets travel in long orbits.
- Earth is the only known planet with life, thanks to liquid water and a suitable atmosphere.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why planets do not twinkle.** Stars are point sources and their light bends irregularly in the atmosphere, so they twinkle. Planets are close and appear as tiny discs, so the shimmering averages out.

2. **Saturn's rings.** Rings are made of countless particles of ice and rock orbiting Saturn, lit by the Sun.

**Practice questions**

1. Which planet is closest to the Sun? (A) Mercury (B) Venus (C) Earth (D) Mars
   - *Answer:* Mercury. Mercury is the innermost planet.
2. The asteroid belt lies between: (A) Mars and Jupiter (B) Earth and Mars (C) Jupiter and Saturn (D) Sun and Mercury
   - *Answer:* Mars and Jupiter. Most asteroids orbit in the gap between Mars and Jupiter.
3. Why is Earth the only planet known to support life?
   - *Answer:* It has liquid water, a suitable atmosphere and a moderate temperature range.

### 17.2 Stars, Constellations and the Universe

Stars are self-luminous balls of gas. The Sun is our nearest star. Distant stars are grouped into constellations that help navigation.

**Key points**

- A light year is the distance light travels in one year.
- The Moon is Earth's natural satellite; it revolves around Earth in about 27 days.
- Constellations such as Ursa Major (Great Bear) and Orion help locate stars.
- Phases of the Moon arise from the changing angle of sunlight on its face.
- The Universe contains billions of galaxies, each with billions of stars.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why the Moon shows phases.** As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different fractions of its sunlit half: new moon, crescent, half, gibbous and full moon.

2. **Using the Pole Star.** The Pole Star appears nearly fixed in the northern sky and lies close to the north direction, so travelers have used it for navigation.

**Practice questions**

1. A light year is a unit of: (A) Distance (B) Time (C) Brightness (D) Temperature
   - *Answer:* Distance. It is the distance light travels in one year.
2. Our nearest star is: (A) The Sun (B) Sirius (C) Polaris (D) Alpha Centauri
   - *Answer:* The Sun. The Sun is the closest star to Earth.
3. What is a constellation? Give one example.
   - *Answer:* A group of stars that appears to form a pattern; for example Ursa Major.

## Chapter 18. Pollution of Air and Water

### 18.1 Air Pollution

Air pollution is the contamination of air by harmful substances from vehicles, industries and domestic activities. It harms health, plants and buildings.

**Key points**

- Pollutants include carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, particulate matter and smoke.
- Sources: vehicles, factories, burning of fossil fuels and biomass, dust and construction.
- Effects: respiratory disease, acid rain, smog, greenhouse effect and ozone depletion.
- Greenhouse gases trap heat; CFCs destroy ozone in the stratosphere.
- Control: cleaner fuels, catalytic converters, filters, afforestation and public transport.

**Worked examples**

1. **The Taj Mahal and acid rain.** Sulphur dioxide from nearby industries reacts with rain to form acid, which attacks the marble of the Taj Mahal. Cleaner fuels and restrictions on polluting factories help protect it.

2. **Why smog forms over cities.** Pollutants and moisture combine under still, cool conditions to form smog, reducing visibility and irritating lungs.

**Practice questions**

1. Which gas causes acid rain? (A) Sulphur dioxide (B) Oxygen (C) Nitrogen (D) Helium
   - *Answer:* Sulphur dioxide. Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides dissolve in rain to form acids.
2. CFCs harm the: (A) Ozone layer (B) Soil only (C) Water only (D) Rocks
   - *Answer:* Ozone layer. Chlorofluorocarbons break down ozone molecules.
3. Name three sources of air pollution.
   - *Answer:* Vehicles, factories and burning of fossil fuels (also dust and biomass burning).

### 18.2 Water Pollution and Conservation

Water pollution is the contamination of water by domestic sewage, industrial waste, agricultural chemicals and plastic. Clean water is a limited resource.

**Key points**

- Sources: sewage, industrial effluents, fertilisers and pesticides, oil spills, plastic waste.
- Effects: waterborne diseases, death of aquatic life, eutrophication and poisoned groundwater.
- Treatment removes solids, consumes organic matter with bacteria and disinfects the water.
- Reduce, reuse and recycle; safe disposal of chemicals.
- Water harvesting and watershed management protect supplies.

**Worked examples**

1. **Eutrophication.** Excess fertiliser runs into a lake and fuels algal growth. When algae die, bacteria decompose them and use up oxygen, suffocating fish.

2. **Oil spills.** Oil forms a film on the sea surface, blocking oxygen and light and coating birds' feathers, so they cannot float or keep warm.

**Practice questions**

1. Eutrophication is caused by: (A) Excess nutrients from fertilisers (B) Oil spills only (C) Plastic only (D) Salt only
   - *Answer:* Excess nutrients from fertilisers. Nutrient enrichment causes excessive algal growth.
2. Which is a waterborne disease? (A) Cholera (B) Rickets (C) Scurvy (D) Night blindness
   - *Answer:* Cholera. Cholera spreads through contaminated water.
3. State two ways to reduce water pollution.
   - *Answer:* Treat sewage before release and avoid dumping industrial chemicals and plastic into water bodies.
