## Chapter 1. Units and Measurements

### 1.1 SI Units, Dimensions and Errors

Physical quantities are measured in standard units. Dimensions express how a quantity depends on the base quantities, and every measurement has uncertainty.

**Key points**

- SI base units: metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole and candela.
- Derived units combine base units, such as N = kg·m/s<sup>2</sup> and J = kg·m<sup>2</sup>/s<sup>2</sup>.
- Dimensional formula of velocity is [M<sup>0</sup>L<sup>1</sup>T<sup>−1</sup>]; force is [M L T<sup>−2</sup>].
- Significant figures reflect the precision of a measurement.
- Errors are absolute, relative or percentage; errors add in quadrature for independent measurements.

**Worked examples**

1. **Dimensional check.** For s = ut + ½at<sup>2</sup>, each term must have dimensions of length: [L] = [L T<sup>−1</sup>][T] = [L T<sup>−2</sup>][T<sup>2</sup>]. The equation is dimensionally consistent.

2. **Percentage error.** A length of 25.0 cm measured with ±0.1 cm uncertainty has percentage error (0.1/25.0) × 100 = 0.4%.

**Practice questions**

1. The SI unit of force is the: (A) Newton (B) Joule (C) Watt (D) Pascal
   - *Answer:* Newton. 1 N = 1 kg·m/s<sup>2</sup>.
2. The dimensional formula of energy is: (A) [ML<sup>2</sup>T<sup>−2</sup>] (B) [MLT<sup>−2</sup>] (C) [ML<sup>2</sup>T<sup>−1</sup>] (D) [ML<sup>−1</sup>T<sup>−2</sup>]
   - *Answer:* [ML<sup>2</sup>T<sup>−2</sup>]. Energy = force × distance, so [ML<sup>2</sup>T<sup>−2</sup>].
3. Convert 72 km/h to m/s.
   - *Answer:* 72 × 5/18 = 20 m/s.

### 1.2 Dimensional Analysis and Its Uses

Dimensional analysis checks equations, derives relations and converts units, but cannot determine dimensionless constants.

**Key points**

- Only quantities with the same dimensions can be added or equated.
- Dimensions of both sides of a valid equation must match.
- Dimensional analysis can predict the form of a physical relation up to a dimensionless constant.
- It cannot handle trigonometric, exponential or logarithmic functions directly.
- Unit conversion uses the ratio of old to new units.

**Worked examples**

1. **Period of a pendulum.** Assuming T depends on length l and g, dimensional analysis gives T ∝ √(l/g). The missing factor 2π cannot be found this way.

2. **Checking the wave equation.** v = fλ: [L T<sup>−1</sup>] = [T<sup>−1</sup>][L], which is consistent.

**Practice questions**

1. Dimensional analysis cannot determine: (A) Dimensionless constants (B) Unit consistency (C) Dependence on variables (D) Dimensional formulas
   - *Answer:* Dimensionless constants. Dimensionless numerical factors are invisible to it.
2. Which of these is dimensionless? (A) Strain (B) Force (C) Velocity (D) Pressure
   - *Answer:* Strain. Strain is a ratio of lengths, so it has no units.
3. Use dimensional analysis to find the dimensions of pressure.
   - *Answer:* Pressure = force/area = [MLT<sup>−2</sup>]/[L<sup>2</sup>] = [M L<sup>−1</sup> T<sup>−2</sup>].

## Chapter 2. Motion in a Straight Line

### 2.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration

Kinematics describes motion without asking about its cause. Position, displacement, velocity and acceleration are the basic variables.

**Key points**

- Displacement is the change in position; distance is the path length travelled.
- Average velocity = displacement ÷ time; instantaneous velocity is the limit as time tends to zero.
- Acceleration = rate of change of velocity.
- For uniform acceleration: v = u + at, s = ut + ½at<sup>2</sup> and v<sup>2</sup> = u<sup>2</sup> + 2as.
- The slope of a position-time graph gives velocity; the area under a velocity-time graph gives displacement.

**Worked examples**

1. **Free fall.** A ball dropped from rest falls ½gt<sup>2</sup>. In 2 s, with g = 9.8 m/s<sup>2</sup>, it falls 19.6 m and reaches 19.6 m/s.

2. **Relative motion.** Two trains moving at 60 km/h in opposite directions approach each other at 120 km/h relative to each other.

**Practice questions**

1. The area under a velocity-time graph gives: (A) Displacement (B) Acceleration (C) Speed (D) Force
   - *Answer:* Displacement. Integrating velocity over time gives displacement.
2. A body thrown upward reaches its highest point when its velocity is: (A) Zero (B) Maximum (C) Equal to g (D) Negative
   - *Answer:* Zero. At the top the vertical velocity is momentarily zero.
3. A car accelerates from rest at 2 m/s<sup>2</sup> for 5 s. Find its final velocity and distance.
   - *Answer:* v = at = 10 m/s; s = ½at<sup>2</sup> = ½ × 2 × 25 = 25 m.

### 2.2 Graphs and Relative Velocity

Graphical analysis turns motion into geometry. Relative velocity lets us describe motion from different frames.

**Key points**

- A straight position-time line means uniform velocity; a curve means acceleration.
- Velocity-time graphs: horizontal line = constant velocity; sloped line = constant acceleration.
- The relative velocity of A with respect to B is v<sub>AB</sub> = v<sub>A</sub> − v<sub>B</sub>.
- Deceleration appears as a negative slope on a velocity-time graph.
- The area between the velocity-time curve and the time axis (with sign) gives displacement.

**Worked examples**

1. **Graph of a ball thrown up.** Velocity decreases linearly with time, becomes zero at the top, then becomes negative as the ball falls back.

2. **River crossing.** A boat heading straight across a flowing river drifts downstream; its resultant velocity is the vector sum of boat and current velocities.

**Practice questions**

1. A negative acceleration with positive velocity means the body is: (A) Slowing down (B) Speeding up (C) At rest (D) Turning
   - *Answer:* Slowing down. Velocity and acceleration in opposite directions reduce speed.
2. Relative velocity of A with respect to B is: (A) v<sub>A</sub> − v<sub>B</sub> (B) v<sub>A</sub> + v<sub>B</sub> (C) v<sub>B</sub> − v<sub>A</sub> (D) v<sub>A</sub> × v<sub>B</sub>
   - *Answer:* v<sub>A</sub> − v<sub>B</sub>. It is the velocity of A measured in B's frame.
3. A stone is dropped from a 45 m cliff. How long does it take to land (g = 10 m/s<sup>2</sup>)?
   - *Answer:* s = ½gt<sup>2</sup> gives 45 = 5t<sup>2</sup>, so t = 3 s.

## Chapter 3. Motion in a Plane

### 3.1 Vectors and Projectile Motion

Motion in two dimensions uses vectors. A projectile is an object moving under gravity with a constant horizontal velocity.

**Key points**

- Vectors add by the triangle or parallelogram law; components resolve a vector along axes.
- Projectile motion: horizontal motion is uniform, vertical motion is uniformly accelerated.
- Time of flight T = 2u sin θ / g; range R = u<sup>2</sup> sin 2θ / g; maximum height H = u<sup>2</sup> sin<sup>2</sup>θ / 2g.
- Maximum range occurs at 45° for a given speed.
- The horizontal velocity stays constant if air resistance is ignored.

**Worked examples**

1. **Football kick.** A ball kicked at 20 m/s at 30° has range R = 400 × sin 60° / 9.8 ≈ 35.3 m and time of flight T = 2 × 20 × 0.5 / 9.8 ≈ 2.04 s.

2. **Why 45° gives maximum range.** sin 2θ is maximum when 2θ = 90°, that is θ = 45°.

**Practice questions**

1. The horizontal acceleration of a projectile (ignoring air resistance) is: (A) Zero (B) g (C) g sin θ (D) g cos θ
   - *Answer:* Zero. Only gravity acts, vertically.
2. Maximum range for a given launch speed occurs at: (A) 45° (B) 30° (C) 60° (D) 90°
   - *Answer:* 45°. R ∝ sin 2θ, maximum at θ = 45°.
3. A ball is thrown at 10 m/s at 45°. Find its range (g = 10 m/s<sup>2</sup>).
   - *Answer:* R = u<sup>2</sup> sin 2θ / g = 100 × 1 / 10 = 10 m.

### 3.2 Uniform Circular Motion

In uniform circular motion the speed is constant but velocity changes because direction changes, producing centripetal acceleration.

**Key points**

- Angular velocity ω = 2π/T = v/r.
- Centripetal acceleration a<sub>c</sub> = v<sup>2</sup>/r = ω<sup>2</sup>r, directed toward the centre.
- Centripetal force F = mv<sup>2</sup>/r.
- Linear speed v = ωr relates the linear and angular descriptions.
- Centrifugal force is a pseudo-force experienced in the rotating frame.

**Worked examples**

1. **Car on a curve.** A 1000 kg car at 20 m/s on a 50 m radius curve needs F = mv<sup>2</sup>/r = 1000 × 400 / 50 = 8000 N of friction toward the centre.

2. **Why cyclists lean inward.** Leaning aligns the resultant of gravity and friction through the centre, preventing skidding.

**Practice questions**

1. Centripetal acceleration is directed: (A) Toward the centre (B) Away from the centre (C) Along velocity (D) Opposite velocity
   - *Answer:* Toward the centre. It always points to the centre of the circle.
2. If speed doubles in the same circle, centripetal force becomes: (A) Four times (B) Twice (C) Half (D) Same
   - *Answer:* Four times. F ∝ v<sup>2</sup>.
3. Find the angular velocity of a point on the equator (radius 6400 km) due to Earth's rotation.
   - *Answer:* T = 86400 s, ω = 2π/T ≈ 7.27 × 10<sup>−5</sup> rad/s.

## Chapter 4. Laws of Motion

### 4.1 Newton's Laws and Common Forces

Newton's three laws explain motion in terms of forces. Friction, tension, normal force and spring force are the forces that appear most often.

**Key points**

- First law: no net force means no change in velocity (inertia).
- Second law: F<sub>net</sub> = ma, or F = dp/dt.
- Third law: action and reaction are equal, opposite and act on different bodies.
- Friction f ≤ μN; static friction adjusts up to a maximum.
- Tension is the same throughout a light, frictionless string.

**Worked examples**

1. **Elevator physics.** A person of mass m in an elevator accelerating upward experiences N = m(g + a); downward acceleration gives N = m(g − a).

2. **Block on an incline.** On a frictionless incline at angle θ, the acceleration is g sin θ regardless of mass.

**Practice questions**

1. The normal force on a body on a horizontal floor is: (A) mg (B) ma (C) m(g + a) always (D) m(g − a) always
   - *Answer:* mg. When not accelerating vertically, N balances weight.
2. A body on a circular path at constant speed has: (A) Changing velocity (B) Constant velocity (C) Zero acceleration (D) No force
   - *Answer:* Changing velocity. Direction of velocity changes, so velocity is not constant.
3. Why do we require seat belts even when a car moves at constant speed?
   - *Answer:* If the car stops suddenly, inertia carries the passenger forward; the belt provides the stopping force.

### 4.2 Circular Motion and Banking

Turning requires a centripetal force. Roads are banked to help vehicles turn without relying entirely on friction.

**Key points**

- Friction supplies centripetal force on a flat road: μmg ≥ mv<sup>2</sup>/r, so v ≤ √(μrg).
- On a banked road, the horizontal component of the normal force supplies centripetal force.
- Optimum banking: tan θ = v<sup>2</sup>/(rg).
- Banking reduces the friction needed, which is why racing tracks and highways are banked.
- A conical pendulum and a body in a vertical circle are classic circular-motion systems.

**Worked examples**

1. **Safe speed on a curve.** With μ = 0.4 and r = 40 m, the maximum safe speed is v = √(0.4 × 40 × 9.8) ≈ 12.5 m/s ≈ 45 km/h.

2. **Banking angle.** For v = 20 m/s and r = 100 m, tan θ = 400/(100 × 9.8) ≈ 0.41, so θ ≈ 22°.

**Practice questions**

1. Banking of roads helps to: (A) Provide centripetal force with less friction (B) Reduce gravity (C) Increase mass (D) Stop the car
   - *Answer:* Provide centripetal force with less friction. The normal force component acts toward the centre.
2. Maximum safe speed on a flat road varies as: (A) √r (B) r (C) r<sup>2</sup> (D) 1/r
   - *Answer:* √r. v ∝ √(μrg).
3. Why is a cyclist able to negotiate a curve by leaning?
   - *Answer:* Leaning shifts the reaction force so its horizontal component provides the centripetal force.

## Chapter 5. Work, Energy and Power

### 5.1 Work and Energy Theorem

Work is the transfer of energy by a force. The work-energy theorem links net work with the change in kinetic energy.

**Key points**

- W = F·s = Fs cos θ; work is a scalar.
- Work is positive when force and displacement are in the same direction, negative when opposite.
- Work-energy theorem: W<sub>net</sub> = ΔKE.
- Kinetic energy KE = ½mv<sup>2</sup>; potential energy for a spring is ½kx<sup>2</sup>.
- Gravity is a conservative force; friction is non-conservative.

**Worked examples**

1. **Work by friction.** A 5 kg box sliding 2 m against 10 N friction loses 20 J of mechanical energy, which becomes heat.

2. **Spring compression.** Compressing a spring of k = 200 N/m by 0.1 m stores ½ × 200 × 0.01 = 1 J of energy.

**Practice questions**

1. Work done is zero when: (A) Force is perpendicular to displacement (B) Force is large (C) Displacement is large (D) Speed is constant
   - *Answer:* Force is perpendicular to displacement. cos 90° = 0, so W = 0.
2. The unit of energy is: (A) Joule (B) Newton (C) Watt (D) Pascal
   - *Answer:* Joule. Energy is measured in joules.
3. A force of 20 N moves a box 4 m in the direction of the force. Find the work done.
   - *Answer:* W = Fs cos 0° = 20 × 4 = 80 J.

### 5.2 Power and Conservation of Energy

Power is the rate of doing work. Energy is conserved but may be converted into less useful forms.

**Key points**

- Power P = W/t = Fv.
- 1 horsepower ≈ 746 W; 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10<sup>6</sup> J.
- Conservation of mechanical energy holds when only conservative forces act.
- Efficiency = useful output energy ÷ total input energy × 100%.
- Collisions conserve momentum; elastic collisions also conserve kinetic energy.

**Worked examples**

1. **Climbing stairs.** A 60 kg person climbing 5 m in 10 s does 60 × 9.8 × 5 = 2940 J, giving a power of 294 W.

2. **Elastic collision.** In an elastic collision between equal masses, one at rest, the moving ball stops and the other moves off with the original velocity.

**Practice questions**

1. Power is: (A) Rate of doing work (B) Force times time (C) Work times time (D) Change in momentum
   - *Answer:* Rate of doing work. Power = work ÷ time.
2. In a perfectly inelastic collision: (A) Kinetic energy is not conserved (B) Momentum is not conserved (C) Mass changes (D) Energy increases
   - *Answer:* Kinetic energy is not conserved. The bodies stick together and some KE becomes heat or deformation.
3. A pump lifts 500 kg of water 10 m in 20 s. Find its power (g = 10 m/s<sup>2</sup>).
   - *Answer:* Work = mgh = 500 × 10 × 10 = 50,000 J; P = 50,000/20 = 2500 W.

## Chapter 6. System of Particles and Rotational Motion

### 6.1 Centre of Mass and Moment of Inertia

Extended bodies are treated as systems of particles. The centre of mass moves as if all forces acted there, and rotational inertia depends on mass distribution.

**Key points**

- The centre of mass is the mass-weighted average position of a system.
- For two masses, the centre of mass divides the distance in inverse ratio to masses.
- Torque τ = r × F; it produces angular acceleration.
- Moment of inertia I = Σmr<sup>2</sup>; larger I means more resistance to rotation.
- Angular momentum L = Iω is conserved when net torque is zero.

**Worked examples**

1. **Balancing on a finger.** A ruler balances at its centre of mass. Adding a heavy mass at one end shifts the centre of mass toward that end.

2. **Figure skater spin.** Pulling arms in reduces I, so ω increases to conserve angular momentum, and the skater spins faster.

**Practice questions**

1. Torque depends on: (A) Force and perpendicular distance (B) Force only (C) Mass only (D) Velocity only
   - *Answer:* Force and perpendicular distance. τ = rF sin θ.
2. When a skater pulls her arms in, her angular velocity: (A) Increases (B) Decreases (C) Stays same (D) Becomes zero
   - *Answer:* Increases. Reducing I increases ω to keep L constant.
3. Why is it easier to open a door by pushing near the handle than near the hinge?
   - *Answer:* The handle is farther from the hinge, so the same force produces a larger torque.

### 6.2 Rotational Kinematics and Rolling

Rotational motion has analogues of every linear quantity. Rolling combines translation and rotation.

**Key points**

- ω = ω<sub>0</sub> + αt; θ = ω<sub>0</sub>t + ½αt<sup>2</sup>; ω<sup>2</sup> = ω<sub>0</sub><sup>2</sup> + 2αθ.
- Rotational KE = ½Iω<sup>2</sup>.
- Rolling without slipping: v = ωR.
- Total kinetic energy of a rolling body = ½mv<sup>2</sup> + ½Iω<sup>2</sup>.
- The rolling body with the smaller I/mR<sup>2</sup> reaches the bottom of an incline first.

**Worked examples**

1. **Solid sphere versus hoop.** A solid sphere (I = 2/5 mR<sup>2</sup>) rolls down faster than a hoop (I = mR<sup>2</sup>) because a larger fraction of its energy goes into translation.

2. **Rotational energy.** A disc with I = 0.5 kg·m<sup>2</sup> spinning at 10 rad/s has KE = ½ × 0.5 × 100 = 25 J.

**Practice questions**

1. Rolling without slipping means: (A) v = ωR (B) v = ω (C) v = R/ω (D) v = 0
   - *Answer:* v = ωR. The contact point is instantaneously at rest.
2. Angular momentum is conserved when: (A) Net torque is zero (B) Mass is zero (C) Force is zero (D) Velocity is zero
   - *Answer:* Net torque is zero. Zero torque means constant L.
3. Calculate the rotational KE of a wheel with I = 2 kg·m<sup>2</sup> spinning at 5 rad/s.
   - *Answer:* KE = ½Iω<sup>2</sup> = ½ × 2 × 25 = 25 J.

## Chapter 7. Gravitation

### 7.1 Gravitational Field, Potential and Orbits

Newton's law of gravitation explains falling bodies, orbits and the motion of planets. Field strength and potential describe gravity at a point.

**Key points**

- F = Gm<sub>1</sub>m<sub>2</sub>/r<sup>2</sup>; gravitational field g = GM/r<sup>2</sup>.
- Gravitational potential energy U = −GMm/r; gravitational potential V = −GM/r.
- Orbital velocity v<sub>o</sub> = √(GM/r); escape velocity v<sub>e</sub> = √(2GM/R).
- Kepler's third law: T<sup>2</sup> ∝ a<sup>3</sup> for orbits around the same central body.
- Inside a uniform spherical shell the gravitational field is zero.

**Worked examples**

1. **Satellite speed.** For a satellite 400 km above Earth (r ≈ 6.77 × 10<sup>6</sup> m) with GM = 3.986 × 10<sup>14</sup> m<sup>3</sup>/s<sup>2</sup>, v = √(GM/r) ≈ 7.67 km/s.

2. **Escape velocity.** v<sub>e</sub> = √(2gR) = √(2 × 9.8 × 6.4 × 10<sup>6</sup>) ≈ 11.2 km/s.

**Practice questions**

1. The escape velocity from Earth is: (A) ≈ 11.2 km/s (B) ≈ 7.9 km/s (C) ≈ 3 × 10<sup>8</sup> m/s (D) ≈ 9.8 m/s
   - *Answer:* ≈ 11.2 km/s. It is about 11.2 km/s.
2. Gravitational potential energy of a satellite in orbit is: (A) Negative (B) Positive (C) Zero (D) Undefined
   - *Answer:* Negative. It is negative because the force is attractive with zero at infinity.
3. Why is g smaller at the equator than at the poles?
   - *Answer:* Earth bulges at the equator (larger radius) and rotation reduces effective gravity there.

### 7.2 Satellites and Weightlessness

A satellite is in continual free fall around Earth. Its horizontal speed keeps it from hitting the ground.

**Key points**

- Orbital period T = 2π√(r<sup>3</sup>/GM).
- Geostationary satellites orbit at about 36,000 km with a period of 24 h.
- Inside an orbiting satellite everything is in free fall, giving apparent weightlessness.
- Total energy of a satellite in orbit is E = −GMm/(2r).
- Rockets need large fuel mass; the rocket equation relates speed gain to exhaust speed and mass ratio.

**Worked examples**

1. **Geostationary orbit.** A satellite at about 36,000 km above the equator takes one day to orbit, so it stays over the same point — ideal for communication and weather.

2. **Weightlessness in orbit.** Astronauts float not because gravity is absent but because they and the spacecraft fall together.

**Practice questions**

1. A geostationary satellite has an orbital period of: (A) 24 hours (B) 12 hours (C) 1 month (D) 1 year
   - *Answer:* 24 hours. It matches Earth's rotation period.
2. Weightlessness in orbit is due to: (A) Free fall (B) Zero gravity (C) No mass (D) No atmosphere
   - *Answer:* Free fall. Gravity provides the centripetal force; the astronaut and cabin fall together.
3. State Kepler's third law.
   - *Answer:* The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis.

## Chapter 8. Mechanical Properties of Solids

### 8.1 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli

Solids deform under force. Stress is force per area and strain is fractional deformation; their ratio is a modulus.

**Key points**

- Stress = force ÷ area; strain = change in dimension ÷ original dimension.
- Young's modulus Y = longitudinal stress ÷ longitudinal strain.
- Bulk modulus B = pressure ÷ fractional volume change.
- Shear modulus G = shear stress ÷ shear strain.
- Steel has a much larger Young's modulus than rubber, so it is stiffer.

**Worked examples**

1. **Stretching a wire.** A steel wire of length 2 m and area 10<sup>−4</sup> m<sup>2</sup> under 100 N stretches by ΔL = FL/(AY). With Y = 2 × 10<sup>11</sup> N/m<sup>2</sup>, ΔL = 100 × 2/(10<sup>−4</sup> × 2 × 10<sup>11</sup>) = 10<sup>−5</sup> m.

2. **Bulk compression.** Water has a high bulk modulus, so it resists compression strongly — which is why it is used in hydraulic systems.

**Practice questions**

1. Young's modulus is the ratio of: (A) Longitudinal stress to longitudinal strain (B) Shear to volume (C) Force to area (D) Pressure to density
   - *Answer:* Longitudinal stress to longitudinal strain. It measures stiffness along the length.
2. A material with a larger Young's modulus is: (A) Stiffer (B) Softer (C) More ductile (D) Less elastic
   - *Answer:* Stiffer. Higher Y means less strain for the same stress.
3. Define strain and give its unit.
   - *Answer:* Strain is the ratio of change in dimension to original dimension; it has no unit.

### 8.2 Stress-Strain Curves and Elasticity in Practice

A stress-strain curve shows the elastic limit, yield point and breaking point of a material. These determine safe design.

**Key points**

- Hooke's law holds in the linear region: stress ∝ strain.
- Beyond the elastic limit, deformation is permanent (plastic).
- Ductile materials stretch a lot before breaking (copper); brittle materials break early (glass, cast iron).
- Elastic hysteresis is energy lost in a loading-unloading cycle, useful in shock absorbers.
- Safety factors ensure structures stay well below breaking stress.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why bridges bend but don't break.** Design keeps stress far below the elastic limit so deformation is reversible; the safety factor covers unexpected loads.

2. **Elastomers.** Rubber can stretch several times its length and return, because its polymer chains uncoil and recoil. Its stress-strain curve is not linear.

**Practice questions**

1. Beyond the elastic limit, a material: (A) Deforms permanently (B) Returns to original shape (C) Breaks immediately (D) Becomes weightless
   - *Answer:* Deforms permanently. Plastic deformation is permanent.
2. Which material is brittle? (A) Glass (B) Copper (C) Rubber (D) Steel wire
   - *Answer:* Glass. Glass fractures with little plastic deformation.
3. Why are steel rods used in concrete?
   - *Answer:* Steel has high tensile strength and Young's modulus, complementing concrete which is strong in compression.

## Chapter 9. Mechanical Properties of Fluids

### 9.1 Pressure, Pascal's Law and Buoyancy

Fluids exert pressure in all directions. Pascal's law and Archimedes' principle underlie hydraulics and floating.

**Key points**

- Pressure at depth h: P = P<sub>0</sub> + ρgh.
- Pascal's law: pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally in all directions.
- Buoyant force equals the weight of fluid displaced (Archimedes' principle).
- A body floats if its average density is less than the fluid's.
- Hydraulic machines use Pascal's law to multiply force.

**Worked examples**

1. **Hydraulic lift.** A small force on a small piston creates pressure transmitted to a larger piston, producing a larger force: F<sub>2</sub> = F<sub>1</sub> A<sub>2</sub>/A<sub>1</sub>.

2. **Iceberg floating.** Ice density is about 0.92 of seawater, so roughly 92% of an iceberg lies below the surface.

**Practice questions**

1. Pressure at depth in a liquid depends on: (A) Depth and density (B) Shape of container (C) Total volume (D) Surface area
   - *Answer:* Depth and density. P = ρgh, so depth and density matter.
2. Archimedes' principle gives the buoyant force as: (A) Weight of displaced fluid (B) Weight of the body (C) Volume of the body (D) Density of the body
   - *Answer:* Weight of displaced fluid. The upthrust equals the weight of the displaced fluid.
3. Why does a ship made of iron float?
   - *Answer:* Its hollow shape displaces water whose weight equals the ship's weight, so it floats.

### 9.2 Fluid Dynamics: Bernoulli and Viscosity

Moving fluids obey continuity and Bernoulli's equation. Real fluids have viscosity that resists flow.

**Key points**

- Equation of continuity: A<sub>1</sub>v<sub>1</sub> = A<sub>2</sub>v<sub>2</sub> for incompressible flow.
- Bernoulli's theorem: P + ½ρv<sup>2</sup> + ρgh = constant along a streamline.
- Faster flow means lower pressure, which explains lift and the working of sprayers.
- Viscous force F = ηA dv/dx; η is the coefficient of viscosity.
- Terminal velocity is reached when viscous drag plus buoyancy balances weight.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why aeroplanes fly.** Air moves faster over the curved upper wing, lowering pressure there, so the higher pressure below produces lift.

2. **Terminal velocity of a raindrop.** Drag increases with speed until it balances weight; the drop then falls at a constant terminal speed instead of accelerating.

**Practice questions**

1. Bernoulli's theorem is based on: (A) Conservation of energy (B) Conservation of mass (C) Conservation of momentum (D) Newton's third law
   - *Answer:* Conservation of energy. It follows from energy conservation in an ideal fluid.
2. At a narrower section of a pipe, the fluid speed: (A) Increases (B) Decreases (C) Stays the same (D) Becomes zero
   - *Answer:* Increases. By continuity, smaller area means higher speed.
3. State the equation of continuity and explain it.
   - *Answer:* A<sub>1</sub>v<sub>1</sub> = A<sub>2</sub>v<sub>2</sub>; the mass flow rate of an incompressible fluid is constant, so speed rises where the pipe narrows.

## Chapter 10. Thermal Properties of Matter

### 10.1 Temperature, Heat and Expansion

Temperature measures hotness; heat is energy transferred due to temperature difference. Materials expand with temperature.

**Key points**

- Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin scales are related; K = °C + 273.15.
- Linear expansion ΔL = LαΔT; area expansion uses 2α; volume expansion uses 3α.
- Specific heat capacity c: Q = mcΔT.
- Latent heat L: Q = mL for a change of state at constant temperature.
- Water has a very high specific heat, which stabilises climate.

**Worked examples**

1. **Heating water.** To heat 2 kg of water by 30 °C, with c = 4200 J/kg·K: Q = 2 × 4200 × 30 = 252,000 J.

2. **Melting ice.** Melting 1 kg of ice at 0 °C takes Q = mL = 1 × 3.34 × 10<sup>5</sup> J = 334 kJ.

**Practice questions**

1. The SI unit of specific heat capacity is: (A) J/kg·K (B) J/K (C) W/m·K (D) J/mol
   - *Answer:* J/kg·K. It is the energy per kilogram per kelvin.
2. During melting, the temperature: (A) Remains constant (B) Rises (C) Falls (D) Doubles
   - *Answer:* Remains constant. Latent heat changes state without changing temperature.
3. How much heat is needed to raise 0.5 kg of water by 10 °C (c = 4200 J/kg·K)?
   - *Answer:* Q = mcΔT = 0.5 × 4200 × 10 = 21,000 J.

### 10.2 Heat Transfer and Newton's Law of Cooling

Heat moves by conduction, convection and radiation. Cooling rates depend on temperature difference and surface.

**Key points**

- Conduction rate H = kA(T<sub>1</sub> − T<sub>2</sub>)/L.
- Convection transfers heat through fluid motion; radiation needs no medium.
- Stefan's law: power radiated ∝ T<sup>4</sup>; Wien's law: λ<sub>max</sub> ∝ 1/T.
- Newton's law of cooling: rate of cooling ∝ temperature difference for small differences.
- Black surfaces absorb and emit better than shiny ones.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why a thermos flask works.** Double walls with a vacuum prevent conduction and convection, and silvered surfaces reduce radiation, so drinks stay hot or cold for hours.

2. **Cooling of coffee.** The coffee cools fast when much hotter than the room and slows as it approaches room temperature, in line with Newton's law of cooling.

**Practice questions**

1. Heat transfer that needs no medium is: (A) Radiation (B) Conduction (C) Convection (D) Evaporation
   - *Answer:* Radiation. Radiation travels through a vacuum, like sunlight.
2. A good absorber is also a good: (A) Emitter (B) Insulator (C) Conductor (D) Reflector
   - *Answer:* Emitter. Kirchhoff's law links absorptivity and emissivity.
3. Why do we feel hot near a fire even in a cold room?
   - *Answer:* The fire radiates infrared energy that reaches us without needing a medium.

## Chapter 11. Thermodynamics

### 11.1 Zeroth and First Law

Thermodynamics describes energy changes in systems. The first law is the conservation of energy applied to heat and work.

**Key points**

- Zeroth law: two bodies in thermal equilibrium with a third are in equilibrium with each other — basis of temperature.
- First law: ΔU = Q − W, where W is work done by the system.
- Work done by a gas at constant pressure: W = PΔV.
- Internal energy is a state function; heat and work are path-dependent.
- Isothermal, adiabatic, isochoric and isobaric processes are the basic ones.

**Worked examples**

1. **Gas expansion.** A gas expanding at constant 10<sup>5</sup> Pa by 2 × 10<sup>−3</sup> m<sup>3</sup> does W = PΔV = 200 J of work.

2. **Isothermal compression.** At constant temperature, compressing a gas requires work, which is given out as heat, so ΔU = 0.

**Practice questions**

1. The first law of thermodynamics is: (A) ΔU = Q − W (B) ΔU = Q + W always (C) Q = ΔU × W (D) W = Q/T
   - *Answer:* ΔU = Q − W. Q is heat added and W is work done by the system.
2. In an adiabatic process: (A) Q = 0 (B) W = 0 (C) ΔU = 0 (D) T is constant
   - *Answer:* Q = 0. No heat is exchanged with the surroundings.
3. A gas absorbs 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work. Find ΔU.
   - *Answer:* ΔU = Q − W = 500 − 200 = 300 J.

### 11.2 Second Law, Heat Engines and Refrigerators

The second law limits the conversion of heat into work. Engines have a maximum possible efficiency.

**Key points**

- Kelvin-Planck statement: no engine converts all heat into work.
- Clausius statement: heat cannot flow from cold to hot without work.
- Carnot efficiency η = 1 − T<sub>c</sub>/T<sub>h</sub> (temperatures in kelvin).
- A heat engine takes Q<sub>h</sub> from the hot reservoir, does work W and rejects Q<sub>c</sub> to the cold reservoir.
- A refrigerator is a reversed heat engine with COP = Q<sub>c</sub>/W.

**Worked examples**

1. **Carnot engine.** Between 500 K and 300 K the Carnot efficiency is 1 − 300/500 = 40%. No real engine can exceed this.

2. **Why engines are inefficient.** Friction, incomplete combustion and heat losses all reduce efficiency below the Carnot limit.

**Practice questions**

1. Carnot efficiency depends on: (A) Source and sink temperatures (B) Fuel type (C) Engine size (D) Speed only
   - *Answer:* Source and sink temperatures. η = 1 − T<sub>c</sub>/T<sub>h</sub>.
2. The second law forbids: (A) Converting all heat into work in a cycle (B) Doing work (C) Heat transfer (D) Cooling
   - *Answer:* Converting all heat into work in a cycle. Some heat must be rejected to a colder reservoir.
3. An engine works between 600 K and 300 K. Find its maximum efficiency.
   - *Answer:* η = 1 − 300/600 = 0.5, so 50%.

## Chapter 12. Kinetic Theory

### 12.1 Molecular Picture of Gases

Kinetic theory explains gas behaviour by treating molecules as tiny particles in random motion.

**Key points**

- Pressure arises from molecular collisions with container walls.
- PV = (1/3) Nm(v<sub>rms</sub>)<sup>2</sup> links macroscopic and microscopic variables.
- Average kinetic energy per molecule = (3/2)kT.
- RMS speed v<sub>rms</sub> = √(3RT/M).
- Temperature is proportional to average molecular kinetic energy.

**Worked examples**

1. **RMS speed of nitrogen.** At 300 K, v<sub>rms</sub> = √(3 × 8.314 × 300/0.028) ≈ 517 m/s.

2. **Why balloons expand in heat.** Higher temperature increases molecular speed and pressure; at constant pressure the gas expands.

**Practice questions**

1. Average kinetic energy of a molecule depends only on: (A) Temperature (B) Pressure (C) Volume (D) Mass
   - *Answer:* Temperature. KE = (3/2)kT per molecule.
2. As temperature increases, RMS speed: (A) Increases (B) Decreases (C) Stays constant (D) Becomes zero
   - *Answer:* Increases. v<sub>rms</sub> ∝ √T.
3. Why does a gas exert pressure on the walls of its container?
   - *Answer:* Molecules collide with the walls and transfer momentum, producing a force per unit area.

### 12.2 Specific Heat of Gases and Mean Free Path

Gases have two specific heats, C<sub>p</sub> and C<sub>v</sub>. Molecules travel only short distances between collisions.

**Key points**

- C<sub>p</sub> − C<sub>v</sub> = R for an ideal gas; C<sub>p</sub> > C<sub>v</sub>.
- Monatomic gas: C<sub>v</sub> = (3/2)R, γ = 5/3; diatomic: C<sub>v</sub> = (5/2)R, γ = 7/5.
- Mean free path λ = 1/(√2 πnd<sup>2</sup>).
- Degrees of freedom determine the energy distribution — the equipartition theorem.
- Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour at high pressure and low temperature.

**Worked examples**

1. **Value of γ.** For a monatomic gas γ = 1.67, for a diatomic gas γ = 1.4. These values affect the speed of sound.

2. **Mean free path.** At standard conditions λ for air molecules is about 10<sup>−7</sup> m — tiny compared with the size of the container.

**Practice questions**

1. For an ideal gas, C<sub>p</sub> − C<sub>v</sub> equals: (A) R (B) 2R (C) R/2 (D) 0
   - *Answer:* R. This is Mayer's relation.
2. γ for a monatomic gas is: (A) 5/3 (B) 7/5 (C) 4/3 (D) 1
   - *Answer:* 5/3. C<sub>p</sub>/C<sub>v</sub> = (5/2)/(3/2) = 5/3.
3. Why does temperature stay constant during an isothermal process?
   - *Answer:* Heat added equals work done by the gas, so internal energy and hence temperature do not change.

## Chapter 13. Oscillations

### 13.1 Simple Harmonic Motion

In SHM the restoring force is proportional to displacement and directed toward the mean position. Displacement, velocity and acceleration vary sinusoidally.

**Key points**

- x = A sin(ωt + φ); v = Aω cos(ωt + φ); a = −ω<sup>2</sup>x.
- Angular frequency ω = 2π/T; maximum speed = Aω; maximum acceleration = Aω<sup>2</sup>.
- Force F = −kx, with ω = √(k/m).
- Total energy E = ½kA<sup>2</sup> is constant, exchanging between kinetic and potential.
- The period is independent of amplitude for small oscillations.

**Worked examples**

1. **Spring-mass system.** A 0.5 kg mass on a spring of k = 50 N/m oscillates with T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√0.01 ≈ 0.63 s.

2. **Pendulum.** A simple pendulum has T = 2π√(l/g); a 1 m pendulum has T ≈ 2.0 s.

**Practice questions**

1. The period of a simple pendulum depends on: (A) Length and g (B) Mass (C) Amplitude (D) Material
   - *Answer:* Length and g. T = 2π√(l/g).
2. At the mean position of SHM, the acceleration is: (A) Zero (B) Maximum (C) Equal to g (D) Negative
   - *Answer:* Zero. Restoring force is zero at the mean position.
3. A spring with k = 100 N/m carries a 1 kg mass. Find its period.
   - *Answer:* T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√(1/100) = 0.628 s.

### 13.2 Damped and Forced Oscillations, Resonance

Real oscillations lose energy (damping). Forced oscillations can build up dramatically at resonance.

**Key points**

- Damping force is proportional to velocity; amplitude decays exponentially.
- Forced oscillations occur when an external periodic force drives the system.
- Resonance occurs when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency.
- At resonance the amplitude is maximum; it can damage structures.
- Examples: musical instruments, tuning radios, soldiers breaking step on bridges.

**Worked examples**

1. **Soldiers on a bridge.** If marching frequency matches the bridge's natural frequency, resonant swaying can build up, so soldiers break step on bridges.

2. **Tuning a radio.** Adjusting the tuning circuit changes its natural frequency until it matches the station, giving resonant maximum response.

**Practice questions**

1. Resonance occurs when: (A) Driving frequency equals natural frequency (B) Amplitude is zero (C) Damping is maximum (D) Force is constant
   - *Answer:* Driving frequency equals natural frequency. At matching frequencies the amplitude peaks.
2. Damping ______ the amplitude of oscillation. (A) Reduces (B) Increases (C) Doubles (D) Keeps constant
   - *Answer:* Reduces. Energy is dissipated, so amplitude decays.
3. Why do soldiers break step while crossing a bridge?
   - *Answer:* To avoid resonance with the bridge's natural frequency, which could cause dangerous oscillations.

## Chapter 14. Waves

### 14.1 Wave Characteristics and Speed

Waves transfer energy without transferring matter. A wave has wavelength, frequency, amplitude and speed.

**Key points**

- v = fλ; the speed depends on the medium.
- Transverse waves have perpendicular particle displacement; longitudinal waves have parallel.
- Sound is longitudinal; light is transverse electromagnetic.
- Wave speed on a stretched string v = √(T/μ).
- The principle of superposition: displacements add algebraically.

**Worked examples**

1. **Speed of a wave on a string.** A string with tension 100 N and linear density 0.01 kg/m supports waves at v = √(100/0.01) = 100 m/s.

2. **Sound in air.** A 440 Hz note in air (v = 340 m/s) has wavelength λ = v/f ≈ 0.77 m.

**Practice questions**

1. The relation between speed, frequency and wavelength is: (A) v = fλ (B) v = f/λ (C) v = λ/f (D) f = vλ
   - *Answer:* v = fλ. Wave speed equals frequency times wavelength.
2. Sound waves in air are: (A) Longitudinal (B) Transverse (C) Electromagnetic (D) Standing
   - *Answer:* Longitudinal. Air particles vibrate along the direction of propagation.
3. A wave has frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 4 m. Find its speed.
   - *Answer:* v = fλ = 50 × 4 = 200 m/s.

### 14.2 Standing Waves, Beats and Doppler Effect

Superposition produces standing waves, beats and the Doppler shift. These explain musical notes and moving-source effects.

**Key points**

- Standing waves form nodes (no displacement) and antinodes (maximum displacement).
- On a string fixed at both ends, λ = 2L/n for the nth harmonic.
- Beats: beat frequency = |f<sub>1</sub> − f<sub>2</sub>|.
- Doppler effect: observed frequency rises when source and observer approach.
- An overtone is any resonant frequency above the fundamental.

**Worked examples**

1. **Guitar string.** A 0.65 m string fixed at both ends has fundamental wavelength 1.3 m; with v = 300 m/s the frequency is f = 300/1.3 ≈ 231 Hz.

2. **Ambulance siren.** The siren sounds higher in pitch as the ambulance approaches and lower as it passes, because of the Doppler shift.

**Practice questions**

1. A node in a standing wave has: (A) Zero displacement (B) Maximum displacement (C) Maximum speed (D) Maximum amplitude
   - *Answer:* Zero displacement. Nodes are points of no motion.
2. Beat frequency equals: (A) Difference of frequencies (B) Sum of frequencies (C) Product of frequencies (D) Half the difference
   - *Answer:* Difference of frequencies. Beats arise from the difference between two close frequencies.
3. Two tuning forks of 256 Hz and 260 Hz are sounded together. Find the beat frequency.
   - *Answer:* Beat frequency = |260 − 256| = 4 Hz.
