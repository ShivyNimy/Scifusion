## Chapter 1. Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants

### 1.1 Flower Structure and Pollination

Flowers contain male and female reproductive organs. Pollination transfers pollen and precedes fertilisation.

**Key points**

- The stamen (androecium) produces pollen in the anther; the pistil (gynoecium) contains ovules.
- Pollination may be self or cross, aided by wind, water, insects, birds or bats.
- Wind-pollinated flowers make light, dry pollen in large amounts.
- Insect-pollinated flowers offer nectar and have showy petals and scent.
- Self-incompatibility prevents self-fertilisation in many plants.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why wind-pollinated flowers lack bright petals.** They do not need to attract insects; resources go into producing large amounts of pollen instead.

2. **Fig and wasp mutualism.** Fig wasps pollinate fig flowers while laying eggs inside them — a tight mutualistic partnership.

**Practice questions**

1. Pollen grains are produced in the: (A) Anther (B) Ovary (C) Stigma (D) Filament
   - *Answer:* Anther. The anther contains pollen sacs.
2. Which agent pollinates colourful, scented flowers? (A) Insects (B) Wind (C) Water only (D) Gravity
   - *Answer:* Insects. Colour and scent attract insects.
3. Differentiate self-pollination and cross-pollination.
   - *Answer:* Self-pollination transfers pollen within the same flower or plant; cross-pollination transfers pollen between different plants.

### 1.2 Double Fertilisation and Seed Development

Angiosperms show double fertilisation: one sperm fertilises the egg and another fuses with polar nuclei, forming endosperm.

**Key points**

- After pollination, pollen germinates and a pollen tube carries male gametes to the ovule.
- One gamete fuses with the egg to form the zygote; the other with two polar nuclei to form triploid endosperm.
- The ovule becomes the seed and the ovary becomes the fruit.
- Parthenocarpy is fruit development without fertilisation (seedless banana).
- Apomixis produces seeds without fertilisation, preserving hybrid traits.

**Worked examples**

1. **Coconut structure.** The coconut's liquid endosperm nourishes the embryo during development, and the hard shell protects the seed.

2. **Seedless grapes.** Growers induce parthenocarpy with hormones so fruits develop without seeds.

**Practice questions**

1. Endosperm in angiosperms is: (A) Triploid (B) Diploid (C) Haploid (D) Tetraploid
   - *Answer:* Triploid. Double fertilisation produces triploid endosperm.
2. A fruit develops from the: (A) Ovary (B) Ovule (C) Stigma (D) Anther
   - *Answer:* Ovary. The ovary wall becomes the fruit.
3. What is double fertilisation?
   - *Answer:* Two male gametes fuse: one with the egg (zygote) and one with polar nuclei (endosperm).

## Chapter 2. Human Reproduction

### 2.1 Male and Female Reproductive Systems

Human reproduction involves specialised organs and gametes. Hormones control gamete production and the menstrual cycle.

**Key points**

- The testes produce sperm and testosterone; sperm mature in the epididymis.
- Ovaries produce eggs and oestrogen and progesterone.
- Fertilisation occurs in the fallopian tube.
- The menstrual cycle averages 28 days, with ovulation around day 14.
- The placenta exchanges nutrients, gases and wastes.

**Worked examples**

1. **Hormonal control of the cycle.** FSH stimulates follicle growth, LH triggers ovulation, and progesterone maintains the uterine lining.

2. **Why the placenta is essential.** It links the foetal and maternal circulations without mixing blood, allowing exchange of gases and nutrients.

**Practice questions**

1. Fertilisation in humans occurs in the: (A) Fallopian tube (B) Uterus (C) Ovary (D) Cervix
   - *Answer:* Fallopian tube. Sperm meets the egg in the fallopian tube.
2. Ovulation is triggered by a surge of: (A) LH (B) FSH (C) Progesterone (D) Prolactin
   - *Answer:* LH. The LH surge induces ovulation.
3. What is the function of the corpus luteum?
   - *Answer:* It secretes progesterone, which maintains the uterine lining for implantation.

### 2.2 Embryonic Development and Parturition

The zygote divides to form a blastocyst, which implants in the uterus. Development proceeds through trimesters until birth.

**Key points**

- Cleavage produces a blastocyst that implants in the endometrium.
- Gastrulation forms the three germ layers.
- Organogenesis builds organs from the germ layers.
- Oxytocin triggers uterine contractions during birth.
- Lactation is stimulated by prolactin.

**Worked examples**

1. **Identical twins.** A single zygote splits into two embryos, so identical twins share the same genetic material.

2. **Oxytocin in childbirth.** Oxytocin causes strong uterine contractions and is also used medically to induce labour.

**Practice questions**

1. The zygote implants in the: (A) Uterus (B) Fallopian tube (C) Ovary (D) Cervix
   - *Answer:* Uterus. Implantation occurs in the uterine wall.
2. Which hormone initiates labour contractions? (A) Oxytocin (B) Insulin (C) Thyroxine (D) Testosterone
   - *Answer:* Oxytocin. Oxytocin stimulates uterine muscle contraction.
3. Why are identical twins genetically the same?
   - *Answer:* They develop from a single fertilised egg that splits into two embryos.

## Chapter 3. Reproductive Health

### 3.1 Reproductive Health and Family Planning

Reproductive health means well-being in all aspects of reproduction. Family planning helps couples decide the number and timing of children.

**Key points**

- Reproductive health includes safe motherhood, contraception and prevention of STIs.
- Contraceptive methods include barrier, hormonal, IUD and surgical methods.
- Condoms also protect against sexually transmitted infections.
- Awareness and counselling are essential for reproductive health.
- Lactational amenorrhea is a natural, temporary method of birth control.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why condoms are dual-purpose.** They prevent pregnancy and reduce the transmission of infections such as HIV.

2. **IUD action.** Copper IUDs release copper ions that affect sperm motility and prevent fertilisation.

**Practice questions**

1. Which contraceptive method also prevents STIs? (A) Condoms (B) Oral pills (C) IUDs (D) Sterilisation
   - *Answer:* Condoms. Barrier methods block both sperm and pathogens.
2. Reproductive health includes: (A) Physical, mental and social well-being (B) Only physical health (C) Only fertility (D) Only contraception
   - *Answer:* Physical, mental and social well-being. It is a broad state of well-being related to reproduction.
3. Why is counselling important for reproductive health?
   - *Answer:* It helps people make informed, safe choices and avoid misinformation and infection.

### 3.2 Infertility, ART and Prenatal Care

Assisted reproductive technologies help couples with infertility. Prenatal care protects mother and child.

**Key points**

- Infertility may be caused by hormonal, anatomical or other factors in either partner.
- ART includes IVF, ZIFT, GIFT and surrogacy.
- IVF fertilises eggs outside the body and transfers the embryo to the uterus.
- Prenatal diagnosis detects genetic disorders but raises ethical issues when misused.
- Good nutrition, avoiding alcohol and tobacco and regular check-ups improve outcomes.

**Worked examples**

1. **IVF procedure.** Eggs are collected, fertilised with sperm in the laboratory, and the resulting embryo is transferred to the uterus.

2. **Why folic acid matters.** Adequate folic acid before and during early pregnancy reduces the risk of neural tube defects.

**Practice questions**

1. IVF stands for: (A) In vitro fertilisation (B) In vivo fertilisation (C) Internal fertilisation (D) Incomplete fertilisation
   - *Answer:* In vitro fertilisation. Fertilisation occurs outside the body in a dish.
2. Which is used to detect genetic disorders before birth? (A) Prenatal diagnosis (B) Vaccination (C) Dialysis (D) Biopsy of the mother
   - *Answer:* Prenatal diagnosis. Prenatal tests examine foetal cells or fluid.
3. What is surrogacy?
   - *Answer:* An arrangement in which a woman carries and delivers a child for another couple or person.

## Chapter 4. Principles of Inheritance and Variation

### 4.1 Mendelian Genetics and Deviations

Inheritance follows patterns described by Mendel. Some traits deviate from simple dominance.

**Key points**

- Law of dominance, segregation and independent assortment describe Mendelian inheritance.
- Incomplete dominance blends traits (pink flowers in snapdragon).
- Codominance expresses both alleles (AB blood group).
- Multiple alleles exist for blood groups (I<sup>A</sup>, I<sup>B</sup>, i).
- Pleiotropy: one gene affects many traits.

**Worked examples**

1. **Blood group inheritance.** A child of parents with blood groups A (I<sup>A</sup>i) and B (I<sup>B</sup>i) can be AB (I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup>), A, B or O.

2. **Incomplete dominance.** Red and white snapdragons cross to give pink F1 flowers because neither allele completely dominates.

**Practice questions**

1. In incomplete dominance, the F1 phenotype is: (A) Intermediate (B) Dominant (C) Recessive (D) Absent
   - *Answer:* Intermediate. Neither allele masks the other fully.
2. AB blood group is an example of: (A) Codominance (B) Incomplete dominance (C) Pleiotropy (D) Epistasis
   - *Answer:* Codominance. Both A and B antigens are expressed.
3. State Mendel's law of independent assortment.
   - *Answer:* Genes for different traits are inherited independently of each other during gamete formation.

### 4.2 Linkage, Sex Determination and Genetic Disorders

Genes on the same chromosome are linked. Sex-linked traits and chromosomal abnormalities show characteristic inheritance patterns.

**Key points**

- Linked genes tend to be inherited together.
- Sex determination in humans is XY: males XY, females XX.
- Haemophilia and colour blindness are X-linked recessive disorders.
- Down syndrome is caused by trisomy of chromosome 21.
- Pedigree analysis traces inheritance through families.

**Worked examples**

1. **Haemophilia inheritance.** A carrier mother (X<sup>H</sup>X<sup>h</sup>) has a 50% chance of an affected son (X<sup>h</sup>Y) with each pregnancy.

2. **Down syndrome.** An extra copy of chromosome 21 causes developmental differences; risk increases with maternal age.

**Practice questions**

1. Colour blindness is: (A) X-linked recessive (B) Autosomal dominant (C) Y-linked only (D) Mitochondrial
   - *Answer:* X-linked recessive. The gene is on the X chromosome.
2. Down syndrome is due to: (A) Trisomy of chromosome 21 (B) Monosomy of 21 (C) Sex chromosome loss (D) Mutation in one gene
   - *Answer:* Trisomy of chromosome 21. Three copies of chromosome 21 cause the condition.
3. Why are more males affected by X-linked recessive disorders?
   - *Answer:* Males have only one X chromosome, so a single recessive allele is expressed.

## Chapter 5. Molecular Basis of Inheritance

### 5.1 DNA Structure, Replication and Transcription

DNA stores genetic information. Replication copies it, and transcription produces RNA.

**Key points**

- DNA is a double helix with antiparallel strands and complementary base pairing.
- Semi-conservative replication produces two molecules, each with one parental strand.
- Transcription uses one DNA strand as a template for mRNA.
- In eukaryotes, mRNA is processed (splicing, capping, poly-A tail).
- The genetic code is triplet, degenerate and nearly universal.

**Worked examples**

1. **Semi-conservative replication.** Meselson and Stahl's experiment with isotope-labelled DNA proved each daughter molecule has one old and one new strand.

2. **Why the genetic code is degenerate.** Most amino acids are specified by more than one codon, which buffers against mutations.

**Practice questions**

1. DNA replication is: (A) Semi-conservative (B) Conservative (C) Dispersive (D) Random
   - *Answer:* Semi-conservative. Each new DNA molecule keeps one parental strand.
2. A codon consists of: (A) Three nucleotides (B) Two nucleotides (C) One nucleotide (D) Four nucleotides
   - *Answer:* Three nucleotides. Codons are triplets of bases.
3. Why is DNA a better genetic material than RNA?
   - *Answer:* Its double-stranded structure allows accurate replication and repair, and its stability protects information.

### 5.2 Translation, Regulation and the Human Genome

Genes are expressed through translation. Gene regulation controls when proteins are made.

**Key points**

- Translation uses ribosomes, tRNA and mRNA to assemble amino acids.
- The lac operon is a classic model of gene regulation in bacteria.
- Regulation can occur at transcription, processing, translation or protein level.
- The Human Genome Project mapped the sequence of human DNA.
- Mutations change DNA sequence and may alter protein function.

**Worked examples**

1. **Lac operon.** Lactose binds the repressor, allowing RNA polymerase to express genes for lactose metabolism only when lactose is present.

2. **Human Genome Project finding.** Only about 1.5–2% of the human genome codes for proteins, revealing many regulatory sequences.

**Practice questions**

1. Translation occurs on the: (A) Ribosome (B) Nucleus (C) Lysosome (D) Golgi body
   - *Answer:* Ribosome. Ribosomes translate mRNA into protein.
2. The lac operon is induced by: (A) Lactose (B) Glucose (C) Galactose (D) Sucrose
   - *Answer:* Lactose. Lactose inactivates the repressor.
3. What is a mutation?
   - *Answer:* A change in the DNA sequence that may alter the protein produced.

## Chapter 6. Evolution

### 6.1 Evidence and Mechanisms of Evolution

Evolution is change in populations over time. Evidence comes from fossils, comparative anatomy and molecular data.

**Key points**

- Fossils document extinct forms and transitional species.
- Homologous structures indicate common ancestry; analogous structures indicate convergent evolution.
- Natural selection is the main mechanism of adaptive evolution.
- Mutation, recombination and migration generate genetic variation.
- Genetic drift changes allele frequencies in small populations.

**Worked examples**

1. **Darwin's finches.** Different beak shapes on the Galápagos Islands reflect adaptation to different food sources.

2. **Antibiotic resistance.** Bacteria with resistance survive treatment and reproduce, so resistance spreads rapidly.

**Practice questions**

1. Homologous organs indicate: (A) Common ancestry (B) Convergent evolution (C) No evolution (D) Random mutation
   - *Answer:* Common ancestry. Similar structure in different species suggests shared origin.
2. The main mechanism of adaptive evolution is: (A) Natural selection (B) Genetic drift (C) Mutation only (D) Migration only
   - *Answer:* Natural selection. Selection favours traits that improve survival and reproduction.
3. How does antibiotic resistance illustrate natural selection?
   - *Answer:* Resistant bacteria survive treatment and reproduce, so the resistant trait increases in the population.

### 6.2 Speciation and Human Evolution

Speciation occurs when populations become reproductively isolated. Human evolution is traced through fossil hominids.

**Key points**

- Allopatric speciation occurs when populations are geographically separated.
- Reproductive isolation prevents gene flow between diverging populations.
- Adaptive radiation produces many species from a common ancestor.
- Hominid evolution includes Australopithecus, Homo habilis and Homo sapiens.
- Molecular clocks estimate divergence times.

**Worked examples**

1. **Marsupial radiation.** In Australia, marsupials diversified into many forms filling niches occupied elsewhere by placental mammals.

2. **Homo sapiens origins.** Fossil and genetic evidence places the origin of modern humans in Africa, with later migration across the world.

**Practice questions**

1. Reproductive isolation leads to: (A) Speciation (B) Extinction (C) Hybrid vigour (D) Cloning
   - *Answer:* Speciation. Isolation stops gene flow and allows divergence.
2. Adaptive radiation produces: (A) Many species from one ancestor (B) One species from many (C) Extinction (D) Cloning
   - *Answer:* Many species from one ancestor. Many species diverge to fill different niches.
3. What is allopatric speciation?
   - *Answer:* Speciation caused by geographic separation of populations, leading to reproductive isolation.

## Chapter 7. Human Health and Disease

### 7.1 Pathogens, Immunity and Vaccines

Pathogens cause disease. The immune system defends the body through innate and acquired immunity.

**Key points**

- Innate immunity is non-specific and present from birth.
- Acquired immunity is specific and develops after exposure.
- Antibodies are produced by B cells; T cells kill infected cells.
- Active immunity develops after infection or vaccination; passive immunity is transferred.
- Vaccines train the immune system without causing disease.

**Worked examples**

1. **How a vaccine works.** A weakened or inactivated pathogen triggers antibody production and memory cells, giving rapid protection on future exposure.

2. **Why colostrum matters.** Breast milk contains antibodies that provide passive immunity to newborns.

**Practice questions**

1. Antibodies are produced by: (A) B cells (B) T cells (C) Red blood cells (D) Platelets
   - *Answer:* B cells. B lymphocytes produce antibodies.
2. Vaccination provides: (A) Active acquired immunity (B) Passive immunity (C) Innate immunity only (D) No immunity
   - *Answer:* Active acquired immunity. The immune system actively responds to the vaccine.
3. Differentiate active and passive immunity.
   - *Answer:* Active immunity is produced by the body after exposure; passive immunity is received ready-made from another source.

### 7.2 Major Diseases and Their Prevention

Diseases differ in transmission and control. HIV, malaria, cancer and drug abuse are major health concerns.

**Key points**

- HIV attacks helper T cells, weakening immunity and leading to AIDS.
- Malaria is transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes.
- Cancer is uncontrolled cell division caused by genetic and environmental factors.
- Drug and alcohol abuse damages health and society; awareness and support are important.
- Prevention includes vaccination, sanitation, safe sex and vector control.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why HIV leads to AIDS.** HIV destroys helper T cells, so the immune system loses coordination and opportunistic infections appear.

2. **Malaria control.** Using mosquito nets, draining stagnant water and prompt treatment reduce malaria transmission.

**Practice questions**

1. Malaria is transmitted by: (A) Female Anopheles mosquito (B) Housefly (C) Aedes aegypti (D) Tsetse fly
   - *Answer:* Female Anopheles mosquito. Anopheles mosquitoes transmit the malaria parasite.
2. HIV primarily attacks: (A) Helper T cells (B) Red blood cells (C) Platelets (D) Neurons
   - *Answer:* Helper T cells. Loss of helper T cells cripples immunity.
3. Why is cancer called a disease of uncontrolled cell division?
   - *Answer:* Control mechanisms fail, so cells divide continuously and can spread to other tissues.

## Chapter 8. Microbes in Human Welfare

### 8.1 Microbes in Food, Industry and Medicine

Microorganisms are used to make food, medicines and industrial products and to treat waste.

**Key points**

- Fermentation by yeast makes bread, beer and wine; Lactobacillus makes curd and cheese.
- Antibiotics such as penicillin come from microorganisms.
- Microbes produce enzymes, organic acids, vitamins and biofuels.
- Biogas is produced by anaerobic digestion of waste.
- Vaccines and recombinant proteins are produced using microbes.

**Worked examples**

1. **Curd and cheese.** Lactobacillus converts milk sugar to lactic acid, curdling the protein and giving curd its tang.

2. **Biogas plant.** Methanogens digest cattle dung and produce methane-rich biogas used for cooking.

**Practice questions**

1. Which microorganism is used in bread making? (A) Yeast (B) Lactobacillus (C) Rhizobium (D) Amoeba
   - *Answer:* Yeast. Yeast ferments sugars and releases CO<sub>2</sub>, raising the dough.
2. Biogas is produced by: (A) Methanogens (B) Nitrifying bacteria (C) Viruses (D) Algae only
   - *Answer:* Methanogens. Methanogenic bacteria generate methane in anaerobic conditions.
3. Name two food products made using microorganisms.
   - *Answer:* Curd (Lactobacillus) and bread (yeast).

### 8.2 Microbes in Agriculture, Waste Treatment and Biocontrol

Microbes improve soil fertility, break down waste and control pests.

**Key points**

- Rhizobium fixes nitrogen in legume roots.
- Mycorrhiza improves phosphorus uptake in plants.
- Sewage is treated by aerobic and anaerobic bacteria.
- Biocontrol agents such as Bacillus thuringiensis and Trichoderma control pests.
- Biofertilisers reduce dependence on chemical fertilisers.

**Worked examples**

1. **Biofertiliser.** Rhizobium inoculants added to legume seeds increase nitrogen fixation and yield.

2. **Bt cotton.** The bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis provides a gene that protects cotton from bollworms.

**Practice questions**

1. Rhizobium helps plants by: (A) Fixing nitrogen (B) Killing insects (C) Producing oxygen only (D) Absorbing water only
   - *Answer:* Fixing nitrogen. It fixes atmospheric nitrogen in nodules.
2. Which is a biocontrol agent? (A) Trichoderma (B) Nitrogen gas (C) Plastic (D) Diesel
   - *Answer:* Trichoderma. Trichoderma is a fungus used against plant pathogens.
3. How does sewage treatment use microorganisms?
   - *Answer:* Aerobic and anaerobic bacteria decompose organic matter, reducing BOD and producing cleaner water.

## Chapter 9. Biotechnology: Principles and Processes

### 9.1 Recombinant DNA Technology

Biotechnology uses living systems to make useful products. Recombinant DNA technology joins DNA from different sources.

**Key points**

- Restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific sequences.
- DNA ligase joins DNA fragments.
- Plasmids act as vectors to carry genes into host cells.
- The polymerase chain reaction amplifies DNA exponentially.
- Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size.

**Worked examples**

1. **Making insulin.** The human insulin gene is inserted into a plasmid, put into bacteria, and the bacteria produce human insulin.

2. **PCR in diagnostics.** PCR amplifies tiny amounts of DNA, so infections and genetic conditions can be detected early.

**Practice questions**

1. Restriction enzymes cut DNA at: (A) Specific recognition sequences (B) Random points (C) Only ends (D) Only RNA
   - *Answer:* Specific recognition sequences. They recognise specific base sequences.
2. PCR is used to: (A) Amplify DNA (B) Cut DNA (C) Join DNA only (D) Extract proteins
   - *Answer:* Amplify DNA. Polymerase chain reaction multiplies DNA copies.
3. What is a plasmid?
   - *Answer:* A small circular DNA molecule used as a vector to carry foreign genes into bacteria.

### 9.2 Cloning, Bioreactors and Safety

Genetic engineering raises opportunities and ethical questions. Safety and regulation are essential.

**Key points**

- A clone is genetically identical to its parent organism.
- Bioreactors provide controlled conditions for large-scale culture.
- Downstream processing purifies the product.
- Genetic engineering requires biosafety and ethical guidelines.
- GM organisms must be assessed for environmental and health effects.

**Worked examples**

1. **Dolly the sheep.** Dolly was produced by somatic cell nuclear transfer, showing that differentiated nuclei can be reprogrammed.

2. **Regulation of GM crops.** GM crops are evaluated for safety before release, because gene flow and ecological effects need careful assessment.

**Practice questions**

1. A clone is: (A) Genetically identical to the parent (B) A hybrid (C) A mutant (D) A virus
   - *Answer:* Genetically identical to the parent. Clones carry the same genetic material.
2. Downstream processing refers to: (A) Purifying the product after reaction (B) Growing cells (C) Cutting DNA (D) Ethical review
   - *Answer:* Purifying the product after reaction. It covers separation and purification of the product.
3. Why are ethical guidelines important in biotechnology?
   - *Answer:* They protect safety, privacy and fairness while enabling beneficial applications.

## Chapter 10. Biotechnology and Its Applications

### 10.1 Agriculture and Medicine

Biotechnology improves crops, animals and human health.

**Key points**

- GM crops have traits such as pest resistance, herbicide tolerance and improved nutrition.
- Bt crops carry a bacterial gene for pest resistance.
- Golden rice was developed to increase vitamin A content.
- Recombinant insulin, vaccines and gene therapy have medical applications.
- Molecular diagnosis detects diseases at an early stage.

**Worked examples**

1. **Recombinant insulin.** Human insulin produced in bacteria is purer and more consistent than insulin extracted from animals.

2. **Gene therapy.** A functional gene is delivered to cells to compensate for a defective one in certain genetic disorders.

**Practice questions**

1. Bt crops are resistant to: (A) Insect pests (B) Herbicides only (C) Viruses only (D) Drought only
   - *Answer:* Insect pests. They carry a gene from Bacillus thuringiensis that kills specific insects.
2. Golden rice is enriched in: (A) Vitamin A (B) Vitamin C (C) Iron (D) Protein only
   - *Answer:* Vitamin A. It was developed to combat vitamin A deficiency.
3. Give one medical application of biotechnology.
   - *Answer:* Production of human insulin by recombinant bacteria for treating diabetes.

### 10.2 Transgenic Animals and Ethical Issues

Transgenic animals are used for research, medicine and food, raising ethical concerns.

**Key points**

- Transgenic animals carry foreign genes in their genome.
- They are used to study diseases, produce medicines and improve livestock.
- Cloning and transgenics require strict ethical review.
- Biosafety and informed consent are important principles.
- Benefits must be balanced against animal welfare and environmental risks.

**Worked examples**

1. **Transgenic sheep.** Sheep engineered to produce therapeutic proteins in their milk are used to make medicines.

2. **Ethical review boards.** Research involving animals must be approved by committees that weigh scientific value and welfare.

**Practice questions**

1. A transgenic animal: (A) Carries a foreign gene (B) Is always a clone (C) Cannot reproduce (D) Is a plant
   - *Answer:* Carries a foreign gene. A foreign gene has been inserted into its genome.
2. Biosafety guidelines protect: (A) Human health and the environment (B) Only industry profit (C) Only animals (D) Only plants
   - *Answer:* Human health and the environment. They reduce risks from GM organisms.
3. Why is animal welfare a concern in biotechnology?
   - *Answer:* Genetic modification and cloning can affect animal health and involve ethical trade-offs.

## Chapter 11. Organisms and Populations

### 11.1 Population Characteristics and Growth

Populations have size, density, age structure and growth patterns that ecologists model.

**Key points**

- Population density = number ÷ area or volume.
- Exponential growth occurs under unlimited resources: dN/dt = rN.
- Logistic growth includes carrying capacity K: dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K).
- Age pyramids show the proportion of young, reproductive and old individuals.
- Population interactions include competition, predation, parasitism and mutualism.

**Worked examples**

1. **Bacteria in a culture.** Under ideal conditions, bacteria double every generation, showing exponential growth until resources run out.

2. **Predator-prey cycles.** Lynx and hare populations oscillate, with predator numbers lagging behind prey numbers.

**Practice questions**

1. Logistic growth accounts for: (A) Carrying capacity (B) Infinite resources (C) No competition (D) Constant death rate
   - *Answer:* Carrying capacity. Growth slows as N approaches K.
2. Population density is: (A) Individuals per unit area (B) Total individuals only (C) Birth rate only (D) Death rate only
   - *Answer:* Individuals per unit area. Density is numbers per unit area or volume.
3. Differentiate exponential and logistic growth.
   - *Answer:* Exponential growth assumes unlimited resources; logistic growth includes a carrying capacity that slows growth.

### 11.2 Species Interactions and Adaptations

Organisms interact with each other and their environment, shaping distributions and adaptations.

**Key points**

- Competition occurs when resources are limited.
- Predation controls prey populations and drives adaptations.
- Parasitism benefits one organism at the expense of another.
- Mutualism benefits both partners, as in lichens and mycorrhizae.
- Adaptations allow organisms to survive extremes of temperature and water.

**Worked examples**

1. **Mycorrhiza.** Fungi extend the root's reach for water and phosphorus while receiving sugars from the plant.

2. **Desert adaptations.** Nocturnal habits, water storage and reduced leaves help desert plants and animals survive.

**Practice questions**

1. Mycorrhiza is an example of: (A) Mutualism (B) Parasitism (C) Competition (D) Predation
   - *Answer:* Mutualism. Both fungus and plant benefit.
2. Predation typically: (A) Controls prey population size (B) Increases prey unlimitedly (C) Has no effect (D) Only affects predators
   - *Answer:* Controls prey population size. Predators reduce prey numbers and shape adaptations.
3. Why are mutualistic relationships important in ecosystems?
   - *Answer:* They improve survival, nutrition and reproduction for both partners, supporting ecosystem stability.

## Chapter 12. Ecosystem

### 12.1 Energy Flow and Nutrient Cycling

Ecosystems have producers, consumers and decomposers. Energy flows one way; nutrients cycle.

**Key points**

- Solar energy is captured by producers through photosynthesis.
- Only about 10% of energy passes to the next trophic level.
- Ecological pyramids show numbers, biomass or energy at each level.
- Nutrients cycle through biogeochemical cycles (carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus).
- Decomposers return nutrients to the soil.

**Worked examples**

1. **Energy pyramid.** A pyramid of energy is always upright because energy decreases at each level.

2. **Carbon cycle.** Photosynthesis removes CO<sub>2</sub> and respiration and combustion return it, cycling carbon through living and non-living reservoirs.

**Practice questions**

1. Energy transfer between trophic levels is about: (A) 10% (B) 50% (C) 90% (D) 100%
   - *Answer:* 10%. The ten per cent law governs energy flow.
2. A pyramid of energy is always: (A) Upright (B) Inverted (C) Horizontal (D) Variable
   - *Answer:* Upright. Energy always decreases upward.
3. Why is the number of trophic levels limited?
   - *Answer:* So little energy remains after several transfers that further levels cannot be supported.

### 12.2 Ecological Succession and Ecosystem Services

Ecosystems change over time through succession and provide services essential for humans.

**Key points**

- Primary succession begins on bare rock; secondary succession follows disturbance.
- Pioneer species modify the habitat, enabling later species.
- Climax communities are relatively stable end stages.
- Ecosystem services include pollination, water purification and climate regulation.
- Biodiversity increases ecosystem resilience.

**Worked examples**

1. **Lichens on bare rock.** Lichens are pioneer species that break down rock and add organic matter, preparing the way for mosses and grasses.

2. **Wetland services.** Wetlands filter pollutants, store floodwater and recharge groundwater — free services to society.

**Practice questions**

1. Pioneer species in primary succession on rock are: (A) Lichens (B) Trees (C) Grasses (D) Ferns
   - *Answer:* Lichens. Lichens can colonise bare rock.

## Chapter 13. Biodiversity and Conservation

### 13.1 Levels and Patterns of Biodiversity

Biodiversity includes genetic, species and ecosystem diversity. It is unevenly distributed and threatened.

**Key points**

- Species diversity is highest in the tropics.
- The species-area relationship shows more species in larger areas.
- Biodiversity hotspots are regions of high endemism and high threat.
- Losses are driven by habitat destruction, overexploitation, invasive species and pollution.
- Extinction is a natural process, but human activity accelerates it.

**Worked examples**

1. **Species-area curve.** Larger islands and habitat patches usually hold more species, following a log relationship.

2. **Invasive species.** Water hyacinth spreads rapidly in Indian water bodies, crowding out native plants and harming aquatic life.

**Practice questions**

1. Which region has the greatest species diversity? (A) Tropics (B) Poles (C) Deserts (D) Deep ocean only
   - *Answer:* Tropics. Tropical regions hold the highest species richness.
2. A biodiversity hotspot has: (A) High endemism and high threat (B) Low endemism (C) Only deserts (D) No threat
   - *Answer:* High endemism and high threat. Hotspots combine richness with vulnerability.
3. Name three causes of biodiversity loss.
   - *Answer:* Habitat destruction, overexploitation and invasive species (also pollution and climate change).

### 13.2 Conservation Strategies

Conservation protects biodiversity in place (in situ) and outside habitats (ex situ).

**Key points**

- In situ conservation: national parks, sanctuaries and biosphere reserves.
- Ex situ conservation: zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks and cryopreservation.
- Community participation makes conservation sustainable.
- International agreements and national laws protect species and habitats.
- Sustainable use balances conservation with human needs.

**Worked examples**

1. **Seed banks.** Seeds stored at low temperature preserve genetic diversity of crops and wild plants for future generations.

2. **Community reserves.** Local communities protect sacred groves and community forests, conserving biodiversity with traditional knowledge.

**Practice questions**

1. A national park is an example of: (A) In situ conservation (B) Ex situ conservation (C) A zoo (D) A seed bank
   - *Answer:* In situ conservation. Protected areas conserve species in their natural habitat.
2. A seed bank is an example of: (A) Ex situ conservation (B) In situ conservation (C) Pollution control (D) Habitat destruction
   - *Answer:* Ex situ conservation. Seeds are stored outside the natural habitat.
3. Why is community participation important for conservation?
   - *Answer:* Local people depend on and manage ecosystems, so their involvement makes protection practical and lasting.
