## Chapter 1. Solutions

### 1.1 Concentration Terms and Raoult's Law

Solutions are homogeneous mixtures. Concentration can be expressed as molarity, molality or mole fraction, and vapour pressure depends on composition.

**Key points**

- Molarity M = moles of solute per litre of solution; molality m = moles per kg of solvent.
- Mole fraction x<sub>i</sub> = n<sub>i</sub>/Σn; the sum of mole fractions is 1.
- Raoult's law: partial vapour pressure p<sub>i</sub> = x<sub>i</sub> p<sub>i</sub>°.
- Ideal solutions obey Raoult's law and have zero enthalpy of mixing.
- Positive deviation gives higher vapour pressure; negative gives lower.

**Worked examples**

1. **Molality calculation.** 10 g of NaCl (M = 58.5) in 100 g of water gives 0.171 mol in 0.1 kg, so m = 1.71 mol/kg.

2. **Raoult's law.** A mixture with x<sub>benzene</sub> = 0.4 and p<sub>benzene</sub>° = 75 mmHg has p<sub>benzene</sub> = 30 mmHg.

**Practice questions**

1. Which concentration term is independent of temperature? (A) Molality (B) Molarity (C) Normality (D) Formality
   - *Answer:* Molality. Molality uses mass of solvent, which does not change with temperature.
2. An ideal solution has enthalpy of mixing: (A) Zero (B) Positive (C) Negative (D) Infinite
   - *Answer:* Zero. No heat is evolved or absorbed on mixing ideal solutions.
3. State Raoult's law.
   - *Answer:* The partial vapour pressure of each component is proportional to its mole fraction in the solution.

### 1.2 Colligative Properties

Colligative properties depend on the number of solute particles: vapour pressure lowering, boiling point elevation, freezing point depression and osmotic pressure.

**Key points**

- ΔT<sub>b</sub> = K<sub>b</sub>m; ΔT<sub>f</sub> = K<sub>f</sub>m.
- Osmotic pressure π = CRT for dilute solutions.
- Van't Hoff factor i accounts for association or dissociation.
- Colligative properties are used to determine molar mass.
- Winter salt spreading lowers the freezing point of water.

**Worked examples**

1. **Freezing point depression.** Adding 1 mol of a non-electrolyte to 1 kg water gives ΔT<sub>f</sub> = 1.86 °C, so the freezing point falls to −1.86 °C.

2. **Van't Hoff factor.** NaCl dissociates into two ions, so i ≈ 2 and the colligative effect nearly doubles.

**Practice questions**

1. Osmotic pressure is given by: (A) π = CRT (B) π = RT/C (C) π = C/RT (D) π = CV
   - *Answer:* π = CRT. π = CRT for dilute solutions.
2. Which property is NOT colligative? (A) Viscosity (B) Boiling point elevation (C) Osmotic pressure (D) Freezing point depression
   - *Answer:* Viscosity. Viscosity depends on the nature of the solute, not just the number of particles.
3. Why does adding salt lower the freezing point of water?
   - *Answer:* The solute particles disrupt the crystal lattice, so more energy must be removed before ice forms.

## Chapter 2. Electrochemistry

### 2.1 Electrochemical Cells and EMF

Electrochemistry connects chemical reactions and electrical energy. Galvanic cells produce electricity; electrolytic cells consume it.

**Key points**

- In a galvanic cell, oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode.
- E°<sub>cell</sub> = E°<sub>cathode</sub> − E°<sub>anode</sub>.
- A positive E°<sub>cell</sub> means the reaction is spontaneous.
- The Nernst equation gives cell potential at non-standard conditions.
- A salt bridge maintains electrical neutrality.

**Worked examples**

1. **Daniel cell.** Zn|Zn<sup>2+</sup> || Cu<sup>2+</sup>|Cu has E° = 0.34 − (−0.76) = 1.10 V, so it is spontaneous.

2. **Nernst equation.** E = E° − (0.059/n) log Q at 25 °C; increasing product concentration lowers E.

**Practice questions**

1. In a galvanic cell, oxidation occurs at the: (A) Anode (B) Cathode (C) Salt bridge (D) Electrolyte
   - *Answer:* Anode. Oxidation always happens at the anode.
2. A positive standard cell potential means the reaction is: (A) Spontaneous (B) Non-spontaneous (C) At equilibrium (D) Reversible only
   - *Answer:* Spontaneous. Positive E° corresponds to negative ΔG.
3. Why is a salt bridge used in a galvanic cell?
   - *Answer:* It completes the circuit and maintains electrical neutrality in the two half-cells.

### 2.2 Conductance, Kohlrausch's Law and Faraday's Laws

Ionic conductivity depends on concentration and ion mobility. Faraday's laws quantify electrolysis.

**Key points**

- Molar conductivity increases with dilution for weak electrolytes.
- Kohlrausch's law: limiting molar conductivity of an electrolyte is the sum of ion contributions.
- Faraday's first law: mass deposited is proportional to charge passed.
- Faraday's second law: masses deposited by the same charge are proportional to equivalent weights.
- 1 Faraday = 96,500 C/mol.

**Worked examples**

1. **Mass deposited.** Depositing copper from Cu<sup>2+</sup> with 2 × 96,500 C gives 1 mol of copper (63.5 g).

2. **Why conductivity falls with concentration.** Ion-ion interactions reduce mobility at higher concentrations.

**Practice questions**

1. One Faraday equals: (A) 96,500 C (B) 1 C (C) 96,500 mol (D) 9.65 C
   - *Answer:* 96,500 C. One mole of electrons carries 96,500 C.
2. Molar conductivity of a weak electrolyte increases on dilution because: (A) Ionisation increases (B) Ions disappear (C) Temperature drops (D) Viscosity rises
   - *Answer:* Ionisation increases. More ions form as the solution is diluted.
3. State Faraday's first law of electrolysis.
   - *Answer:* The mass of substance deposited at an electrode is proportional to the quantity of charge passed.

## Chapter 3. Chemical Kinetics

### 3.1 Rate of Reaction and Rate Laws

Kinetics studies reaction rates. Rate laws relate rate to reactant concentrations and reveal reaction order.

**Key points**

- Average rate = change in concentration ÷ time.
- Order is the sum of concentration exponents in the experimental rate law.
- First-order rate constant k = (2.303/t) log(a/(a − x)).
- Half-life of a first-order reaction is independent of initial concentration.
- Zero-order reactions have a constant rate.

**Worked examples**

1. **First-order half-life.** A first-order reaction with k = 0.0693 min<sup>−1</sup> has t<sub>1/2</sub> = 0.693/0.0693 = 10 min.

2. **Determining order.** Doubling one reactant doubles the rate means first order in that reactant.

**Practice questions**

1. For a first-order reaction, half-life is: (A) Independent of concentration (B) Proportional to concentration (C) Proportional to concentration squared (D) Zero
   - *Answer:* Independent of concentration. t<sub>1/2</sub> = 0.693/k for first order.
2. The unit of a zero-order rate constant is: (A) mol L<sup>−1</sup> s<sup>−1</sup> (B) s<sup>−1</sup> (C) L mol<sup>−1</sup> s<sup>−1</sup> (D) mol<sup>−2</sup> L<sup>2</sup> s<sup>−1</sup>
   - *Answer:* mol L<sup>−1</sup> s<sup>−1</sup>. A zero-order rate is constant in concentration units per time.
3. A first-order reaction has k = 0.693 s<sup>−1</sup>. Find its half-life.
   - *Answer:* t<sub>1/2</sub> = 0.693/k = 1 s.

### 3.2 Factors Affecting Rate and Arrhenius Equation

Temperature, catalysts, surface area and concentration affect rate. The Arrhenius equation links rate constant and activation energy.

**Key points**

- Arrhenius equation: k = A e<sup>−Ea/(RT)</sup>.
- A catalyst lowers activation energy without being consumed.
- A 10 °C rise in temperature roughly doubles many reaction rates.
- Collision theory: only molecules with energy above E<sub>a</sub> react.
- Increasing surface area increases the frequency of collisions.

**Worked examples**

1. **Temperature effect.** If E<sub>a</sub> = 50 kJ/mol, raising temperature from 300 K to 310 K increases k by roughly a factor of 2.

2. **Catalyst in industry.** Iron catalyses ammonia synthesis by lowering activation energy, so the process works at more moderate conditions.

**Practice questions**

1. A catalyst works by: (A) Lowering activation energy (B) Increasing activation energy (C) Changing ΔH (D) Changing equilibrium
   - *Answer:* Lowering activation energy. Lower E<sub>a</sub> gives faster reaction at the same temperature.
2. The Arrhenius equation relates k to: (A) Temperature and activation energy (B) Pressure only (C) Volume (D) Concentration only
   - *Answer:* Temperature and activation energy. k depends exponentially on temperature and E<sub>a</sub>.
3. Why does a catalyst not change the equilibrium constant?
   - *Answer:* It speeds up forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached sooner but its position is unchanged.

## Chapter 4. The d- and f-Block Elements

### 4.1 Transition Metal Properties

Transition metals show variable oxidation states, form coloured compounds and act as catalysts.

**Key points**

- Variable oxidation states arise from similar 4s and 3d energies.
- Coloured ions come from d-d electronic transitions.
- Many transition metals and their compounds catalyse reactions.
- They form complex compounds with ligands.
- Trends across the series reflect increasing nuclear charge and 3d filling.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why Mn shows many oxidation states.** Manganese has five 3d electrons and two 4s electrons, so it can lose different numbers, giving states from +2 to +7.

2. **Coloured Cu<sup>2+</sup> solutions.** Hydrated Cu<sup>2+</sup> absorbs red-orange light from d-d transitions, so the solution appears blue.

**Practice questions**

1. Transition metals show variable oxidation states because: (A) 3d and 4s energies are close (B) They have only s electrons (C) They lack d electrons (D) They are non-metals
   - *Answer:* 3d and 4s energies are close. Electrons from both 3d and 4s can be used.
2. Colour of transition metal ions is due to: (A) d-d transitions (B) Nuclear transitions (C) Vibrations only (D) Ionisation
   - *Answer:* d-d transitions. Absorption of visible light excites d electrons.
3. Why is Zn not considered a typical transition metal?
   - *Answer:* Its 3d subshell is full, so it does not show variable oxidation states or coloured compounds.

### 4.2 Lanthanoids and Actinoids

The f-block elements show lanthanoid and actinoid contraction and characteristic chemistry.

**Key points**

- Lanthanoids fill the 4f subshell and show mostly +3 oxidation state.
- Lanthanoid contraction causes a gradual decrease in size across the series.
- Actinoids fill 5f orbitals and show more variable oxidation states.
- Actinoids are mostly radioactive, with uranium and plutonium important in nuclear energy.
- Lanthanoids are used in magnets, lasers, screens and alloys.

**Worked examples**

1. **Lanthanoid contraction.** Poor shielding by 4f electrons pulls the nuclei closer, so atomic radii shrink across the lanthanoid series.

2. **Neodymium magnets.** Lanthanoid-based magnets are extremely strong and are used in motors, headphones and wind turbines.

**Practice questions**

1. Lanthanoids are characterised by filling of: (A) 4f orbitals (B) 3d orbitals (C) 5f orbitals (D) 4p orbitals
   - *Answer:* 4f orbitals. Lanthanoids fill 4f orbitals.
2. Actinoids are mostly: (A) Radioactive (B) Non-radioactive (C) Gases (D) Halogens
   - *Answer:* Radioactive. All actinoids are radioactive.
3. What is lanthanoid contraction?
   - *Answer:* The gradual decrease in atomic and ionic radii across the lanthanoid series due to poor shielding of 4f electrons.

## Chapter 5. Surface Chemistry

### 5.1 Adsorption and Catalysis

Surface chemistry studies phenomena at interfaces. Adsorption concentrates molecules on a surface; catalysis speeds reactions at surfaces.

**Key points**

- Adsorption may be physisorption (weak van der Waals forces) or chemisorption (chemical bonds).
- Physisorption is reversible and has low enthalpy; chemisorption is often irreversible with high enthalpy.
- Freundlich isotherm: x/m = kp<sup>1/n</sup> describes adsorption at a given temperature.
- Catalysts may be homogeneous or heterogeneous; heterogeneous catalysis occurs at surfaces.
- Catalyst promoters increase activity; poisons reduce it.

**Worked examples**

1. **Activated charcoal.** Charcoal has a huge surface area and adsorbs gases and colouring matter, which is why it is used in gas masks and water filters.

2. **Iron in ammonia synthesis.** The solid iron catalyst provides a surface where nitrogen and hydrogen adsorb and react more easily, lowering activation energy.

**Practice questions**

1. Physisorption is characterised by: (A) Weak van der Waals forces (B) Strong chemical bonds (C) Irreversibility (D) High enthalpy
   - *Answer:* Weak van der Waals forces. Physical adsorption involves weak forces and is reversible.
2. A promoter in catalysis: (A) Increases catalyst activity (B) Poisons the catalyst (C) Stops the reaction (D) Is a product
   - *Answer:* Increases catalyst activity. Promoters enhance catalytic efficiency.
3. Differentiate physisorption and chemisorption.
   - *Answer:* Physisorption uses weak forces and is reversible; chemisorption involves chemical bonds and is usually irreversible.

### 5.2 Colloids and Emulsions

Colloidal particles are larger than molecules but too small to settle. Their surfaces give them special properties.

**Key points**

- Colloids are classified as lyophilic or lyophobic depending on affinity for the medium.
- The Tyndall effect, Brownian motion and electrophoresis reveal colloidal nature.
- Coagulation destroys a colloid; electrolytes cause coagulation.
- Emulsions are colloids of one liquid in another, stabilised by emulsifiers.
- Colloids are used in medicine, food, paints and water purification.

**Worked examples**

1. **Milk as an emulsion.** Fat droplets are dispersed in water and stabilised by proteins, making milk an oil-in-water emulsion.

2. **River delta formation.** River water carrying clay colloids meets salty sea water; ions coagulate the clay, which settles as a delta.

**Practice questions**

1. Which effect confirms the colloidal nature of a mixture? (A) Tyndall effect (B) Photoelectric effect (C) Doppler effect (D) Zeeman effect
   - *Answer:* Tyndall effect. Colloidal particles scatter light.
2. An emulsion is a colloid of: (A) One liquid in another (B) Gas in liquid only (C) Solid in solid (D) Gas in gas
   - *Answer:* One liquid in another. Emulsions involve two immiscible liquids.
3. Why is milk called an emulsion?
   - *Answer:* Fat droplets are dispersed throughout water and stabilised by emulsifying proteins.

## Chapter 6. Coordination Compounds

### 6.1 Nomenclature and Isomerism

Coordination compounds contain a central metal bonded to ligands. Naming follows IUPAC rules and several isomerism types are possible.

**Key points**

- A coordination entity has a central atom and ligands in a definite ratio.
- Ligand names end with -o (chlorido, cyanido); anionic complexes end with -ate.
- The coordination number is the number of donor atoms bonded to the metal.
- Isomers include geometrical (cis/trans), optical and structural forms.
- Chelate ligands bind through more than one atom and increase stability.

**Worked examples**

1. **Naming [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]Cl<sub>3</sub>.** The octahedral complex of cobalt(III) with six ammine ligands is hexaamminecobalt(III) chloride.

2. **Cis-platin.** cis-[PtCl<sub>2</sub>(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>] binds DNA and is used in cancer chemotherapy; the trans isomer is inactive.

**Practice questions**

1. In [Fe(CN)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>4−</sup>, the coordination number of iron is: (A) 6 (B) 4 (C) 2 (D) 8
   - *Answer:* 6. Six cyanide ligands bind to iron.
2. A chelating ligand: (A) Binds through more than one donor atom (B) Binds through one atom (C) Cannot bind (D) Only forms ionic bonds
   - *Answer:* Binds through more than one donor atom. Chelates form stable ring structures.
3. Why is cis-platin an effective anticancer drug?
   - *Answer:* It binds to DNA and prevents replication of cancer cells.

### 6.2 Crystal Field Theory and Colour

Crystal field theory explains colour and magnetism by splitting d orbitals in a ligand field.

**Key points**

- Ligands split d orbitals into lower-energy t<sub>2g</sub> and higher-energy e<sub>g</sub> sets.
- The splitting energy Δ<sub>o</sub> depends on the metal and ligand.
- Strong-field ligands produce large splitting and low-spin complexes.
- Colour arises when electrons absorb visible light to jump between the sets.
- The spectrochemical series orders ligands by field strength: I<sup>−</sup> < Br<sup>−</sup> < Cl<sup>−</sup> < F<sup>−</sup> < H<sub>2</sub>O < NH<sub>3</sub> < CN<sup>−</sup>.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why [Ni(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> is green.** Absorption of light corresponding to Δ<sub>o</sub> promotes d electrons, and the transmitted colour is green.

2. **High spin versus low spin.** Weak-field ligands like Cl<sup>−</sup> give high-spin complexes with unpaired electrons; strong-field CN<sup>−</sup> gives low-spin.

**Practice questions**

1. The colour of coordination compounds is due to: (A) d-d transitions (B) s-s transitions (C) Nuclear spin (D) Rotation
   - *Answer:* d-d transitions. Electron transitions between split d orbitals absorb visible light.
2. Which ligand produces the largest crystal field splitting? (A) CN<sup>−</sup> (B) Cl<sup>−</sup> (C) H<sub>2</sub>O (D) F<sup>−</sup>
   - *Answer:* CN<sup>−</sup>. CN<sup>−</sup> is high in the spectrochemical series.
3. Why is [Cu(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> blue?
   - *Answer:* It absorbs red-orange light in d-d transitions and transmits blue light.

## Chapter 7. The p-Block Elements

### 7.1 Group 15 and 16 Elements in Detail

The p-block groups show trends in oxidation states, hydrides and oxides as we move down each group.

**Key points**

- Group 15: N, P, As, Sb, Bi; oxidation states −3 to +5, with +3 becoming more stable down the group (inert pair effect).
- Nitrogen forms stable N<sub>2</sub> with a triple bond, making it inert.
- Phosphorus exists as white (reactive, toxic) and red (stable) allotropes.
- Group 16: O, S, Se, Te, Po; oxygen shows only −2 and −1, while sulphur can show +4 and +6.
- Oxides of these groups are acidic; nitrogen oxides contribute to pollution.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why nitrogen is inert.** N≡N has a very high bond dissociation energy, so nitrogen gas reacts only under extreme conditions or with catalysts.

2. **Allotropes of phosphorus.** White phosphorus burns spontaneously in air and is stored under water; red phosphorus is stable and used in matchboxes.

**Practice questions**

1. Which allotrope of phosphorus is stored under water? (A) White phosphorus (B) Red phosphorus (C) Black phosphorus (D) Violet phosphorus
   - *Answer:* White phosphorus. White phosphorus is reactive and is kept under water.
2. The inert pair effect makes which oxidation state more stable down group 15? (A) +3 (B) +5 (C) +1 (D) −3
   - *Answer:* +3. Lower oxidation states become more stable down the group.
3. Why is nitrogen gas chemically inert?
   - *Answer:* Its triple bond has very high dissociation energy, so breaking it requires extreme conditions.

### 7.2 Halogens and Noble Gases

Group 17 elements are reactive non-metals with important industrial uses; group 18 elements are notably inert.

**Key points**

- Halogens: F<sub>2</sub>, Cl<sub>2</sub>, Br<sub>2</sub>, I<sub>2</sub>; reactivity decreases down the group.
- Chlorine is used for water disinfection and in making PVC.
- The bleaching action of chlorine comes from oxidation (hypochlorous acid).
- Noble gases have filled outer shells; xenon forms compounds such as XeF<sub>2</sub> and XeF<sub>4</sub>.
- Where the group 17 and 18 elements are located matters for periodic trends.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why fluorine is the most reactive halogen.** Its small size and high electronegativity make it eager to gain an electron, so it reacts with almost every element.

2. **Xenon fluorides.** Xenon reacts with fluorine under controlled conditions to form compounds such as XeF<sub>4</sub>, showing that noble gases are not completely inert.

**Practice questions**

1. Which halogen is most reactive? (A) Fluorine (B) Chlorine (C) Bromine (D) Iodine
   - *Answer:* Fluorine. Reactivity decreases down group 17.
2. Chlorine bleaches by: (A) Oxidation (B) Reduction (C) Adsorption (D) Neutralisation
   - *Answer:* Oxidation. Hypochlorous acid oxidises colouring matter.
3. Why are noble gases largely inert?
   - *Answer:* Their filled outer shells give very high ionisation energies and little tendency to gain electrons.

## Chapter 8. Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

### 8.1 Nucleophilic Substitution

Haloalkanes undergo substitution. S<sub>N</sub>1<sup> and S<sub>N</sub>2</sup> mechanisms differ in rate law and stereochemistry.

**Key points**

- S<sub>N</sub>2^ is a one-step reaction with a rate depending on both substrate and nucleophile.
- S<sub>N</sub>1^ goes through a carbocation intermediate and shows first-order kinetics.
- Tertiary substrates favour S<sub>N</sub>1<sup>; primary favour S<sub>N</sub>2</sup>.
- S<sub>N</sub>2^ causes inversion of configuration (Walden inversion).
- Haloarenes are unreactive toward S<sub>N</sub>2^ because of partial double-bond character of the C–X bond.

**Worked examples**

1. **S<sub>N</sub>2^ inversion.** Hydrolysis of an optically active halide by S<sub>N</sub>2^ gives the product with inverted configuration.

2. **Why chlorobenzene is inert.** Resonance shortens and strengthens the C–Cl bond, so nucleophiles cannot easily displace chloride.

**Practice questions**

1. S<sub>N</sub>1^ reactions are favoured by: (A) Tertiary halides (B) Primary halides (C) Methyl halides (D) Haloarenes
   - *Answer:* Tertiary halides. Tertiary carbocations are more stable, favouring S<sub>N</sub>1^.
2. S<sub>N</sub>2^ substitution produces: (A) Inversion of configuration (B) Retention only (C) Racemisation only (D) No change
   - *Answer:* Inversion of configuration. The nucleophile attacks the opposite side of the leaving group.
3. Why are haloarenes less reactive than haloalkanes?
   - *Answer:* Partial double-bond character of the C–X bond makes it stronger and harder to break.

### 8.2 Reactions and Uses of Halo Compounds

Haloalkanes are versatile intermediates and also include important environmental pollutants.

**Key points**

- Grignard reagents (RMgX) are formed from haloalkanes and magnesium and are powerful synthetic tools.
- Elimination reactions give alkenes (dehydrohalogenation).
- Haloalkanes react with metals to form organometallics.
- CFCs deplete the ozone layer and are widely restricted.
- DDT is a persistent pesticide that accumulates in food chains.

**Worked examples**

1. **Grignard reaction.** CH<sub>3</sub>MgBr reacts with HCHO to give ethanol after hydrolysis, showing carbon–carbon bond formation.

2. **CFC ban.** The Montreal Protocol phased out CFCs because chlorine radicals destroy stratospheric ozone.

**Practice questions**

1. Grignard reagents contain: (A) Mg (B) Na (C) Fe (D) Cl only
   - *Answer:* Mg. RMgX contains magnesium.
2. CFCs harm the: (A) Ozone layer (B) Soil only (C) Water only (D) Rocks
   - *Answer:* Ozone layer. Chlorine radicals break down ozone.
3. Why are CFCs dangerous for the environment?
   - *Answer:* They release chlorine radicals in the stratosphere that catalytically destroy ozone molecules.

## Chapter 9. Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

### 9.1 Alcohols and Phenols

Alcohols contain −OH on saturated carbon; phenols have −OH on an aromatic ring. Their reactivity differs sharply.

**Key points**

- Alcohols hydrogen-bond, giving high boiling points.
- Phenols are acidic because the phenoxide ion is resonance-stabilised.
- Alcohols are acidic but weaker than water; phenols are much more acidic.
- Primary alcohols oxidise to aldehydes then carboxylic acids.
- Phenols give characteristic colour with FeCl<sub>3</sub> and are easily brominated.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why phenol is acidic.** The negative charge on the phenoxide ion is delocalised into the ring, stabilising the conjugate base.

2. **Lucas test.** Tertiary alcohols react immediately with Lucas reagent; primary alcohols react only on heating, distinguishing the classes.

**Practice questions**

1. Phenol is more acidic than ethanol because: (A) Phenoxide is resonance-stabilised (B) It is aromatic (C) It dissolves in water (D) It boils higher
   - *Answer:* Phenoxide is resonance-stabilised. Resonance stabilises the phenoxide anion.
2. Oxidation of a primary alcohol gives first: (A) An aldehyde (B) A ketone (C) A carboxylic acid (D) An ester
   - *Answer:* An aldehyde. Controlled oxidation stops at the aldehyde stage.
3. Why do alcohols have higher boiling points than ethers of similar mass?
   - *Answer:* Alcohols form intermolecular hydrogen bonds; ethers do not.

### 9.2 Ethers and Important Alcohols

Ethers are relatively unreactive solvents; some alcohols are vital industrial and biological compounds.

**Key points**

- Ethers are less soluble in water but dissolve many organic compounds.
- Williamson synthesis makes ethers from an alkoxide and a haloalkane.
- Methanol is toxic and causes blindness; it is used as a solvent and fuel additive.
- Ethanol is produced by fermentation and is used as a fuel and solvent.
- Phenol and its derivatives are used in disinfectants and pharmaceuticals.

**Worked examples**

1. **Williamson ether synthesis.** Sodium ethoxide plus bromoethane gives diethyl ether, a classic ether preparation.

2. **Denatured alcohol.** Adding methanol to ethanol makes it unfit to drink while keeping it usable as a solvent.

**Practice questions**

1. Williamson synthesis produces: (A) Ethers (B) Esters (C) Alcohols (D) Aldehydes
   - *Answer:* Ethers. An alkoxide and a haloalkane give an ether.
2. Which alcohol is highly toxic? (A) Methanol (B) Ethanol (C) Propanol (D) Butanol
   - *Answer:* Methanol. Methanol is toxic and can cause blindness.
3. Why is ethanol used as a fuel?
   - *Answer:* It burns cleanly and can be produced from renewable crops by fermentation.

## Chapter 10. Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids

### 10.1 Structure, Nomenclature and Reactions

Aldehydes and ketones contain the carbonyl group. Nucleophilic addition and oxidation distinguish them.

**Key points**

- Aldehydes are easily oxidised; ketones are not.
- Tollens' and Fehling's tests detect aldehydes.
- Nucleophilic addition is the key reaction of carbonyls.
- Aldol condensation and Cannizzaro reactions are important syntheses.
- Carboxylic acids are weakly acidic and give esters with alcohols.

**Worked examples**

1. **Tollens' silver mirror.** An aldehyde reduces [Ag(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>]<sup>+</sup> to silver metal, forming a mirror on the test tube.

2. **Esterification.** Ethanoic acid and ethanol with acid catalyst give ethyl ethanoate, an ester with fruity smell.

**Practice questions**

1. Tollens' reagent gives a silver mirror with: (A) Aldehydes (B) Ketones (C) Alcohols (D) Ethers
   - *Answer:* Aldehydes. Aldehydes are oxidised while silver ions are reduced.
2. Carboxylic acids are: (A) Weakly acidic (B) Strongly basic (C) Neutral (D) Strongly acidic
   - *Answer:* Weakly acidic. They ionise partially to give a carboxylate ion.
3. Why are carboxylic acids more acidic than phenols?
   - *Answer:* The carboxylate ion is stabilised by resonance over two equivalent oxygens.

### 10.2 Preparations and Uses

Carbonyl compounds are prepared by oxidation of alcohols and are used in many industries.

**Key points**

- Primary alcohols oxidise to aldehydes; secondary alcohols to ketones.
- Formaldehyde is used in plastics and as a preservative.
- Acetone is an important solvent.
- Acetic acid is used in food and manufacturing.
- Grignard reagents react with CO<sub>2</sub> to give carboxylic acids.

**Worked examples**

1. **Acetone from propan-2-ol.** Oxidising a secondary alcohol gives a ketone; propan-2-ol gives propanone (acetone).

2. **Formaldehyde in resins.** Formaldehyde polymerises with phenol to make bakelite, a thermosetting plastic.

**Practice questions**

1. Oxidation of a secondary alcohol gives a: (A) Ketone (B) Aldehyde (C) Carboxylic acid (D) Ether
   - *Answer:* Ketone. Secondary alcohols give ketones.
2. Formaldehyde is used in: (A) Plastics and preservation (B) Fuels only (C) Glass only (D) Fertilisers only
   - *Answer:* Plastics and preservation. It is a key synthetic intermediate.
3. How would you distinguish an aldehyde from a ketone?
   - *Answer:* Tollens' or Fehling's test: the aldehyde gives a positive result, the ketone does not.

## Chapter 11. Amines

### 11.1 Structure, Basicity and Preparation

Amines are derivatives of ammonia. Basicity depends on electron density on nitrogen and substitution.

**Key points**

- Amines are basic due to a lone pair on nitrogen.
- Aliphatic amines are more basic than ammonia; aromatic amines are less basic.
- Aniline is less basic because the lone pair is delocalised into the ring.
- Amines are prepared by reduction of nitro compounds or by Gabriel phthalimide synthesis.
- Amines react with acids to form salts.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why aniline is less basic.** Delocalisation of the nitrogen lone pair into the benzene ring reduces availability for protonation.

2. **Amine salt.** Methylamine reacts with HCl to give methylammonium chloride, a water-soluble salt.

**Practice questions**

1. Which amine is least basic? (A) Aniline (B) Ammonia (C) Methylamine (D) Ethylamine
   - *Answer:* Aniline. Delocalisation makes aniline's lone pair less available.
2. Gabriel phthalimide synthesis is used to prepare: (A) Primary amines (B) Secondary amines (C) Tertiary amines (D) Amides
   - *Answer:* Primary amines. The Gabriel synthesis yields primary amines.
3. Why are amines basic?
   - *Answer:* They have a lone pair of electrons on nitrogen that can accept a proton.

### 11.2 Reactions and Diazonium Salts

Amines undergo acylation, alkylation and coupling reactions. Diazonium salts are versatile intermediates.

**Key points**

- Acylation gives amides; alkylation adds alkyl groups.
- The Hinsberg test distinguishes primary, secondary and tertiary amines.
- Diazonium salts form from aniline with nitrous acid at low temperature.
- Diazonium salts undergo coupling to give azo dyes.
- Amines are found in medicines, dyes and proteins.

**Worked examples**

1. **Azo dye formation.** Benzenediazonium chloride couples with phenol to give an orange azo dye.

2. **Hinsberg test.** Primary amines form a soluble product with benzenesulphonyl chloride; tertiary amines do not react.

**Practice questions**

1. Diazonium salts are prepared from aniline at: (A) Low temperature (B) High temperature (C) Boiling water (D) Strong base only
   - *Answer:* Low temperature. Low temperature (0–5 °C) stabilises the diazonium ion.
2. Azo dyes are formed by: (A) Coupling of diazonium salts with phenols (B) Oxidation of amines (C) Reduction of nitro compounds (D) Hydrolysis of amides
   - *Answer:* Coupling of diazonium salts with phenols. Coupling produces the coloured azo group.
3. What is the Hinsberg test used for?
   - *Answer:* It distinguishes primary, secondary and tertiary amines by their reaction with benzenesulphonyl chloride.

## Chapter 12. Biomolecules

### 12.1 Carbohydrates and Proteins

Biomolecules include carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids and vitamins. Their structures determine their roles in life.

**Key points**

- Monosaccharides (glucose, fructose) cannot be hydrolysed further.
- Sucrose is a disaccharide of glucose and fructose; starch and cellulose are polysaccharides.
- Proteins are polymers of amino acids joined by peptide bonds.
- Enzymes are biological catalysts, mostly proteins.
- Denaturation destroys protein structure and function.

**Worked examples**

1. **Reducing sugars.** Glucose reduces Tollens' and Fehling's reagents, while sucrose does not until hydrolysed.

2. **Why boiling an egg is irreversible.** Heat denatures proteins; the folded structure cannot refold after cooling.

**Practice questions**

1. Proteins are polymers of: (A) Amino acids (B) Nucleotides (C) Sugars (D) Fatty acids
   - *Answer:* Amino acids. Peptide bonds link amino acids.
2. Which sugar is a monosaccharide? (A) Glucose (B) Sucrose (C) Maltose (D) Lactose
   - *Answer:* Glucose. Glucose is a single sugar unit.
3. What is denaturation of proteins?
   - *Answer:* Loss of the protein's three-dimensional structure and function due to heat, pH or chemicals.

### 12.2 Nucleic Acids, Vitamins and Hormones

Nucleic acids store genetic information, vitamins and hormones regulate metabolism.

**Key points**

- DNA has a double helix with base pairing A–T and G–C.
- RNA carries and translates genetic information.
- Vitamins are essential in small amounts; deficiency causes specific diseases.
- Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K; water-soluble B and C.
- Hormones are chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands.

**Worked examples**

1. **Base pairing.** Adenine pairs with thymine by two hydrogen bonds, guanine with cytosine by three.

2. **Vitamin C deficiency.** Lack of ascorbic acid causes scurvy, with bleeding gums and poor wound healing.

**Practice questions**

1. DNA bases pair as: (A) A–T and G–C (B) A–G and T–C (C) A–C and T–G (D) A–U and G–C
   - *Answer:* A–T and G–C. Adenine pairs with thymine and guanine with cytosine.
2. Which vitamin is fat-soluble? (A) Vitamin A (B) Vitamin C (C) Vitamin B1 (D) Vitamin B12
   - *Answer:* Vitamin A. Vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fats.
3. Why are vitamins required in small amounts?
   - *Answer:* They act as coenzymes and regulators rather than as energy sources.

## Chapter 13. Polymers

### 13.1 Classification and Polymerisation

Polymers are large molecules formed by joining monomers. Addition and condensation polymerisation produce different types.

**Key points**

- Addition polymers form from unsaturated monomers, e.g. polythene from ethene.
- Condensation polymers form with loss of a small molecule, e.g. nylon from a diamine and diacid.
- Homopolymers have one monomer type; copolymers have more than one.
- Thermoplastics soften on heating; thermosetting plastics do not.
- Natural polymers include starch, cellulose and proteins.

**Worked examples**

1. **Nylon formation.** Hexamethylenediamine plus adipic acid condense to nylon-6,6, losing water and forming strong amide bonds.

2. **Polythene.** Ethene molecules add to each other to form long chains; the polymer is used for bags and bottles.

**Practice questions**

1. Polythene is formed by: (A) Addition polymerisation (B) Condensation polymerisation (C) Hydrolysis (D) Esterification
   - *Answer:* Addition polymerisation. Ethene monomers add without losing atoms.
2. Nylon is a: (A) Condensation polymer (B) Addition polymer (C) Natural polymer (D) Monomer
   - *Answer:* Condensation polymer. It forms with loss of water.
3. Differentiate addition and condensation polymerisation.
   - *Answer:* Addition joins unsaturated monomers without loss of molecules; condensation joins monomers with loss of a small molecule such as water.

### 13.2 Important Polymers and Their Uses

Many polymers are used in daily life, but their persistence raises environmental issues.

**Key points**

- PVC, polystyrene, bakelite and Teflon have distinct properties and uses.
- Bakelite is a thermosetting plastic used in electrical fittings.
- Teflon is non-stick and chemically inert.
- Bakelite, melamine and epoxy are thermosetting.
- Biodegradable polymers and recycling reduce plastic pollution.

**Worked examples**

1. **Teflon in cookware.** PTFE's inertness and low friction make it ideal for non-stick surfaces.

2. **Recycling PET.** PET bottles can be recycled into fibres, saving raw material and energy.

**Practice questions**

1. Which polymer is used for non-stick cookware? (A) Teflon (B) Nylon (C) PVC (D) Polystyrene
   - *Answer:* Teflon. PTFE (Teflon) resists sticking and chemicals.
2. Bakelite is a: (A) Thermosetting plastic (B) Thermoplastic (C) Natural polymer (D) Elastomer
   - *Answer:* Thermosetting plastic. It cannot be remoulded after setting.
3. Why is plastic pollution a concern?
   - *Answer:* Most plastics are non-biodegradable and persist for a very long time, harming wildlife and ecosystems.

## Chapter 14. Chemistry in Everyday Life

### 14.1 Drugs, Antibiotics and Their Action

Drugs are chemicals that interact with biological systems. Their specificity depends on molecular structure.

**Key points**

- Receptors are proteins that bind specific molecules and trigger responses.
- Antacids neutralise stomach acid; antihistamines block histamine receptors.
- Antibiotics kill bacteria through specific targets such as cell-wall synthesis.
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics act on many bacteria; narrow-spectrum on few.
- Drug misuse and overuse can lead to resistance.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why antibiotics do not cure colds.** Colds are viral, and antibiotics target bacterial structures that viruses lack.

2. **Antacid action.** A base such as magnesium hydroxide neutralises excess HCl, relieving acid indigestion.

**Practice questions**

1. Antacids are: (A) Bases that neutralise stomach acid (B) Acids (C) Enzymes (D) Hormones
   - *Answer:* Bases that neutralise stomach acid. They reduce acidity in the stomach.
2. Antibiotic resistance develops because: (A) Bacteria evolve resistance under selection (B) Antibiotics expire (C) Drugs are powders (D) Diseases change names
   - *Answer:* Bacteria evolve resistance under selection. Surviving bacteria multiply and pass on resistance genes.
3. Why is antibiotic overuse a public health problem?
   - *Answer:* It selects resistant bacteria, making infections harder to treat.

### 14.2 Food Additives, Cleansing Agents and Environmental Impact

Chemicals improve food quality and hygiene but can have environmental consequences.

**Key points**

- Preservatives prevent microbial spoilage; antioxidants prevent oxidation.
- Artificial sweeteners add sweetness without much energy.
- Soaps are salts of fatty acids; detergents are synthetic surfactants.
- Detergents work in hard water; soaps do not lather well in hard water.
- Non-biodegradable detergents and fertilisers pollute water bodies.

**Worked examples**

1. **Soap in hard water.** Calcium ions form an insoluble scum with soap, wasting soap and reducing cleaning.

2. **Eutrophication from detergents.** Phosphate-rich detergents enrich lakes with nutrients, causing algal blooms and oxygen depletion.

**Practice questions**

1. Soaps are salts of: (A) Fatty acids (B) Petroleum sulphonates (C) Alcohols (D) Amines
   - *Answer:* Fatty acids. Soap is a sodium or potassium salt of a fatty acid.
2. Detergents are better than soaps in hard water because: (A) They do not form scum (B) They are cheaper (C) They dissolve faster (D) They are natural
   - *Answer:* They do not form scum. Synthetic surfactants remain soluble in hard water.
3. Why are phosphates in detergents harmful?
   - *Answer:* They promote eutrophication, leading to algal blooms and oxygen depletion in water bodies.
