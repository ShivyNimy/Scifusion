## Chapter 1. Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry

### 1.1 Mole Concept and Stoichiometry

Chemistry counts particles in moles. The mole links mass, number of particles and volumes of gases, making quantitative reactions possible.

**Key points**

- 1 mole = 6.022 × 10<sup>23</sup> particles (Avogadro's number).
- Molar mass is the mass in grams of one mole of a substance.
- Moles n = mass ÷ molar mass.
- At STP, 1 mole of an ideal gas occupies 22.4 L.
- Percentage composition = (mass of element ÷ molar mass) × 100.

**Worked examples**

1. **Moles of water.** 36 g of water contains 36/18 = 2 mol, i.e. 1.204 × 10<sup>24</sup> molecules.

2. **Empirical formula.** A compound with 40% C, 6.7% H and 53.3% O gives a mole ratio 1 : 2 : 1, so the empirical formula is CH<sub>2</sub>O.

**Practice questions**

1. Avogadro's number is: (A) 6.022 × 10<sup>23</sup> (B) 6.022 × 10<sup>22</sup> (C) 3.14 × 10<sup>23</sup> (D) 1.6 × 10<sup>19</sup>
   - *Answer:* 6.022 × 10<sup>23</sup>. One mole contains 6.022 × 10<sup>23</sup> particles.
2. Number of moles in 4 g of helium (M = 4 g/mol) is: (A) 1 (B) 2 (C) 4 (D) 0.5
   - *Answer:* 1. n = 4/4 = 1 mol.
3. Calculate the number of molecules in 9 g of water.
   - *Answer:* Moles = 9/18 = 0.5; molecules = 0.5 × 6.022 × 10<sup>23</sup> = 3.011 × 10<sup>23</sup>.

### 1.2 Stoichiometry and Limiting Reagents

Balanced equations give mole ratios. The limiting reagent determines how much product can form.

**Key points**

- A balanced equation gives the ratio in which reactants combine and products form.
- The limiting reagent is completely consumed first and controls the yield.
- Theoretical yield is calculated from stoichiometry; actual yield is measured.
- Percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100.
- Excess reagent remains after the reaction completes.

**Worked examples**

1. **Hydrogen and oxygen.** 2H<sub>2</sub> + O<sub>2</sub> -> 2H<sub>2</sub>O. If 4 mol H<sub>2</sub> reacts with 1 mol O<sub>2</sub>, hydrogen is in excess and only 2 mol of water can form from the oxygen available.

2. **Percentage yield.** If a reaction should give 20 g but gives 16 g, the yield is (16/20) × 100 = 80%.

**Practice questions**

1. The limiting reagent is the one that: (A) Is completely consumed first (B) Is in excess (C) Has the largest mass (D) Has the largest molar mass
   - *Answer:* Is completely consumed first. It limits the amount of product formed.
2. Percentage yield is calculated as: (A) Actual/theoretical × 100 (B) Theoretical/actual × 100 (C) Actual × theoretical (D) Actual + theoretical
   - *Answer:* Actual/theoretical × 100. It compares actual and theoretical yields.
3. In 2H<sub>2</sub> + O<sub>2</sub> -> 2H<sub>2</sub>O, how many moles of water form from 2 mol H<sub>2</sub> and 2 mol O<sub>2</sub>?
   - *Answer:* H<sub>2</sub> is limiting; 2 mol H<sub>2</sub> gives 2 mol H<sub>2</sub>O, leaving 1 mol O<sub>2</sub> unused.

## Chapter 2. Structure of Atom

### 2.1 Quantum Numbers and Orbitals

Quantum mechanics describes electrons in orbitals defined by four quantum numbers. Orbitals have shapes and energies.

**Key points**

- Principal quantum number n gives the shell and energy level.
- Azimuthal quantum number l gives the subshell (s, p, d, f).
- Magnetic quantum number m<sub>l</sub> gives orbital orientation; spin m<sub>s</sub> is ±½.
- Number of orbitals in a subshell = 2l + 1; each orbital holds 2 electrons.
- Aufbau principle: electrons fill lower-energy orbitals first.

**Worked examples**

1. **Quantum numbers of a 2p electron.** n = 2, l = 1, m<sub>l</sub> can be −1, 0 or +1, and m<sub>s</sub> is ±½. The 2p subshell has 3 orbitals and holds 6 electrons.

2. **Filling chromium.** Chromium is [Ar]3d<sup>5</sup>4s<sup>1</sup> rather than 3d<sup>4</sup>4s<sup>2</sup> because half-filled d orbitals are extra stable.

**Practice questions**

1. The maximum number of electrons in a d subshell is: (A) 10 (B) 6 (C) 2 (D) 14
   - *Answer:* 10. 5 orbitals × 2 electrons = 10.
2. Which quantum number describes orbital shape? (A) Azimuthal (B) Principal (C) Spin (D) Magnetic
   - *Answer:* Azimuthal. l determines the subshell type: s, p, d, f.
3. State the Pauli exclusion principle.
   - *Answer:* No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers.

### 2.2 Electronic Configuration and Periodic Trends

Electron configurations explain the periodic table and trends such as atomic radius, ionisation energy and electronegativity.

**Key points**

- Electrons fill 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d … following the (n + l) rule.
- Atomic radius decreases across a period and increases down a group.
- Ionisation energy increases across a period and decreases down a group.
- Electronegativity increases across a period and decreases down a group.
- Hund's rule: degenerate orbitals fill singly first with parallel spins.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why sodium is more reactive than magnesium.** Sodium loses its single 3s electron easily, while magnesium needs to lose two. Lower ionisation energy makes sodium more reactive.

2. **Why noble gases are inert.** Their filled outer shells give high ionisation energies and no tendency to gain or lose electrons.

**Practice questions**

1. Across a period, atomic radius generally: (A) Decreases (B) Increases (C) Remains constant (D) Doubles
   - *Answer:* Decreases. Increasing nuclear charge pulls electrons closer.
2. Which has the largest atomic radius? (A) Potassium (B) Sodium (C) Lithium (D) Hydrogen
   - *Answer:* Potassium. Radius increases down the group.
3. Why does ionisation energy decrease down a group?
   - *Answer:* The outer electron is farther from the nucleus and shielded by more inner shells, so it is easier to remove.

## Chapter 3. Classification of Elements and Periodicity

### 3.1 The Modern Periodic Table and Its Trends

Elements are arranged by atomic number. Position determines properties, and trends recur periodically.

**Key points**

- Periods are horizontal rows; groups are vertical columns.
- Elements in a group have the same valence electron configuration.
- Metallic character decreases across a period and increases down a group.
- Anomalies exist, such as the similar chemistry of lithium and magnesium (diagonal relationship).
- The table separates s, p, d and f blocks by the subshell being filled.

**Worked examples**

1. **Diagonal relationship.** Lithium and magnesium show similar chemistry because of comparable charge/radius ratios, even though they are in different groups.

2. **Noble gas placement.** Group 18 elements have complete octets and very high ionisation energies, giving them their characteristic inertness.

**Practice questions**

1. Elements in the same group have the same: (A) Number of valence electrons (B) Atomic mass (C) Number of shells (D) Atomic radius
   - *Answer:* Number of valence electrons. Group number reflects valence configuration.
2. Metallic character ______ down a group. (A) Increases (B) Decreases (C) Stays constant (D) Vanishes
   - *Answer:* Increases. Atoms lose electrons more easily as size increases.
3. Why does electronegativity increase across a period?
   - *Answer:* Effective nuclear charge increases while atomic size decreases, so atoms attract bonding electrons more strongly.

### 3.2 Periodic Properties in Detail

Ionisation enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy and valence describe reactivity and bonding.

**Key points**

- First ionisation enthalpy is the energy to remove the most loosely bound electron.
- Electron gain enthalpy is the energy change when an electron is added.
- Group 17 elements have very negative electron gain enthalpies, making them reactive non-metals.
- Valence is the number of electrons an atom gains, loses or shares to complete its octet.
- Atomic and ionic radii vary with effective nuclear charge and shielding.

**Worked examples**

1. **Fluorine versus chlorine electron gain.** Fluorine's small size causes electron-electron repulsion, so chlorine has a more negative electron gain enthalpy than fluorine.

2. **Radius of cation versus atom.** Na<sup>+</sup> is smaller than Na because losing an electron reduces repulsion and the same nuclear charge pulls the remaining electrons closer.

**Practice questions**

1. A cation is ______ than its parent atom. (A) Smaller (B) Larger (C) Equal in size (D) Twice as large
   - *Answer:* Smaller. Loss of electrons and reduced repulsion contract the ion.
2. Which element has the highest first ionisation enthalpy? (A) Helium (B) Sodium (C) Potassium (D) Caesium
   - *Answer:* Helium. Helium's tightly held 1s electrons need the most energy to remove.
3. Why is the second ionisation energy of sodium much greater than the first?
   - *Answer:* Removing a second electron from a stable noble-gas configuration requires far more energy.

## Chapter 4. Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

### 4.1 Ionic and Covalent Bonding, VSEPR

Atoms bond to achieve stable electron configurations. VSEPR theory predicts molecular shapes from electron-pair repulsion.

**Key points**

- Ionic bonds form by complete transfer of electrons between metals and non-metals.
- Covalent bonds form by sharing electrons, giving single, double or triple bonds.
- VSEPR: electron pairs repel and arrange to minimise repulsion.
- Lone pairs repel more strongly than bond pairs, compressing bond angles.
- Common shapes: linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal and bent.

**Worked examples**

1. **Water's bent shape.** Oxygen has two lone pairs in water, which push the O–H bonds closer, making the bond angle about 104.5° instead of the tetrahedral 109.5°.

2. **Methane versus ammonia.** Methane (CH<sub>4</sub>) is tetrahedral at 109.5°; ammonia (NH<sub>3</sub>) has one lone pair, so it is trigonal pyramidal with an angle near 107°.

**Practice questions**

1. The shape of a methane molecule is: (A) Tetrahedral (B) Square planar (C) Linear (D) Trigonal planar
   - *Answer:* Tetrahedral. Four bonding pairs arrange tetrahedrally.
2. Lone pair–lone pair repulsion is: (A) Stronger than bond pair–bond pair (B) Weaker (C) Equal (D) Zero
   - *Answer:* Stronger than bond pair–bond pair. Lone pairs occupy more space near the central atom.
3. Predict the shape of BF<sub>3</sub>.
   - *Answer:* Boron has three bonding pairs and no lone pairs, so they spread 120° apart.

### 4.2 Hybridisation, Molecular Orbital Theory and Bond Parameters

Hybridisation explains geometry while molecular orbital theory explains magnetism and bond order. Bond length, order and energy describe bond strength.

**Key points**

- sp: linear (180°); sp<sup>2</sup>: trigonal planar (120°); sp<sup>3</sup>: tetrahedral (109.5°).
- Bond order = (bonding electrons − antibonding electrons)/2.
- Higher bond order means shorter, stronger bonds.
- O<sub>2</sub> is paramagnetic because of unpaired electrons in π* orbitals.
- Dipole moment measures bond polarity and molecular polarity.

**Worked examples**

1. **Bond order of O<sub>2</sub>.** MO theory gives bond order 2 and two unpaired electrons, correctly predicting that oxygen is paramagnetic.

2. **Why CO<sub>2</sub> is nonpolar.** Each C=O bond is polar, but the linear shape makes the two dipoles cancel, so the molecule has zero net dipole moment.

**Practice questions**

1. Hybridisation in ethene is: (A) sp<sup>2</sup> (B) sp<sup>3</sup> (C) sp (D) sp<sup>3</sup>d
   - *Answer:* sp<sup>2</sup>. The C=C double bond uses sp<sup>2</sup> hybrid orbitals with a π bond.
2. Bond order of N<sub>2</sub> is: (A) 3 (B) 2 (C) 1 (D) 4
   - *Answer:* 3. The triple bond gives bond order 3.
3. Why is the bond angle in water less than in ammonia?
   - *Answer:* Water has two lone pairs versus one in ammonia, so lone-pair repulsion compresses the angle more.

## Chapter 5. States of Matter

### 5.1 Gas Laws and the Ideal Gas Equation

Gas behaviour is described by Boyle's, Charles' and Avogadro's laws, combined in the ideal gas equation.

**Key points**

- Boyle's law: PV = constant at constant T.
- Charles' law: V/T = constant at constant P.
- Avogadro's law: V ∝ n at constant T and P.
- Ideal gas equation: PV = nRT, with R = 8.314 J/mol·K = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K.
- Dalton's law: total pressure is the sum of partial pressures.

**Worked examples**

1. **Gas volume change.** 1 mol of gas at 1 atm occupies 22.4 L at 273 K. At 546 K and the same pressure it occupies 44.8 L.

2. **Using the ideal gas law.** 2 mol at 300 K in 10 L: P = nRT/V = 2 × 0.0821 × 300/10 ≈ 4.93 atm.

**Practice questions**

1. Boyle's law states that at constant temperature: (A) PV is constant (B) V/T is constant (C) P/T is constant (D) n is constant
   - *Answer:* PV is constant. Pressure and volume are inversely proportional.
2. The value of R in SI units is: (A) 8.314 J/mol·K (B) 0.0821 J/mol·K (C) 1.987 J/mol·K (D) 22.4 J/mol·K
   - *Answer:* 8.314 J/mol·K. R = 8.314 J/mol·K in SI units.
3. A gas occupies 2 L at 1 atm. What volume at 2 atm, temperature unchanged?
   - *Answer:* PV = constant, so V = 2 × 1/2 = 1 L.

### 5.2 Kinetic Molecular Theory and Real Gases

Kinetic theory links molecular motion to pressure and temperature; real gases deviate from ideal behaviour and are described by the van der Waals equation.

**Key points**

- Gas pressure arises from molecular collisions with walls.
- Average kinetic energy is proportional to absolute temperature.
- Real gases deviate at high pressure and low temperature due to molecular volume and attractions.
- van der Waals equation: (P + an<sup>2</sup>/V<sup>2</sup>)(V − nb) = nRT.
- Critical temperature is the temperature above which a gas cannot be liquefied by pressure.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why helium liquefies last.** Helium has very weak intermolecular forces, so its critical temperature is extremely low (about 5.2 K).

2. **Deviation of a real gas.** Attractions reduce pressure and molecular volume reduces free space, so real gases deviate most when molecules are close together.

**Practice questions**

1. Real gases behave most ideally: (A) At low pressure and high temperature (B) At high pressure (C) At low temperature (D) At critical point
   - *Answer:* At low pressure and high temperature. Molecules are far apart and interact little.
2. The van der Waals constant 'b' accounts for: (A) Molecular volume (B) Molecular attraction (C) Kinetic energy (D) Temperature
   - *Answer:* Molecular volume. b corrects for the finite volume of molecules.
3. Why does a real gas deviate from ideal behaviour at high pressure?
   - *Answer:* Molecules are forced close together, so their finite volume and intermolecular attractions become significant.

## Chapter 6. Thermodynamics

### 6.1 First Law and Enthalpy

Thermodynamics studies energy changes. Enthalpy measures heat exchanged at constant pressure.

**Key points**

- First law: ΔU = q + w, where w is work done on the system.
- Work done on a gas: w = −PΔV.
- Enthalpy H = U + PV; ΔH = q<sub>p</sub> at constant pressure.
- Exothermic reactions have ΔH < 0; endothermic have ΔH > 0.
- Hess's law: enthalpy change is independent of the route.

**Worked examples**

1. **Hess's law calculation.** If the enthalpy of formation of CO<sub>2</sub> is −394 kJ/mol and of CO is −111 kJ/mol, the combustion of CO releases 283 kJ/mol.

2. **Enthalpy of a reaction.** ΔH = ΣΔH<sub>f</sub>(products) − ΣΔH<sub>f</sub>(reactants) gives the heat of reaction from formation enthalpies.

**Practice questions**

1. For an exothermic reaction, ΔH is: (A) Negative (B) Positive (C) Zero (D) Undefined
   - *Answer:* Negative. Heat is released, so enthalpy decreases.
2. Hess's law follows from: (A) Conservation of energy (B) Conservation of mass (C) Ideal gas law (D) Le Chatelier's principle
   - *Answer:* Conservation of energy. Enthalpy is a state function, so the route does not matter.
3. State the first law of thermodynamics.
   - *Answer:* Energy can be exchanged as heat or work, but the total energy of the universe is conserved: ΔU = q + w.

### 6.2 Entropy, Gibbs Energy and Spontaneity

The second law introduces entropy. Gibbs energy combines enthalpy and entropy to predict spontaneity.

**Key points**

- Entropy S is a measure of disorder; ΔS<sub>universe</sub> > 0 for a spontaneous process.
- Gibbs energy G = H − TS.
- ΔG < 0 means spontaneous; ΔG = 0 means equilibrium; ΔG > 0 means non-spontaneous.
- ΔG° = −RT ln K relates free energy to the equilibrium constant.
- Exothermic reactions with increasing entropy are always spontaneous.

**Worked examples**

1. **Melting ice.** Above 0 °C, ΔG for melting is negative because the entropy gain outweighs the enthalpy cost; below 0 °C the reverse is true.

2. **Thermodynamic versus kinetic control.** A reaction may be thermodynamically favourable (ΔG < 0) but very slow; a catalyst affects only the rate.

**Practice questions**

1. A spontaneous reaction has: (A) ΔG < 0 (B) ΔG > 0 (C) ΔG = 0 (D) ΔH > 0 always
   - *Answer:* ΔG < 0. Negative Gibbs energy means spontaneity.
2. Entropy is a measure of: (A) Disorder (B) Heat content (C) Pressure (D) Volume
   - *Answer:* Disorder. Higher disorder means higher entropy.
3. Why is the combustion of fuels spontaneous even though it releases heat?
   - *Answer:* It is strongly exothermic and increases entropy of the surroundings, giving ΔG < 0.

## Chapter 7. Equilibrium

### 7.1 Chemical Equilibrium and Le Chatelier's Principle

Reversible reactions reach equilibrium when forward and backward rates are equal. Le Chatelier's principle predicts the response to change.

**Key points**

- At equilibrium, concentrations are constant but reactions continue in both directions.
- K<sub>c</sub> = [products]<sup>n</sup>/[reactants]<sup>m</sup> for a balanced reaction.
- K<sub>p</sub> = K<sub>c</sub>(RT)<sup>Δn</sup> for gases.
- Le Chatelier: a system shifts to oppose the change imposed.
- A catalyst speeds up both directions equally and does not change K.

**Worked examples**

1. **Haber process.** N<sub>2</sub> + 3H<sub>2</sub> ⇌ 2NH<sub>3</sub> is exothermic and reduces gas moles, so high pressure and moderate temperature favour ammonia.

2. **Effect of removing product.** Removing a product shifts the equilibrium to the right, increasing the forward reaction until equilibrium is re-established.

**Practice questions**

1. Adding a catalyst to an equilibrium: (A) Does not change the equilibrium constant (B) Increases K (C) Decreases K (D) Shifts the equilibrium right
   - *Answer:* Does not change the equilibrium constant. It speeds both forward and reverse reactions equally.
2. Increasing pressure favours the side with: (A) Fewer moles of gas (B) More moles of gas (C) Equal moles (D) No gas
   - *Answer:* Fewer moles of gas. Le Chatelier's principle favours the smaller volume.
3. How does increasing temperature affect an exothermic equilibrium?
   - *Answer:* It shifts the equilibrium to the left, decreasing the product concentration.

### 7.2 Ionic Equilibrium: Acids, Bases and Buffers

Acids and bases ionise in water. pH, buffers and solubility product describe ionic equilibria.

**Key points**

- K<sub>w</sub> = [H<sup>+</sup>][OH<sup>−</sup>] = 10<sup>−14</sup> at 25 °C.
- pH = −log[H<sup>+</sup>]; pOH = −log[OH<sup>−</sup>]; pH + pOH = 14.
- Strong acids dissociate completely; weak acids partially (K<sub>a</sub> small).
- A buffer resists pH change and works best near pK<sub>a</sub>.
- Henderson-Hasselbalch: pH = pK<sub>a</sub> + log([salt]/[acid]).

**Worked examples**

1. **pH of a strong acid.** 0.01 M HCl gives [H<sup>+</sup>] = 10<sup>−2</sup> M and pH = 2.

2. **Buffer in blood.** The carbonic acid–bicarbonate buffer keeps blood pH near 7.4, protecting enzymes from pH swings.

**Practice questions**

1. The pH of a neutral solution at 25 °C is: (A) 7 (B) 0 (C) 14 (D) 1
   - *Answer:* 7. [H<sup>+</sup>] = [OH<sup>−</sup>] = 10<sup>−7</sup> M at 25 °C.
2. A buffer works best when: (A) pH ≈ pK<sub>a</sub> (B) pH = 0 (C) pH = 14 (D) The acid is strong
   - *Answer:* pH ≈ pK<sub>a</sub>. Maximum buffering capacity is near the pK<sub>a</sub>.
3. Calculate the pH of 0.001 M HCl.
   - *Answer:* pH = −log(10<sup>−3</sup>) = 3.

## Chapter 8. Redox Reactions

### 8.1 Oxidation Numbers and Redox

Redox reactions involve changes in oxidation state. Oxidation numbers track electron transfer.

**Key points**

- Oxidation is loss of electrons (increase in oxidation number); reduction is gain.
- Oxidation numbers follow rules: pure elements 0, oxygen usually −2, hydrogen usually +1.
- Disproportionation: one element is simultaneously oxidised and reduced.
- Oxidising agents are reduced; reducing agents are oxidised.
- Balancing redox reactions uses the ion-electron method.

**Worked examples**

1. **Oxidation number of Mn in KMnO<sub>4</sub>.** +1 + x + 4(−2) = 0 gives x = +7.

2. **Chlorine in bleach.** Chlorine (0) in Cl<sub>2</sub> disproportionates to Cl<sup>−</sup> (−1) and ClO<sup>−</sup> (+1) in alkaline solution.

**Practice questions**

1. Oxidation involves: (A) Loss of electrons (B) Gain of electrons (C) No change (D) Gain of protons
   - *Answer:* Loss of electrons. Oxidation number increases as electrons are lost.
2. The oxidation number of oxygen in most compounds is: (A) −2 (B) +2 (C) 0 (D) −1
   - *Answer:* −2. Oxygen is usually −2 except in peroxides.
3. Find the oxidation number of sulphur in H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>.
   - *Answer:* 2(+1) + x + 4(−2) = 0 gives x = +6.

### 8.2 Redox Titrations and Electrode Potentials

Redox reactions power titrations and electrochemical cells. Electrode potentials rank oxidising and reducing strength.

**Key points**

- In a redox titration, a standard solution of known concentration oxidises or reduces the analyte.
- Standard electrode potentials E° rank half-reactions by tendency to gain electrons.
- A species with a higher E° is a stronger oxidising agent.
- Cell potential E°<sub>cell</sub> = E°<sub>cathode</sub> − E°<sub>anode</sub>.
- Redox reactions are central to corrosion and biological respiration.

**Worked examples**

1. **Permanganate titration.** KMnO<sub>4</sub> in acidic medium oxidises Fe<sup>2+</sup> to Fe<sup>3+</sup>, and the purple colour fades at the end-point.

2. **Why zinc displaces copper.** Zinc has a more negative reduction potential, so it is oxidised while copper ions are reduced and deposited.

**Practice questions**

1. In a galvanic cell, oxidation occurs at the: (A) Anode (B) Cathode (C) Salt bridge (D) Voltmeter
   - *Answer:* Anode. Anodes are where oxidation happens in all electrochemical cells.
2. A stronger oxidising agent has: (A) Higher reduction potential (B) Lower reduction potential (C) Zero potential (D) Negative mass
   - *Answer:* Higher reduction potential. Higher E° means greater tendency to be reduced.
3. Define a redox reaction and give one example.
   - *Answer:* A reaction in which oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously, e.g. Zn + CuSO<sub>4</sub> -> ZnSO<sub>4</sub> + Cu.

## Chapter 9. Hydrogen

### 9.1 Hydrogen and Its Compounds

Hydrogen has unique chemistry with three isotopes. It forms hydrides and is important in energy and industry.

**Key points**

- Isotopes: protium, deuterium and tritium.
- Hydrides: ionic (NaH), covalent (HCl) and interstitial (with transition metals).
- Hydrogen resembles alkali metals and halogens in some reactions, so its position is unique.
- Heavy water (D<sub>2</sub>O) is used as a moderator in nuclear reactors.
- Hydrogen is studied as a clean fuel, producing only water on combustion.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why hydrogen has two positions.** It loses one electron like alkali metals and gains one like halogens, so it can be placed with either group.

2. **Hydrogen as fuel.** Combustion gives 2H<sub>2</sub> + O<sub>2</sub> -> 2H<sub>2</sub>O, releasing energy with no carbon dioxide, but storage and production costs are challenges.

**Practice questions**

1. Heavy water contains: (A) Deuterium (B) Tritium only (C) Protium only (D) Helium
   - *Answer:* Deuterium. D<sub>2</sub>O contains deuterium instead of protium.
2. Hydrogen can act as: (A) Both an oxidising and reducing agent (B) Only oxidising (C) Only reducing (D) Neither
   - *Answer:* Both an oxidising and reducing agent. It can gain or lose one electron.
3. Why is hydrogen called a clean fuel?
   - *Answer:* It burns to give only water, with no carbon dioxide or soot.

### 9.2 Water, Hydrogen Peroxide and Hard Water

Water has unusual properties due to hydrogen bonding. Hydrogen peroxide is a useful oxidant, and hard water contains calcium and magnesium ions.

**Key points**

- Water is a polar molecule with strong hydrogen bonds, giving high boiling point and surface tension.
- Hardness is temporary (bicarbonates) or permanent (sulphates and chlorides of Ca and Mg).
- Water hardness is determined by EDTA titration.
- H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> is a strong oxidant and decomposes to water and oxygen.
- Water pollution and purification involve filtration, chlorination and aeration.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why ice floats.** Hydrogen-bonded water molecules form an open lattice in ice, making it less dense than liquid water.

2. **Bleaching with H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>.** Hydrogen peroxide bleaches by oxidation and breaks down into water and oxygen, making it an environment-friendly bleach.

**Practice questions**

1. Temporary hardness of water is due to: (A) Calcium and magnesium bicarbonates (B) Chlorides (C) Sulphates (D) Sodium salts
   - *Answer:* Calcium and magnesium bicarbonates. Bicarbonates can be removed by boiling.
2. Hydrogen peroxide decomposes into: (A) Water and oxygen (B) Hydrogen and oxygen (C) Water only (D) Ozone
   - *Answer:* Water and oxygen. 2H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> -> 2H<sub>2</sub>O + O<sub>2</sub>.
3. Why does water have a high boiling point?
   - *Answer:* Extensive hydrogen bonding requires a lot of energy to break before boiling.

## Chapter 10. The s-Block Elements

### 10.1 Alkali and Alkaline Earth Metals

Group 1 and 2 metals are highly reactive, with trends in atomic size, ionisation energy and solubility.

**Key points**

- Alkali metals (Li–Cs) react vigorously with water to give hydroxides and hydrogen.
- Alkaline earth metals (Be–Ba) are less reactive but still reactive.
- Reactivity increases down both groups as ionisation energy decreases.
- Hydroxide solubility increases down group 2 for most compounds; sulphates decrease.
- Flame colours help identify these metals.

**Worked examples**

1. **Sodium in water.** 2Na + 2H<sub>2</sub>O -> 2NaOH + H<sub>2</sub> with vigorous fizzing and a yellow flame; potassium is even more violent.

2. **Solubility trend.** Beryllium hydroxide is nearly insoluble while barium hydroxide is quite soluble, due to lattice and hydration enthalpy changes.

**Practice questions**

1. Which group 1 metal reacts most vigorously with water? (A) Caesium (B) Lithium (C) Sodium (D) Potassium
   - *Answer:* Caesium. Reactivity increases down the group.
2. Magnesium burns in air with a: (A) Brilliant white flame (B) Green flame (C) Blue flame (D) No flame
   - *Answer:* Brilliant white flame. Magnesium gives a bright white light.
3. Why are alkali metals stored in kerosene?
   - *Answer:* They react rapidly with air and moisture, so kerosene keeps them dry and unreactive.

### 10.2 Important Compounds and Biological Roles

Compounds of s-block metals are central to industry and life: sodium chloride, limestone, sodium hydroxide and calcium phosphate.

**Key points**

- Sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) is made by electrolysis of brine and used in soap making.
- Sodium carbonate is used in glass and detergents.
- Calcium oxide (quicklime) and calcium hydroxide are used in mortar and whitewash.
- Calcium and potassium ions are essential for nerves, muscles and bones.
- Excess sodium in the diet is linked to high blood pressure.

**Worked examples**

1. **Limestone in construction.** Calcium carbonate decomposes to CaO and CO<sub>2</sub> on heating; CaO with water gives slaked lime, used in mortar.

2. **Potassium in nerves.** Potassium ions maintain the resting potential of nerve cells; imbalance disturbs heartbeat and nerve signals.

**Practice questions**

1. Caustic soda is: (A) Sodium hydroxide (B) Sodium carbonate (C) Sodium chloride (D) Sodium bicarbonate
   - *Answer:* Sodium hydroxide. NaOH is known as caustic soda.
2. Which compound is used to whitewash walls? (A) Calcium hydroxide (B) Calcium carbonate (C) Sodium chloride (D) Magnesium sulphate
   - *Answer:* Calcium hydroxide. Slaked lime forms a white protective layer.
3. Why is sodium important in the human body?
   - *Answer:* Sodium maintains fluid balance, nerve impulses and muscle contraction.

## Chapter 11. The p-Block Elements

### 11.1 Group 13 and 14 Elements

Boron and carbon groups show varied bonding and oxidation states, from covalent to metallic characters.

**Key points**

- Group 13: B, Al, Ga, In, Tl; aluminium is the most important industrial metal.
- Group 14: C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb; carbon shows catenation and silicon is the backbone of semiconductors.
- Aluminium oxide is amphoteric; it reacts with acids and bases.
- Carbon exists as diamond, graphite and fullerenes with very different properties.
- Inert pair effect reduces the stability of the highest oxidation state down the group.

**Worked examples**

1. **Diamond versus graphite.** Diamond is hard because each carbon is tetrahedrally bonded; graphite is slippery because layers are weakly held and conduct electricity along the layers.

2. **Silicon semiconductors.** Silicon's four valence electrons form a stable crystal lattice whose conductivity can be controlled by doping.

**Practice questions**

1. Which is the hardest natural substance? (A) Diamond (B) Graphite (C) Quartz (D) Silicon
   - *Answer:* Diamond. Diamond's rigid covalent network makes it hardest.
2. Aluminium oxide is: (A) Amphoteric (B) Acidic only (C) Basic only (D) Neutral
   - *Answer:* Amphoteric. It reacts with both acids and bases.
3. Why does graphite conduct electricity?
   - *Answer:* Each carbon has a delocalised electron that is free to move along the layers.

### 11.2 Group 15 to 18 Elements

Nitrogen, oxygen, halogens and noble gases show trends in bonding, reactivity and physical state.

**Key points**

- Group 15: N, P, As, Sb, Bi — nitrogen is a gas and phosphorus forms P<sub>4</sub> molecules.
- Group 16: O, S, Se, Te, Po — oxygen is a gas and sulphur forms S<sub>8</sub> rings.
- Halogens (17) are coloured, reactive non-metals; reactivity decreases down the group.
- Noble gases are monatomic with very low reactivity; xenon forms some compounds.
- Allotropes are important: oxygen/ozone, phosphorus white/red, sulphur rhombic/monoclinic.

**Worked examples**

1. **Ozone versus oxygen.** Ozone (O<sub>3</sub>) is a pale blue, reactive gas that absorbs ultraviolet light; oxygen (O<sub>2</sub>) is essential for respiration.

2. **Halogen displacement.** Chlorine displaces bromide from bromide solutions because chlorine is more reactive, showing the trend down group 17.

**Practice questions**

1. Which halogen is the most reactive? (A) Fluorine (B) Chlorine (C) Bromine (D) Iodine
   - *Answer:* Fluorine. Reactivity decreases down group 17.
2. Phosphorus is stored in: (A) Water (B) Kerosene (C) Acid (D) Alcohol
   - *Answer:* Water. White phosphorus reacts with air and is kept under water.
3. Why are noble gases chemically inert?
   - *Answer:* They have filled outer shells with very high ionisation energies and no tendency to gain electrons.

## Chapter 12. Organic Chemistry: Some Basic Principles

### 12.1 Representation and Isomerism

Organic compounds are based on carbon skeletons. Formulas can be condensed, expanded or shown as structures, and isomers share a formula but differ in arrangement.

**Key points**

- Structural isomers differ in connectivity; stereoisomers differ in spatial arrangement.
- Functional groups determine chemical properties.
- Skeletal structures show the carbon backbone with hydrogens implied.
- IUPAC names are systematic: prefix + root + suffix.
- Degree of unsaturation indicates rings and double/triple bonds.

**Worked examples**

1. **Naming a compound.** CH<sub>3</sub>–CH<sub>2</sub>–OH is ethanol: a two-carbon root with an alcohol (−OH) functional group.

2. **Structural isomers of butane.** Butane (straight chain) and 2-methylpropane (branched) share C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>10</sub> but have different structures and boiling points.

**Practice questions**

1. Isomers have the same: (A) Molecular formula (B) Structure (C) Boiling point (D) Functional group
   - *Answer:* Molecular formula. Isomers differ in Arrangement but share the formula.
2. The IUPAC suffix for an alcohol is: (A) −ol (B) −oic acid (C) −al (D) −one
   - *Answer:* −ol. Alcohols are named with the suffix −ol.
3. Write the IUPAC name of CH<sub>3</sub>COOH.
   - *Answer:* Two carbons with a −COOH group gives ethanoic acid.

### 12.2 Electronic Effects and Reaction Types

Organic reactions are classified by how bonds break and form. Inductive, resonance and hyperconjugation effects explain stability and reactivity.

**Key points**

- Homolytic cleavage gives radicals; heterolytic gives ions.
- Inductive effect: electron withdrawal or donation along sigma bonds.
- Resonance delocalises π electrons over several atoms.
- Electrophiles are electron-poor; nucleophiles are electron-rich.
- Reactions: substitution, addition, elimination and rearrangement.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why benzene is stable.** Delocalised π electrons around the ring (resonance) give benzene extra stability, so it undergoes substitution rather than addition.

2. **Markovnikov's rule.** In addition of HX to propene, hydrogen adds to the carbon with more hydrogens, giving the more stable carbocation intermediate.

**Practice questions**

1. A nucleophile is: (A) Electron-rich (B) Electron-poor (C) Always positive (D) A radical
   - *Answer:* Electron-rich. Nucleophiles donate electron pairs to electron-poor centres.
2. Benzene undergoes mainly: (A) Substitution (B) Addition (C) Elimination (D) Rearrangement
   - *Answer:* Substitution. Aromatic stability favours substitution over addition.
3. Why does benzene not decolourise bromine water?
   - *Answer:* Its delocalised π system is stable, so it does not undergo addition like alkenes.

## Chapter 13. Hydrocarbons

### 13.1 Alkanes, Alkenes and Alkynes

Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen. Saturation determines their reactions and properties.

**Key points**

- Alkanes are saturated and undergo substitution by free radicals.
- Alkenes have C=C double bonds and undergo addition reactions.
- Alkynes have C≡C triple bonds and also undergo addition; they are acidic at terminal carbons.
- Markovnikov's rule predicts the major product in addition to alkenes.
- Boiling points increase with chain length and branching lowers them.

**Worked examples**

1. **Bromine water test.** Bromine water decolourises with an alkene or alkyne (addition) but not with an alkane, so it distinguishes unsaturated from saturated hydrocarbons.

2. **Combustion and soot.** Alkanes burn with a clean flame when oxygen is plentiful; alkynes with more carbon content tend to produce smoky flames.

**Practice questions**

1. Alkanes react with halogens by: (A) Substitution (B) Addition (C) Elimination (D) Oxidation only
   - *Answer:* Substitution. Free-radical substitution replaces a hydrogen.
2. Which hydrocarbon decolourises bromine water? (A) Ethene (B) Ethane (C) Propane (D) Methane
   - *Answer:* Ethene. Unsaturated hydrocarbons add bromine and decolourise the solution.
3. Why do alkanes make good fuels?
   - *Answer:* They are highly reduced and release a lot of heat on complete combustion.

### 13.2 Aromatic Hydrocarbons

Benzene and its derivatives are aromatic: planar rings with delocalised π electrons conferring extra stability.

**Key points**

- Aromatic compounds follow Hückel's rule: 4n + 2 delocalised π electrons.
- Benzene has six delocalised π electrons over a planar ring.
- Electrophilic aromatic substitution is the main reaction type.
- Substituents direct incoming groups ortho/para or meta.
- Fossil fuels and incomplete combustion release aromatic pollutants such as benzene.

**Worked examples**

1. **Nitration of benzene.** Benzene reacts with mixed acid (HNO<sub>3</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>) to give nitrobenzene, a classic electrophilic aromatic substitution.

2. **Methylbenzene and nitration.** A methyl group directs incoming groups to ortho and para positions because it donates electron density by hyperconjugation.

**Practice questions**

1. Benzene has: (A) 6 delocalised π electrons (B) 4 π electrons (C) 2 π electrons (D) No π electrons
   - *Answer:* 6 delocalised π electrons. Six π electrons satisfy Hückel's rule for n = 1.
2. A methyl group on benzene is: (A) Ortho-para directing (B) Meta directing (C) Deactivating (D) Neutral
   - *Answer:* Ortho-para directing. Electron-donating groups direct ortho and para.
3. Why is benzene unusually stable?
   - *Answer:* Its delocalised π electrons are spread over the ring, lowering the energy of the molecule.

## Chapter 14. Environmental Chemistry

### 14.1 Air Pollution and Its Effects

Pollutants from combustion and industry damage health, ecosystems and buildings.

**Key points**

- Primary pollutants are emitted directly; secondary pollutants form in the atmosphere.
- CO binds to haemoglobin more strongly than oxygen, causing suffocation.
- SO<sub>2</sub> and NO<sub>x</sub> cause acid rain, damaging soil, water and monuments.
- Photochemical smog forms when nitrogen oxides and hydrocarbons react in sunlight.
- Particulates cause respiratory disease and reduce visibility.

**Worked examples**

1. **Photochemical smog.** In sunny cities, NO<sub>x</sub> and volatile organic compounds react to form ozone and other oxidants, creating brown smog that irritates lungs.

2. **Taj Mahal corrosion.** Sulphur dioxide from industry forms acid rain that attacks the marble, turning it yellow and pitted.

**Practice questions**

1. Carbon monoxide is dangerous because it: (A) Binds haemoglobin more strongly than oxygen (B) Smells bad (C) Is an allergen (D) Blocks sunlight
   - *Answer:* Binds haemoglobin more strongly than oxygen. It reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood.
2. Acid rain is caused mainly by: (A) SO<sub>2</sub> and NO<sub>x</sub> (B) CO<sub>2</sub> alone (C) Ozone (D) Hydrogen
   - *Answer:* SO<sub>2</sub> and NO<sub>x</sub>. These oxides form acids with water.
3. Why is photochemical smog worse on sunny days?
   - *Answer:* Sunlight drives the reactions that produce ozone and other oxidants.

### 14.2 Water and Soil Pollution

Contaminants in water and soil harm ecosystems and human health. Treatment and responsible practice reduce pollution.

**Key points**

- BOD measures the oxygen demand of organic matter in water; high BOD means pollution.
- Eutrophication is nutrient enrichment that causes algal blooms and oxygen depletion.
- Pesticides and heavy metals accumulate in soils and food chains.
- Industrial effluent must be treated before discharge.
- Green chemistry aims to reduce hazardous materials from the start.

**Worked examples**

1. **BOD and sewage.** Untreated sewage has a high BOD because bacteria consume oxygen to decompose organic matter, leaving less oxygen for fish.

2. **Reducing pollution at source.** Using biodegradable detergents avoids persistent foams and reduces harm to aquatic life.

**Practice questions**

1. High BOD indicates: (A) Organic pollution (B) Clean water (C) Low bacteria (D) High oxygen
   - *Answer:* Organic pollution. More organic matter means more oxygen is consumed by bacteria.
2. Eutrophication leads to: (A) Oxygen depletion (B) More oxygen (C) Cleaner water (D) Less algae
   - *Answer:* Oxygen depletion. Algal blooms die and decomposition uses up oxygen.
3. What is green chemistry?
   - *Answer:* Designing chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate hazardous substances.
