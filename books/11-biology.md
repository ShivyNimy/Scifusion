## Chapter 1. The Living World

### 1.1 Taxonomy and Taxonomic Hierarchy

Taxonomy identifies, names and classifies organisms. The hierarchical system groups species into increasingly broad categories.

**Key points**

- Taxonomic categories: species, genus, family, order, class, phylum/division, kingdom.
- Binomial nomenclature: genus + specific epithet, written in italics (Homo sapiens).
- Taxonomical aids include herbaria, botanical gardens, museums, zoological parks and keys.
- A species is the fundamental unit of taxonomy.
- Systematics studies the diversity and evolutionary relationships of organisms.

**Worked examples**

1. **Naming a mango.** Mangifera indica: Mangifera is the genus, indica the species. The name is universal and avoids confusion across languages.

2. **Herbarium sheet.** A pressed, dried and labelled plant specimen preserves the plant's identity for reference and future study.

**Practice questions**

1. The correct way to write a scientific name is: (A) Genus capitalised, species lowercase, italicised (B) Both capitalised (C) Both lowercase (D) Species capitalised
   - *Answer:* Genus capitalised, species lowercase, italicised. Binomial names follow strict capitalisation and italicisation rules.
2. The basic unit of classification is the: (A) Species (B) Genus (C) Family (D) Order
   - *Answer:* Species. Species is the basic taxonomic unit.
3. Arrange in increasing order: order, species, family, genus, class.
   - *Answer:* Species -> genus -> family -> order -> class.

### 1.2 Diversity of Life and Its Measurement

Biodiversity is unevenly distributed; some regions are exceptionally rich and are called hotspots. Classification keeps the variety manageable.

**Key points**

- About 1.7–1.8 million species have been described, but many more exist.
- India is a megadiverse country with several biodiversity hotspots.
- Hotspots include the Western Ghats, Himalaya, Indo-Burma and Sundaland (parts in India).
- Taxonomic keys use pairs of contrasting statements (couplets).
- Museums and zoological parks preserve specimens and living collections.

**Worked examples**

1. **Western Ghats hotspot.** The Western Ghats are home to thousands of endemic plants and animals, which is why they are protected as a biodiversity hotspot.

2. **Using a key.** A dichotomous key asks whether a plant has, for example, net-like or parallel veins, leading step by step to the correct group.

**Practice questions**

1. Which is a biodiversity hotspot in India? (A) Western Ghats (B) Thar desert (C) Gangetic plain (D) Deccan plateau
   - *Answer:* Western Ghats. The Western Ghats are recognised globally for high endemism.
2. A herbarium stores: (A) Preserved plant specimens (B) Live animals (C) Seeds only (D) Soil samples
   - *Answer:* Preserved plant specimens. Herbaria keep dried, pressed and labelled plant specimens.
3. What is a taxonomic key?
   - *Answer:* A device of paired contrasting statements used to identify organisms step by step.

## Chapter 2. Biological Classification

### 2.1 The Five Kingdom System

Organisms are classified into five kingdoms based on cell type, nutrition and body organisation.

**Key points**

- Monera: prokaryotic, includes bacteria and cyanobacteria.
- Protista: eukaryotic unicellular organisms such as Paramecium and diatoms.
- Fungi: heterotrophic, saprophytic, cell walls of chitin.
- Plantae: autotrophic eukaryotes with cell walls of cellulose.
- Animalia: heterotrophic, multicellular eukaryotes without cell walls.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why fungi are not plants.** Fungi lack chlorophyll, have chitinous walls and absorb nutrients from dead or living matter, which is why they form their own kingdom.

2. **Kingdom Protista.** Amoeba and Paramecium are single eukaryotic cells that carry out all life processes, showing the diversity of protists.

**Practice questions**

1. Which kingdom includes cyanobacteria? (A) Monera (B) Protista (C) Fungi (D) Plantae
   - *Answer:* Monera. Cyanobacteria are prokaryotes in Monera.
2. Fungal cell walls contain: (A) Chitin (B) Cellulose (C) Peptidoglycan (D) Lignin
   - *Answer:* Chitin. Chitin is characteristic of fungi.
3. Name the five kingdoms and one example of each.
   - *Answer:* Monera (bacteria), Protista (Amoeba), Fungi (yeast), Plantae (mango), Animalia (human).

### 2.2 Viruses, Viroids, Prions and Lichens

Viruses are acellular infectious agents. Viroids and prions are simpler, and lichens are obligate partnerships.

**Key points**

- Viruses contain nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein capsid, and are obligate parasites.
- Viroids are naked RNA molecules that infect plants.
- Prions are infectious proteins that cause neurological diseases.
- Lichens are symbiotic associations of algae and fungi.
- Viruses show characteristics of both living and non-living things.

**Worked examples**

1. **Tobacco mosaic virus.** TMV is an RNA virus causing mosaic patterns on tobacco leaves; its discovery founded virology.

2. **Lichens as pollution indicators.** Lichens are sensitive to air pollution, so their presence indicates clean air and their absence signals poor air quality.

**Practice questions**

1. A virus contains: (A) Nucleic acid and a protein capsid (B) Only proteins (C) Only DNA (D) Cell wall and nucleus
   - *Answer:* Nucleic acid and a protein capsid. The capsid encloses the genetic material.
2. A lichen is an association of: (A) Alga and fungus (B) Virus and bacteria (C) Two fungi (D) Two algae
   - *Answer:* Alga and fungus. The alga photosynthesises and the fungus provides shelter.
3. Why are viruses considered both living and non-living?
   - *Answer:* They have genetic material and can reproduce inside a host, but outside they are inert.

## Chapter 3. Plant Kingdom

### 3.1 Algae to Gymnosperms

Plants are grouped by vascular tissue, seeds and flowers: algae, bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms and angiosperms.

**Key points**

- Algae are mostly aquatic and lack true roots, stems and leaves.
- Bryophytes (mosses) show the first true multicellular body but need water for reproduction.
- Pteridophytes (ferns) have vascular tissue and reproduce by spores.
- Gymnosperms have naked seeds and no fruit; many are evergreen conifers.
- Angiosperms produce flowers and fruits and dominate modern plants.

**Worked examples**

1. **Fern life cycle.** Ferns alternate between a dominant spore-producing plant and a small gametophyte that needs water for fertilisation.

2. **Why bryophytes are amphibious.** They live on land but need water for sperm to swim to the egg, like amphibians.

**Practice questions**

1. Which group produces naked seeds? (A) Gymnosperms (B) Bryophytes (C) Pteridophytes (D) Algae
   - *Answer:* Gymnosperms. Gymnosperm seeds are not enclosed in fruits.
2. Ferns reproduce by: (A) Spores (B) Seeds (C) Flowers (D) Fragmentation
   - *Answer:* Spores. Pteridophytes produce spores.
3. List the plant groups in evolutionary order.
   - *Answer:* Algae -> bryophytes -> pteridophytes -> gymnosperms -> angiosperms.

### 3.2 Angiosperms and Plant Life Cycles

Angiosperms are flowering plants with enclosed seeds. Their life cycle alternates between sporophyte and gametophyte generations.

**Key points**

- Angiosperms are divided into dicots and monocots.
- Flowers may be unisexual or bisexual; pollination precedes fertilisation.
- Double fertilisation produces a zygote and a triploid endosperm in angiosperms.
- Alternation of generations: diploid sporophyte and haploid gametophyte.
- Fruits protect seeds and aid dispersal.

**Worked examples**

1. **Double fertilisation.** One male gamete fuses with the egg to form the zygote, while another fuses with two polar nuclei to form the triploid endosperm — unique to angiosperms.

2. **Monocot versus dicot.** Monocots have parallel veins and one cotyledon; dicots have net veins and two cotyledons.

**Practice questions**

1. Endosperm in angiosperms is: (A) Triploid (B) Diploid (C) Haploid (D) Absent
   - *Answer:* Triploid. Double fertilisation produces triploid endosperm.
2. Which group dominates modern vegetation? (A) Angiosperms (B) Gymnosperms (C) Pteridophytes (D) Bryophytes
   - *Answer:* Angiosperms. Flowering plants are the most diverse and widespread.
3. What is alternation of generations?
   - *Answer:* The life cycle alternates between a haploid gametophyte and a diploid sporophyte generation.

## Chapter 4. Animal Kingdom

### 4.1 Invertebrate Phyla

Animals are grouped by body symmetry, body cavity and development. Invertebrates include sponges through echinoderms.

**Key points**

- Porifera (sponges): cellular level, water canal system.
- Cnidaria: radial symmetry with stinging cells (Hydra, jellyfish).
- Platyhelminthes: flatworms; Aschelminthes: roundworms.
- Annelida: segmented worms (earthworm); Arthropoda: jointed legs and exoskeleton (insects).
- Mollusca: soft bodies with shells; Echinodermata: spiny skinned with water-vascular system.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why arthropods are so successful.** Jointed appendages, a protective exoskeleton and metamorphosis allow insects to exploit almost every habitat.

2. **Earthworm and soil.** Segmented bodies and setae let earthworms burrow, mixing and aerating the soil.

**Practice questions**

1. Which phylum has jointed legs? (A) Arthropoda (B) Annelida (C) Mollusca (D) Echinodermata
   - *Answer:* Arthropoda. Arthropods have jointed appendages and exoskeletons.
2. Sponges belong to the phylum: (A) Porifera (B) Cnidaria (C) Mollusca (D) Annelida
   - *Answer:* Porifera. Porifera are sponges.
3. Name three features of arthropods.
   - *Answer:* Jointed legs, exoskeleton and segmented body (also compound eyes).

### 4.2 Vertebrate Classes

Vertebrates have a backbone and are divided into fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

**Key points**

- Fish are aquatic with gills and scales; they include cartilaginous and bony fish.
- Amphibians live in water and on land and have moist skin.
- Reptiles have dry scaly skin and lay shelled eggs on land.
- Birds have feathers, wings, beaks and are endothermic.
- Mammals have hair, mammary glands and most give birth to live young.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why amphibians depend on water.** Their eggs lack shells and their larvae have gills, so breeding requires water or moisture.

2. **Bird flight adaptations.** Hollow bones, feathers, air sacs and powerful muscles reduce weight and increase oxygen supply.

**Practice questions**

1. Which group is warm-blooded? (A) Birds and mammals (B) Fish and amphibians (C) Reptiles only (D) All vertebrates
   - *Answer:* Birds and mammals. Birds and mammals maintain constant body temperature.
2. Reptiles lay eggs: (A) On land with leathery shells (B) In water without shells (C) Inside the mother (D) Not at all
   - *Answer:* On land with leathery shells. Shelled eggs protect embryos on dry land.
3. Give two distinguishing features of mammals.
   - *Answer:* Hair on the body and mammary glands for feeding young.

## Chapter 5. Morphology of Flowering Plants

### 5.1 Root, Stem and Leaf Modifications

Plant organs are adapted for storage, support and survival. Modifications reveal function.

**Key points**

- Tap roots store food (carrot, turnip); prop roots support banyan; pneumatophores aid breathing in mangroves.
- Stems may be underground (potato, ginger) or modified for climbing (tendrils).
- Leaves may become spines (cactus) or tendrils (pea) and may store water (aloe).
- Phyllotaxy is the arrangement of leaves on the stem.
- Modifications are adaptations to environment and function.

**Worked examples**

1. **Mangrove pneumatophores.** Roots grow upwards above the mud to take in air in waterlogged soil, an adaptation to saline, oxygen-poor habitats.

2. **Cactus spines.** Leaves reduced to spines reduce water loss, while the green stem performs photosynthesis.

**Practice questions**

1. Potato is a modified: (A) Stem (B) Root (C) Leaf (D) Flower
   - *Answer:* Stem. The potato tuber is an underground stem with eyes (buds).
2. Tendrils in peas are modified: (A) Leaves (B) Roots (C) Stems (D) Flowers
   - *Answer:* Leaves. Pea tendrils are modified leaf parts that help climbing.
3. Why are cactus leaves reduced to spines?
   - *Answer:* To reduce transpiration and protect the plant while the green stem carries out photosynthesis.

### 5.2 Flower, Fruit and Seed Structure

The flower contains the reproductive organs. Fruits and seeds protect and disperse the embryo.

**Key points**

- A typical flower has calyx (sepals), corolla (petals), androecium (stamens) and gynoecium (carpels).
- The ovary contains ovules; after fertilisation it becomes the fruit.
- Aestivation is the arrangement of sepals or petals in a bud.
- Placentation is the arrangement of ovules within the ovary.
- Seeds contain an embryo, cotyledons and a seed coat.

**Worked examples**

1. **Reading a flower in the field.** Count the sepals, petals, stamens and carpels. The numbers and fusion help identify the family and species.

2. **From ovary to fruit.** The ovary wall becomes the pericarp; the ovule becomes the seed. The fruit protects and helps disperse the seed.

**Practice questions**

1. The gynoecium consists of: (A) Carpels (B) Stamens (C) Petals (D) Sepals
   - *Answer:* Carpels. Carpels are the female reproductive organs.
2. The fruit develops from the: (A) Ovary (B) Stamen (C) Sepal (D) Petal
   - *Answer:* Ovary. The ovary enlarges into the fruit after fertilisation.
3. Name the four whorls of a flower.
   - *Answer:* Calyx, corolla, androecium and gynoecium.

## Chapter 6. Anatomy of Flowering Plants

### 6.1 Tissues and Tissue Systems

Plant anatomy studies internal structure. Tissues form three systems: epidermal, ground and vascular.

**Key points**

- Epidermal tissue covers surfaces; guard cells regulate stomata.
- Ground tissue includes parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma.
- Vascular tissue is xylem (water) and phloem (food).
- Xylem elements: tracheids, vessels, xylem fibres and parenchyma.
- Phloem elements: sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem fibres and parenchyma.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why sclerenchyma is dead at maturity.** Thick lignified walls provide strength but leave no room for a living protoplast, so the cells are dead when mature.

2. **Guard cells.** Swelling and shrinking of guard cells opens and closes stomata, controlling gas exchange and water loss.

**Practice questions**

1. Which tissue provides mechanical strength to plants? (A) Sclerenchyma (B) Parenchyma (C) Phloem (D) Epidermis
   - *Answer:* Sclerenchyma. Lignified sclerenchyma fibres give strength.
2. Water-conducting elements of xylem are: (A) Tracheids and vessels (B) Sieve tubes (C) Companion cells (D) Guard cells
   - *Answer:* Tracheids and vessels. Tracheids and vessels transport water.
3. Differentiate xylem and phloem on the basis of function.
   - *Answer:* Xylem transports water and minerals; phloem transports food.

### 6.2 Monocot and Dicot Anatomy

Monocot and dicot stems and roots differ in vascular bundle arrangement, which helps identify plant groups.

**Key points**

- Dicot stems have vascular bundles in a ring; monocot stems have scattered bundles.
- Dicot roots have a central xylem core; monocot roots have a pith.
- Secondary growth occurs in dicots through the cambium.
- Monocots lack secondary growth because they have no cambium ring.
- Wood is secondary xylem formed by cambial activity.

**Worked examples**

1. **Tree rings and age.** Each year's secondary xylem forms one ring, so counting rings estimates the age of a tree.

2. **Bamboo has scattered bundles.** As a monocot, bamboo has scattered vascular bundles and shows no secondary thickening.

**Practice questions**

1. Vascular bundles in dicot stems are arranged: (A) In a ring (B) Scattered (C) Radially (D) Randomly
   - *Answer:* In a ring. A ring of bundles is typical of dicots.
2. Secondary growth is due to the: (A) Cambium (B) Epidermis (C) Pith (D) Cortex
   - *Answer:* Cambium. The cambium produces secondary xylem and phloem.
3. Why do monocots not show secondary growth?
   - *Answer:* They lack a vascular cambium, so they cannot form secondary tissues.

## Chapter 7. Structural Organisation in Animals

### 7.1 Animal Tissues

Animal tissues are epithelial, connective, muscular and nervous. Their structure matches their function.

**Key points**

- Epithelium may be simple or compound; it covers, protects and secretes.
- Connective tissue includes loose, dense, cartilage, bone and blood.
- Muscle tissue is skeletal, smooth or cardiac.
- Nervous tissue conducts impulses through neurons.
- Tissues combine into organs and organs into systems.

**Worked examples**

1. **Tendon versus ligament.** Tendons attach muscle to bone and ligaments attach bone to bone; both are dense connective tissue with parallel fibres.

2. **Blood as connective tissue.** Blood has cells suspended in plasma and connects organs by transporting substances, so it is a fluid connective tissue.

**Practice questions**

1. Which tissue joins bone to bone? (A) Ligament (B) Tendon (C) Cartilage (D) Blood
   - *Answer:* Ligament. Ligaments connect bones at joints.
2. Smooth muscle is found in the: (A) Intestine (B) Biceps (C) Heart (D) Tendon
   - *Answer:* Intestine. Smooth muscle lines internal organs and is involuntary.
3. Name the four types of animal tissue.
   - *Answer:* Epithelial, connective, muscular and nervous.

### 7.2 Frog, Earthworm and Cockroach Anatomy

Studying representative animals shows how organ systems are arranged to support different lifestyles.

**Key points**

- The frog has moist skin, lungs and a three-chambered heart.
- The earthworm has a segmented body, closed circulatory system and nephridia for excretion.
- The cockroach has an exoskeleton, tracheal respiration, Malpighian tubules and a dorsal heart.
- Organ systems are integrated with the organism's habitat and feeding habits.
- Dissection studies reveal comparative anatomy.

**Worked examples**

1. **Frog respiration.** Frogs breathe through skin on land and in water and use lungs when active, an adaptation to amphibious life.

2. **Cockroach excretion.** Malpighian tubules remove nitrogenous wastes into the gut for excretion, conserving water in a dry habitat.

**Practice questions**

1. The heart of a frog has: (A) Three chambers (B) Two chambers (C) Four chambers (D) One chamber
   - *Answer:* Three chambers. Two atria and one ventricle.
2. Excretory organs of an earthworm are: (A) Nephridia (B) Malpighian tubules (C) Kidneys (D) Gills
   - *Answer:* Nephridia. Nephridia remove nitrogenous waste.
3. Why is the earthworm's circulatory system called closed?
   - *Answer:* Blood flows entirely within vessels and is pumped by hearts.

## Chapter 8. Cell: The Unit of Life

### 8.1 Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

Cells are prokaryotic or eukaryotic. Eukaryotic cells contain membrane-bound organelles that compartmentalise functions.

**Key points**

- Prokaryotes lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; they have a cell wall and 70S ribosomes.
- Eukaryotes have a true nucleus and organelles such as mitochondria, ER and Golgi.
- The plasma membrane is a fluid mosaic of lipids and proteins.
- Cell walls of plants are cellulose; fungi have chitin; bacteria have peptidoglycan.
- Cell size and shape reflect function.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why prokaryotes are efficient.** Their small size gives a high surface-area-to-volume ratio, so diffusion alone can supply nutrients and remove wastes.

2. **Fluid mosaic model.** Proteins float in or on a phospholipid bilayer, allowing lateral movement and selective transport.

**Practice questions**

1. Which is found in prokaryotes? (A) 70S ribosome (B) Nucleus (C) Mitochondria (D) Golgi body
   - *Answer:* 70S ribosome. Prokaryotes have 70S ribosomes and lack membrane-bound organelles.
2. The cell wall of plants is made of: (A) Cellulose (B) Chitin (C) Peptidoglycan (D) Protein
   - *Answer:* Cellulose. Cellulose is the main structural polysaccharide in plant cell walls.
3. Why is the plasma membrane called selectively permeable?
   - *Answer:* It allows some substances to pass while restricting others.

### 8.2 Cell Organelles

Organelles perform specialised tasks: energy conversion, protein synthesis, transport, digestion and storage.

**Key points**

- Mitochondria produce ATP and contain their own DNA.
- Ribosomes synthesise proteins; ER transports and modifies them.
- Golgi complex packages and exports materials.
- Lysosomes digest waste; peroxisomes handle oxidative reactions.
- Plastids carry out photosynthesis and storage in plants.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why mitochondria have folded inner membranes.** Cristae increase the surface area for the electron transport chain and ATP synthesis.

2. **Ribosome factory.** Ribosomes translate mRNA into polypeptide chains, the first step of protein synthesis.

**Practice questions**

1. Which organelle packages and exports proteins? (A) Golgi complex (B) Mitochondria (C) Ribosome (D) Lysosome
   - *Answer:* Golgi complex. The Golgi modifies, packages and exports materials.
2. Chloroplasts are found in: (A) Plant cells (B) Animal cells (C) Bacteria (D) Viruses
   - *Answer:* Plant cells. Chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis in plant cells.
3. Why are lysosomes called suicidal bags?
   - *Answer:* Under stress they may rupture and release digestive enzymes, damaging the cell.

## Chapter 9. Biomolecules

### 9.1 Carbohydrates, Proteins, Lipids and Nucleic Acids

Biomolecules are the building blocks of life. Each class has characteristic structure and function.

**Key points**

- Carbohydrates are energy sources and structural materials (starch, cellulose, glycogen).
- Proteins are polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds and perform many functions.
- Lipids are hydrophobic molecules used for membranes and energy storage.
- Nucleic acids (DNA, RNA) store and transmit genetic information.
- Enzymes are proteins that catalyse biological reactions.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why cooking changes an egg.** Heat denatures egg proteins, breaking weak bonds so the proteins unfold and coagulate irreversibly.

2. **DNA versus RNA.** DNA stores genetic information with deoxyribose and thymine; RNA carries information and uses ribose and uracil.

**Practice questions**

1. Proteins are polymers of: (A) Amino acids (B) Nucleotides (C) Fatty acids (D) Sugars
   - *Answer:* Amino acids. Peptide bonds link amino acids into proteins.
2. Which biomolecule is hydrophobic? (A) Lipid (B) Protein (C) Carbohydrate (D) Nucleic acid
   - *Answer:* Lipid. Lipids repel water.
3. What is the difference between DNA and RNA?
   - *Answer:* DNA has deoxyribose and thymine and stores information; RNA has ribose and uracil and usually carries information.

### 9.2 Enzymes and Their Kinetics

Enzymes lower activation energy and are specific. Their activity depends on pH, temperature and concentration.

**Key points**

- Enzymes are biological catalysts, mostly proteins.
- They lower activation energy by stabilising transition states.
- Optimum temperature and pH maximise enzyme activity.
- Competitive inhibitors bind the active site; non-competitive inhibitors bind elsewhere.
- Michaelis-Menten kinetics describes initial rates; K<sub>m</sub> indicates substrate affinity.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why fever makes you feel weak.** High temperature denatures enzymes and disturbs metabolism, which is why fever is dangerous.

2. **Enzyme in the stomach.** Pepsin works best at acidic pH, while intestinal enzymes prefer alkaline conditions — the environment matches the organ.

**Practice questions**

1. Enzymes increase the rate of reaction by: (A) Lowering activation energy (B) Raising temperature (C) Changing products (D) Adding energy
   - *Answer:* Lowering activation energy. Enzymes lower the activation energy barrier.
2. A competitive inhibitor binds to the: (A) Active site (B) Allosteric site (C) Product (D) Substrate
   - *Answer:* Active site. It competes with the substrate for the active site.
3. Why is enzyme activity reduced at very high temperature?
   - *Answer:* The enzyme's tertiary structure denatures, so the active site loses its shape and function.

## Chapter 10. Cell Cycle and Cell Division

### 10.1 Mitosis and Meiosis

Cells divide for growth and reproduction. Mitosis gives identical cells; meiosis produces gametes with genetic variation.

**Key points**

- The cell cycle has interphase (G1, S, G2) and mitosis.
- In the S phase DNA replicates, doubling chromosome content.
- Mitosis stages: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase.
- Meiosis has two divisions and produces four haploid cells.
- Crossing over in prophase I creates genetic variation.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why crossing over matters.** Genetic recombination in meiosis produces new combinations of alleles, increasing variation in offspring.

2. **Cancer and the cell cycle.** Cancer results from loss of cell-cycle control, causing uncontrolled division.

**Practice questions**

1. DNA replicates during: (A) S phase (B) G1 phase (C) G2 phase (D) Telophase
   - *Answer:* S phase. Synthesis of DNA occurs in S phase.
2. Meiosis produces: (A) Four haploid cells (B) Two identical cells (C) One cell (D) Eight cells
   - *Answer:* Four haploid cells. Meiosis halves the chromosome number.
3. Differentiate mitosis and meiosis.
   - *Answer:* Mitosis gives two identical diploid cells for growth; meiosis gives four haploid gametes with genetic variation.

### 10.2 Significance of Cell Division

Cell division maintains body size, repairs damage and enables reproduction. Uncontrolled division causes disease.

**Key points**

- Mitosis replaces worn-out cells and heals wounds.
- In plants, meristems divide throughout life to produce new growth.
- Meiosis maintains the chromosome number across generations.
- Apoptosis is programmed cell death, balancing division.
- Checkpoints ensure damage is repaired before division.

**Worked examples**

1. **Wound healing.** Cells around a cut divide by mitosis, filling the gap and restoring the tissue.

2. **Programmed cell death.** Apoptosis removes damaged or unwanted cells; loss of apoptosis contributes to cancer.

**Practice questions**

1. Programmed cell death is called: (A) Apoptosis (B) Necrosis (C) Mitosis (D) Cytokinesis
   - *Answer:* Apoptosis. Apoptosis is controlled, programmed cell death.
2. Which cells divide throughout plant life? (A) Meristematic cells (B) Mature xylem (C) Sclerenchyma (D) Epidermis
   - *Answer:* Meristematic cells. Meristems contain actively dividing cells.
3. Why are checkpoints important in the cell cycle?
   - *Answer:* They verify that DNA is intact and replicated before division, preventing the propagation of errors.

## Chapter 11. Transport in Plants

### 11.1 Water Potential, Plasmolysis and Absorption

Water moves by osmosis from high to low water potential. Plant cells use water potential and pressure to regulate turgor.

**Key points**

- Water potential ψ<sub>w</sub> = ψ<sub>s</sub> + ψ<sub>p</sub> (solute and pressure potentials).
- Osmosis moves water toward lower water potential.
- Turgid cells have positive pressure potential; plasmolysed cells lose turgor.
- Root hairs increase surface area for water absorption.
- Mineral ions are often actively absorbed using energy.

**Worked examples**

1. **Fresh lettuce in salt water.** Salt water has lower water potential than the cells, so water leaves and the lettuce wilts — plasmolysis.

2. **Root pressure.** Active ion transport into the root draws water in and pushes it up, explaining guttation in some plants.

**Practice questions**

1. Water moves by osmosis from: (A) Higher water potential to lower (B) Lower to higher (C) Low solute to high (D) Cold to hot
   - *Answer:* Higher water potential to lower. Water moves down the water potential gradient.
2. Plasmolysis occurs when a cell is placed in: (A) Hypertonic solution (B) Hypotonic solution (C) Pure water (D) Isotonic solution
   - *Answer:* Hypertonic solution. Water leaves the cell in a hypertonic solution.
3. Define water potential and give its units.
   - *Answer:* The potential energy of water per unit volume relative to pure water; measured in bars or pascals.

### 11.2 Transpiration, Ascent of Sap and Translocation

Water moves up tall trees mainly by transpiration pull; food moves through the phloem by pressure flow.

**Key points**

- Transpiration is water loss from leaves through stomata.
- Cohesion-tension theory explains the ascent of sap: transpiration pull draws a continuous water column.
- Guttation is the loss of water droplets from leaf margins.
- Translocation moves sugars in the phloem from source to sink.
- The pressure-flow hypothesis explains phloem transport.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why trees wilt in drought.** Transpiration exceeds water uptake, so cells lose turgor and leaves droop.

2. **Sugar transport in spring.** Stored sugars move from roots (source) to developing buds (sink) through the phloem.

**Practice questions**

1. Ascent of sap is mainly explained by: (A) Cohesion-tension theory (B) Root pressure (C) Active transport (D) Diffusion
   - *Answer:* Cohesion-tension theory. Transpiration pull drives water up the xylem.
2. Food is transported in: (A) Phloem (B) Xylem (C) Stomata (D) Lenticels
   - *Answer:* Phloem. Phloem carries sugars and other solutes.
3. Why does transpiration pull work in tall trees?
   - *Answer:* Water molecules cohere, forming a continuous column that is pulled up by evaporation at the leaves.

## Chapter 12. Mineral Nutrition

### 12.1 Macro and Micronutrients

Plants need mineral nutrients in different amounts. Deficiency causes specific symptoms.

**Key points**

- Macronutrients: C, H, O, N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S.
- Micronutrients: Fe, Mn, Cu, Mo, Zn, B, Cl.
- Nitrogen is needed for proteins and chlorophyll; deficiency causes chlorosis.
- Nutrients are absorbed from soil solution by roots.
- Deficiency symptoms appear in specific parts of the plant.

**Worked examples**

1. **Nitrogen deficiency.** Leaves turn pale yellow because chlorophyll synthesis is limited and growth slows.

2. **Chlorosis versus necrosis.** Chlorosis is yellowing from loss of chlorophyll; necrosis is death of tissue from severe deficiency.

**Practice questions**

1. Which is a macronutrient? (A) Nitrogen (B) Iron (C) Zinc (D) Copper
   - *Answer:* Nitrogen. Nitrogen is needed in large amounts.
2. Deficiency of nitrogen causes: (A) Chlorosis (B) Necrosis only (C) Wilting only (D) Leaf drop only
   - *Answer:* Chlorosis. Pale yellow leaves indicate nitrogen deficiency.
3. Why are micronutrients needed in small amounts?
   - *Answer:* They serve as cofactors and catalysts rather than structural building materials.

### 12.2 Nitrogen Fixation and Nitrogen Cycle

Plants cannot use atmospheric nitrogen directly. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert it into usable compounds.

**Key points**

- Nitrogen fixation converts N<sub>2</sub> into ammonia.
- Rhizobium fixes nitrogen in root nodules of legumes.
- Azotobacter and cyanobacteria also fix nitrogen.
- Nitrification converts ammonia to nitrite and nitrate.
- Denitrification returns nitrogen to the atmosphere.

**Worked examples**

1. **Root nodules.** Rhizobium in root nodules receives carbon compounds and supplies nitrogen compounds to the plant — a mutualistic symbiosis.

2. **Crop rotation with legumes.** Growing legumes restores soil nitrogen, reducing the need for nitrogen fertiliser.

**Practice questions**

1. Which bacterium fixes nitrogen in legume roots? (A) Rhizobium (B) Nitrosomonas (C) E. coli (D) Lactobacillus
   - *Answer:* Rhizobium. Rhizobium forms nitrogen-fixing nodules on legume roots.
2. Nitrification converts ammonia to: (A) Nitrate (B) Nitrogen gas (C) Protein (D) Ammonia
   - *Answer:* Nitrate. Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonia to nitrate.
3. How does adding legumes improve soil fertility?
   - *Answer:* Symbiotic nitrogen fixation adds usable nitrogen compounds to the soil.

## Chapter 13. Photosynthesis in Higher Plants

### 13.1 Light Reactions and Photosystems

Photosynthesis uses light energy to produce ATP and NADPH, which drive sugar synthesis.

**Key points**

- Chlorophyll a and b absorb light, mainly in blue and red regions.
- Photosystem II splits water, releasing oxygen and electrons.
- The electron transport chain pumps protons, producing a gradient used to make ATP (photophosphorylation).
- Photosystem I produces NADPH.
- Light reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes.

**Worked examples**

1. **Effect of green light.** Chlorophyll reflects green light, so it is least effective for photosynthesis, which is why a leaf appears green.

2. **Where the oxygen comes from.** Oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from water, not carbon dioxide — shown by isotope experiments.

**Practice questions**

1. Oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from: (A) Water (B) Carbon dioxide (C) Glucose (D) Chlorophyll
   - *Answer:* Water. Photolysis of water in PSII releases oxygen.
2. Light reactions occur in the: (A) Thylakoid membranes (B) Stroma (C) Cytoplasm (D) Mitochondria
   - *Answer:* Thylakoid membranes. The thylakoids contain the photosystems.
3. Why is chlorophyll green?
   - *Answer:* It absorbs red and blue light strongly and reflects green light.

### 13.2 Calvin Cycle and Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

The Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugar. Many factors limit the rate.

**Key points**

- The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma of the chloroplast.
- CO<sub>2</sub> is fixed by RuBisCO onto RuBP.
- The cycle uses ATP and NADPH to reduce carbon compounds to sugar.
- C3 plants fix CO<sub>2</sub> once; C4 plants fix it twice and are more efficient in hot climates.
- Limiting factors include light, CO<sub>2</sub> concentration and temperature.

**Worked examples**

1. **C4 advantage.** C4 plants concentrate CO<sub>2</sub> in bundle sheath cells, reducing photorespiration and boosting productivity in heat.

2. **Greenhouse CO<sub>2</sub> enrichment.** Increasing CO<sub>2</sub> can increase photosynthesis, up to the point where another factor becomes limiting.

**Practice questions**

1. The Calvin cycle occurs in the: (A) Stroma (B) Thylakoid membrane (C) Cytoplasm (D) Mitochondria
   - *Answer:* Stroma. Carbon fixation happens in the stroma.
2. The enzyme that fixes CO<sub>2</sub> in C3 plants is: (A) RuBisCO (B) Pepsin (C) Catalase (D) Nitrogenase
   - *Answer:* RuBisCO. RuBisCO catalyses the first step of carbon fixation.
3. Why are C4 plants more efficient in hot climates?
   - *Answer:* They concentrate CO<sub>2</sub> at the site of fixation, minimising photorespiration.

## Chapter 14. Respiration in Plants

### 14.1 Glycolysis, Krebs Cycle and Electron Transport

Aerobic respiration releases energy from glucose in three stages: glycolysis, Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation.

**Key points**

- Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm and yields 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose.
- Pyruvate enters mitochondria and is converted to acetyl CoA.
- Krebs cycle occurs in the mitochondrial matrix and yields CO<sub>2</sub>, ATP, NADH and FADH<sub>2</sub>.
- The electron transport chain and ATP synthase produce most ATP.
- The overall aerobic yield is about 30–38 ATP per glucose depending on shuttle efficiency.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why aerobic respiration yields more ATP.** Complete oxidation transfers more electrons to the electron transport chain, producing far more ATP than fermentation.

2. **Respiratory quotient.** RQ = CO<sub>2</sub> released ÷ O<sub>2</sub> consumed; it is 1 for carbohydrates and less than 1 for fats.

**Practice questions**

1. Glycolysis occurs in the: (A) Cytoplasm (B) Mitochondria (C) Chloroplast (D) Nucleus
   - *Answer:* Cytoplasm. Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm.
2. Most ATP is produced by: (A) Oxidative phosphorylation (B) Glycolysis (C) Krebs cycle (D) Fermentation
   - *Answer:* Oxidative phosphorylation. The electron transport chain produces most ATP.
3. Write the overall equation for aerobic respiration.
   - *Answer:* C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>12</sub>O<sub>6</sub> + 6O<sub>2</sub> -> 6CO<sub>2</sub> + 6H<sub>2</sub>O + energy (about 30–38 ATP).

### 14.2 Anaerobic Respiration and Amphibolic Pathway

Fermentation allows energy release without oxygen, but yields much less ATP. The respiratory pathway is amphibolic.

**Key points**

- In yeast, pyruvate is converted to ethanol and CO<sub>2</sub>.
- In muscles, pyruvate becomes lactic acid during vigorous exercise.
- Fermentation yields only 2 ATP per glucose.
- The respiratory pathway provides intermediates for other biosynthetic reactions (amphibolic).
- Respiration is controlled by the energy needs of the cell.

**Worked examples**

1. **Muscle cramp.** Lactic acid from anaerobic respiration accumulates during intense exercise, causing cramps.

2. **Bread rising.** Yeast ferments sugars and releases CO<sub>2</sub> bubbles, which make dough rise.

**Practice questions**

1. Anaerobic respiration in muscles produces: (A) Lactic acid (B) Ethanol (C) CO<sub>2</sub> only (D) Pyruvate
   - *Answer:* Lactic acid. Lactic acid forms when oxygen is limited.
2. Fermentation yields how many ATP per glucose? (A) 2 (B) 36 (C) 30 (D) 38
   - *Answer:* 2. Fermentation yields only 2 ATP.
3. Why is the respiratory pathway called amphibolic?
   - *Answer:* It both breaks down (catabolic) and supplies intermediates for synthesis (anabolic) of molecules.

## Chapter 15. Plant Growth and Development

### 15.1 Growth Hormones and Regulators

Plant growth is regulated by hormones that promote or inhibit growth and development.

**Key points**

- Auxins stimulate cell elongation and control phototropism.
- Gibberellins promote stem elongation, seed germination and flowering.
- Cytokinins promote cell division and delay senescence.
- Abscisic acid inhibits growth and closes stomata under stress.
- Ethylene promotes ripening and abscission.

**Worked examples**

1. **Auxin in phototropism.** Auxin accumulates on the shaded side and elongates those cells, bending the shoot toward light.

2. **Ripening fruit.** Ethylene released by ripe fruit accelerates ripening of nearby fruit; that is why one ripe banana ripens others in a bag.

**Practice questions**

1. Which hormone promotes ripening? (A) Ethylene (B) Auxin (C) Cytokinin (D) Gibberellin
   - *Answer:* Ethylene. Ethylene promotes fruit ripening.
2. Abscisic acid is mainly: (A) Inhibitory (B) Stimulatory for growth (C) A cytokinin (D) An auxin
   - *Answer:* Inhibitory. ABA inhibits growth and closes stomata under stress.
3. Explain phototropism in terms of auxin.
   - *Answer:* Light causes auxin to accumulate on the shaded side, where it stimulates elongation, bending the shoot toward light.

### 15.2 Growth Stages and Seed Germination

Seed germination, growth, flowering and senescence are regulated by internal and external factors.

**Key points**

- Imbibition is the first step of seed germination, absorbing water.
- Germination needs water, oxygen and a suitable temperature.
- Some seeds need light or stratification to germinate.
- Growth curves show an initial slow phase, rapid growth and a plateau.
- Senescence and abscission are controlled processes that recycle nutrients.

**Worked examples**

1. **Dormancy.** A dormant seed waits for favourable conditions; the seed coat may block water or oxygen, or inhibitors may block germination.

2. **Growth curve.** An annual crop grows slowly at first, rapidly in the middle season, then levels off as nutrients go into grain formation.

**Practice questions**

1. The first step of seed germination is: (A) Imbibition (B) Photosynthesis (C) Flowering (D) Senescence
   - *Answer:* Imbibition. Seeds first absorb water and swell.
2. Seed dormancy helps the seed to: (A) Survive unfavourable conditions (B) Grow faster (C) Photosynthesise (D) Avoid water
   - *Answer:* Survive unfavourable conditions. Dormancy delays germination until conditions are suitable.
3. Why do seeds need oxygen to germinate?
   - *Answer:* Germination requires energy from aerobic respiration, which needs oxygen.

## Chapter 16. Digestion and Absorption

### 16.1 The Human Digestive System

Digestion breaks food into absorbable molecules. The alimentary canal and associated glands secrete enzymes that act at specific pH values.

**Key points**

- The mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine and large intestine form the alimentary canal.
- Saliva contains amylase, which begins starch digestion.
- The stomach secretes HCl and pepsin, digesting proteins in an acidic medium.
- The small intestine receives bile and pancreatic juice and completes digestion; villi absorb nutrients.
- The large intestine absorbs water and electrolytes and forms faeces.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why the stomach does not digest itself.** A thick mucus layer and rapid cell replacement protect the stomach lining from acid and pepsin.

2. **Bile and fat digestion.** Bile salts emulsify large fat droplets into smaller ones, increasing the surface area for lipase.

**Practice questions**

1. Protein digestion begins in the: (A) Stomach (B) Mouth (C) Small intestine (D) Large intestine
   - *Answer:* Stomach. Pepsin in the stomach starts protein digestion.
2. Bile is produced by the: (A) Liver (B) Pancreas (C) Stomach (D) Gall bladder
   - *Answer:* Liver. The liver produces bile, stored in the gall bladder.
3. Why is the small intestine the main site of absorption?
   - *Answer:* Its long length and millions of villi and microvilli give an enormous surface area.

### 16.2 Absorption and Digestive Disorders

Nutrients are absorbed in different regions of the gut. Disorders can be caused by infection, diet or organ dysfunction.

**Key points**

- Glucose and amino acids are absorbed in the small intestine by active transport.
- Fatty acids and glycerol are absorbed as chylomicrons into lymph.
- Vitamin B12 absorption needs intrinsic factor from the stomach.
- Jaundice is associated with liver dysfunction and bilirubin accumulation.
- Vomiting and diarrhoea cause dehydration and loss of electrolytes.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why rapid fluid replacement is needed in diarrhoea.** Diarrhoea causes severe water and electrolyte loss, so ORS restores fluids and salts.

2. **Fat-soluble vitamins.** Vitamins A, D, E and K need bile for absorption because they are fat-soluble.

**Practice questions**

1. Which vitamin needs intrinsic factor for absorption? (A) Vitamin B12 (B) Vitamin C (C) Vitamin B1 (D) Vitamin K
   - *Answer:* Vitamin B12. Intrinsic factor from the stomach is needed for B12 uptake.
2. Most water is absorbed in the: (A) Large intestine (B) Stomach (C) Oesophagus (D) Mouth
   - *Answer:* Large intestine. The large intestine absorbs most remaining water.
3. Why is jaundice linked to liver problems?
   - *Answer:* The liver processes bilirubin; when it cannot, bilirubin accumulates and yellows the skin and eyes.

## Chapter 17. Breathing and Exchange of Gases

### 17.1 Respiratory Organs and Gas Exchange

Gas exchange surfaces are thin, moist and richly supplied with blood. In humans, alveoli exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide.

**Key points**

- Insects use tracheae; fish use gills; humans use lungs with alveoli.
- Alveoli have a large surface area and thin walls for diffusion.
- Breathing involves inspiration by the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.
- Oxygen diffuses into blood and binds haemoglobin; CO<sub>2</sub> is transported mainly as bicarbonate.
- Vital capacity is the maximum volume exhaled after a maximum inhalation.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why lungs are spongy.** Millions of alveoli give a total surface area of about 70 m<sup>2</sup>, enough for efficient gas exchange.

2. **CO<sub>2</sub> transport.** About 70% of CO<sub>2</sub> is carried as bicarbonate ions, which also help maintain blood pH.

**Practice questions**

1. Gas exchange in humans occurs in the: (A) Alveoli (B) Trachea (C) Bronchi (D) Larynx
   - *Answer:* Alveoli. Alveoli are the functional units of exchange.
2. Most CO<sub>2</sub> is transported in blood as: (A) Bicarbonate (B) Carbamino haemoglobin (C) Dissolved CO<sub>2</sub> (D) Carbonate
   - *Answer:* Bicarbonate. About 70% travels as bicarbonate.
3. Why must respiratory surfaces be thin and moist?
   - *Answer:* Thin walls and moisture allow fast diffusion of gases and protect the delicate surface.

### 17.2 Lung Volumes and Disorders

Respiratory volumes describe lung function. Disorders reduce gas exchange and require care.

**Key points**

- Tidal volume is the air moved in one normal breath.
- Functional residual capacity is the air left after normal expiration.
- Asthma narrows airways and makes breathing difficult.
- Emphysema destroys alveolar walls and reduces surface area.
- Smoking damages cilia and alveoli and increases risk of lung disease.

**Worked examples**

1. **Asthma attack.** Smooth muscle constricts the bronchioles and mucus increases, so airways narrow and wheezing occurs.

2. **Effect of altitude.** Lower oxygen pressure stimulates faster breathing and red blood cell production.

**Practice questions**

1. Which disorder destroys alveolar walls? (A) Emphysema (B) Asthma (C) Bronchitis (D) Pneumonia
   - *Answer:* Emphysema. Emphysema reduces the surface for gas exchange.
2. The volume of air breathed in normal rest is called: (A) Tidal volume (B) Vital capacity (C) Residual volume (D) Total capacity
   - *Answer:* Tidal volume. Tidal volume is the resting breath volume.
3. Why is smoking harmful to the respiratory system?
   - *Answer:* It damages cilia and alveoli, reduces surface area and increases the risk of cancer and chronic disease.

## Chapter 18. Body Fluids and Circulation

### 18.1 Blood and Its Components

Blood transports substances and defends the body. It contains plasma and formed elements.

**Key points**

- Plasma is about 55% of blood and carries proteins, nutrients, hormones and wastes.
- Red blood cells contain haemoglobin and transport oxygen.
- White blood cells defend the body against infection.
- Platelets help in blood clotting.
- The average adult has about 5 L of blood.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why blood is red.** Haemoglobin containing iron binds oxygen, giving blood its red colour.

2. **Blood clotting.** Platelets and clotting factors convert fibrinogen into a fibrin mesh that seals the wound.

**Practice questions**

1. Which blood cells transport oxygen? (A) Red blood cells (B) White blood cells (C) Platelets (D) Plasma
   - *Answer:* Red blood cells. Haemoglobin in RBCs carries oxygen.
2. The percentage of plasma in blood is about: (A) 55% (B) 45% (C) 25% (D) 75%
   - *Answer:* 55%. Plasma makes up about 55% of blood volume.
3. What is the function of platelets?
   - *Answer:* They help form clots and stop bleeding at injury sites.

### 18.2 Heart, Cardiac Cycle and Circulatory Disorders

The heart pumps blood through pulmonary and systemic circuits. Its rhythm and output are measurable.

**Key points**

- The heart has four chambers: two atria and two ventricles.
- The cardiac cycle is one heartbeat with systole and diastole phases.
- Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume.
- Blood pressure is measured as systolic/diastolic.
- Hypertension, atherosclerosis and heart attack are common disorders.

**Worked examples**

1. **Double circulation.** Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit — once to the lungs and once to the body — which improves oxygenation efficiency.

2. **Electrocardiogram.** The ECG records electrical activity of the heart and helps detect arrhythmias and heart damage.

**Practice questions**

1. The normal resting heart rate of an adult is about: (A) 72 beats per minute (B) 50 (C) 100 (D) 120
   - *Answer:* 72 beats per minute. Typical resting rate is around 70–75 bpm.
2. Double circulation means blood passes through the heart: (A) Twice per complete circuit (B) Once (C) Three times (D) Never
   - *Answer:* Twice per complete circuit. Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit.
3. What is cardiac output?
   - *Answer:* The volume of blood pumped by the heart per minute, equal to heart rate × stroke volume.

## Chapter 19. Excretory Products and Their Elimination

### 19.1 The Kidney and Urine Formation

Kidneys filter blood and form urine. The nephron is the functional unit, and the process has three stages.

**Key points**

- The nephron has a glomerulus, Bowman's capsule, tubules and collecting duct.
- Ultrafiltration occurs in the glomerulus under pressure.
- Reabsorption returns water, glucose and ions from the tubules to blood.
- Tubular secretion moves wastes from blood into the filtrate.
- The counter-current mechanism in the loop of Henle conserves water.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why urine is concentrated.** The loop of Henle builds a salt gradient that allows water reabsorption, concentrating the urine and conserving water.

2. **Glucose generally absent from urine.** Glucose is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule; its presence in urine may indicate diabetes.

**Practice questions**

1. The functional unit of the kidney is the: (A) Nephron (B) Neuron (C) Alveolus (D) Glomerulus
   - *Answer:* Nephron. Each kidney contains about a million nephrons.
2. Ultrafiltration occurs in the: (A) Glomerulus (B) Loop of Henle (C) Collecting duct (D) Ureter
   - *Answer:* Glomerulus. Pressure forces filtrate into Bowman's capsule.
3. Name the three processes of urine formation.
   - *Answer:* Ultrafiltration, selective reabsorption and tubular secretion.

### 19.2 Osmoregulation and Excretory Disorders

The body regulates water and solute balance through the kidneys and hormones. Dialysis replaces kidney function in failure.

**Key points**

- Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) increases water reabsorption.
- Aldosterone regulates sodium reabsorption.
- Renal failure requires dialysis or a transplant.
- Kidney stones are crystallised mineral deposits in the urinary tract.
- Ammonia, urea and uric acid are nitrogenous wastes; their form depends on water availability.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why we urinate less when dehydrated.** ADH release increases water reabsorption, producing small amounts of concentrated urine.

2. **Dialysis.** A dialysis machine removes nitrogenous wastes from blood when kidneys have failed.

**Practice questions**

1. ADH increases: (A) Water reabsorption (B) Sodium excretion (C) Urine glucose (D) Protein loss
   - *Answer:* Water reabsorption. ADH makes collecting ducts more permeable to water.
2. A person whose kidneys fail may need: (A) Dialysis (B) Physiotherapy (C) Vaccination (D) Radiotherapy
   - *Answer:* Dialysis. Dialysis filters waste from blood.
3. Why do desert animals excrete uric acid?
   - *Answer:* Uric acid is almost insoluble and requires little water to excrete, conserving water.

## Chapter 20. Locomotion and Movement

### 20.1 Muscles and the Sliding Filament Model

Muscles contract to produce movement. Skeletal muscle consists of bundles of fibres containing actin and myosin filaments.

**Key points**

- A muscle fibre contains myofibrils with sarcomeres.
- In contraction, actin filaments slide over myosin, shortening the sarcomere.
- Calcium ions trigger binding of actin and myosin.
- ATP powers myosin heads and is needed for relaxation too.
- Red muscle fibres are slow and fatigue-resistant; white fibres are fast and tire quickly.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why muscles need calcium.** Calcium binds troponin and exposes myosin-binding sites on actin, allowing cross-bridge cycling.

2. **Rigor mortis.** After death, ATP is depleted so myosin cross-bridges cannot detach, making muscles stiff.

**Practice questions**

1. Muscle contraction occurs by: (A) Sliding of actin over myosin (B) Shortening of actin filaments (C) Myosin shrinking (D) Sarcomere growing
   - *Answer:* Sliding of actin over myosin. The sliding filament model explains contraction.
2. Which ion triggers contraction? (A) Calcium (B) Sodium (C) Potassium (D) Chloride
   - *Answer:* Calcium. Calcium release triggers cross-bridge cycling.
3. Why is ATP needed for muscle relaxation?
   - *Answer:* ATP is needed to pump calcium back and detach myosin heads so the muscle can relax.

### 20.2 Skeletal System and Joints

The human skeleton supports the body, protects organs and allows movement through joints.

**Key points**

- The adult skeleton has 206 bones.
- The axial skeleton includes skull, vertebral column and rib cage.
- The appendicular skeleton includes limbs and girdles.
- Joints may be fibrous, cartilaginous or synovial.
- Osteoporosis and arthritis affect bones and joints.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why the skull has sutures.** Immovable fibrous joints between skull bones lock them tightly to protect the brain.

2. **Knee joint.** A synovial hinge joint with cartilage and fluid allows smooth bending and straightening.

**Practice questions**

1. The number of bones in an adult human is: (A) 206 (B) 300 (C) 150 (D) 500
   - *Answer:* 206. Adults have 206 bones.
2. The knee is a: (A) Synovial hinge joint (B) Fibrous joint (C) Ball-and-socket joint (D) Immovable joint
   - *Answer:* Synovial hinge joint. The knee allows motion in one plane.
3. Differentiate the axial and appendicular skeleton.
   - *Answer:* The axial skeleton forms the central axis (skull, spine, ribs); the appendicular skeleton forms the limbs and girdles.

## Chapter 21. Neural Control and Coordination

### 21.1 Neurons and Nerve Impulses

Neurons transmit electrical impulses. The resting potential and action potential depend on ion movement across the membrane.

**Key points**

- A neuron has dendrites, a cell body and an axon.
- The resting membrane potential is about −70 mV.
- Depolarisation opens sodium channels, generating an action potential.
- Repolarisation restores the potential by potassium movement.
- Impulses jump between nodes of Ranvier in myelinated axons, speeding conduction.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why myelinated axons conduct faster.** Myelin insulates the axon except at nodes, so the impulse jumps from node to node — saltatory conduction.

2. **Local anaesthetics.** They block sodium channels, preventing action potentials and therefore pain signals.

**Practice questions**

1. The resting potential of a neuron is about: (A) −70 mV (B) +70 mV (C) 0 mV (D) −140 mV
   - *Answer:* −70 mV. The inside is negative relative to the outside.
2. Depolarisation is caused by movement of: (A) Sodium ions inward (B) Potassium ions outward (C) Calcium ions inward (D) Chloride ions inward
   - *Answer:* Sodium ions inward. Sodium influx raises the membrane potential.
3. Why are impulses faster in myelinated neurons?
   - *Answer:* Conduction is saltatory, jumping between nodes of Ranvier rather than travelling continuously.

### 21.2 Synapses, Brain and Reflexes

Signals cross synapses chemically. The brain and spinal cord coordinate complex and reflex actions.

**Key points**

- Synaptic transmission uses neurotransmitters released by calcium-triggered exocytosis.
- Excitatory synapses depolarise the postsynaptic cell; inhibitory ones hyperpolarise it.
- The cerebral cortex handles higher functions; the cerebellum coordinates movement.
- The medulla controls breathing and heartbeat.
- Reflex arcs provide rapid responses to stimuli.

**Worked examples**

1. **Reflex arc.** A hammer tap on the knee stretches a tendon, triggering a sensory neuron that synapses in the spinal cord and excites a motor neuron — the knee jerks.

2. **Neurotransmitter balance.** Dopamine and serotonin influence mood and movement; imbalances are linked to Parkinson's disease and depression.

**Practice questions**

1. Neurotransmitters are released from the: (A) Axon terminal (B) Dendrite (C) Cell body (D) Myelin sheath
   - *Answer:* Axon terminal. Vesicles in the axon terminal release neurotransmitters.
2. Which brain region controls balance? (A) Cerebellum (B) Cerebrum (C) Medulla (D) Hypothalamus
   - *Answer:* Cerebellum. The cerebellum coordinates posture and balance.
3. What is a reflex arc?
   - *Answer:* The neural pathway that mediates a reflex action, from receptor to effector.

## Chapter 22. Chemical Coordination and Integration

### 22.1 Endocrine Glands and Hormones

Hormones are chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands into the blood, regulating growth, metabolism and reproduction.

**Key points**

- The pituitary gland is the master gland, controlling other endocrine glands.
- The thyroid gland produces thyroxine, regulating metabolism.
- The pancreas secretes insulin and glucagon to control blood glucose.
- The adrenal glands produce adrenaline and corticosteroids.
- The gonads produce sex hormones.

**Worked examples**

1. **Goitre.** Iodine deficiency reduces thyroxine production; the thyroid enlarges as it tries to compensate.

2. **Insulin and glucose.** After a meal, insulin helps cells absorb glucose; in diabetes this fails, so blood glucose remains high.

**Practice questions**

1. The master endocrine gland is the: (A) Pituitary (B) Thyroid (C) Adrenal (D) Pancreas
   - *Answer:* Pituitary. The pituitary controls other endocrine glands.
2. Insulin is secreted by the: (A) Pancreas (B) Thyroid (C) Adrenal (D) Pituitary
   - *Answer:* Pancreas. Beta cells of the pancreas secrete insulin.
3. Why is iodine necessary in the diet?
   - *Answer:* It is needed to synthesise thyroid hormones; deficiency causes goitre and hypothyroidism.

### 22.2 Mechanism of Hormone Action

Hormones act on target cells through receptors. Water-soluble hormones use surface receptors; lipid-soluble hormones enter cells.

**Key points**

- Protein hormones bind surface receptors and trigger second messengers such as cAMP.
- Steroid hormones enter cells and bind intracellular receptors, altering gene expression.
- Hormone levels are regulated by feedback loops.
- The hypothalamus links the nervous and endocrine systems.
- Hormonal imbalance causes diseases such as diabetes and hypothyroidism.

**Worked examples**

1. **Negative feedback.** High thyroxine levels inhibit TSH release from the pituitary, keeping thyroid activity in check.

2. **Steroid hormone action.** Cortisol enters cells and binds receptors that act as transcription factors, changing gene expression slowly but durably.

**Practice questions**

1. Steroid hormones act by: (A) Binding intracellular receptors (B) Binding surface receptors only (C) Entering the blood directly (D) Acting as enzymes
   - *Answer:* Binding intracellular receptors. They enter cells and affect gene expression.
2. A negative feedback loop: (A) Reduces the stimulus that started it (B) Increases hormone secretion indefinitely (C) Ignores hormone levels (D) Stops the gland permanently
   - *Answer:* Reduces the stimulus that started it. The response counteracts the initial stimulus.
3. Why does hormone action take longer than nerve action?
   - *Answer:* Hormones travel in blood, bind receptors and often alter gene expression, which takes time.
