## Chapter 1. Chemical Reactions and Equations

### 1.1 Writing and Balancing Chemical Equations

A chemical equation uses symbols and formulas to describe a reaction. Balancing shows that mass is conserved.

**Key points**

- Reactants are on the left of the arrow and products on the right.
- Physical states are shown as (s), (l), (g) and (aq).
- A balanced equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides.
- Conditions such as heat, light or a catalyst are written above the arrow.
- Combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement and redox are the main reaction types.

**Worked examples**

1. **Balancing hydrogen combustion.** H<sub>2</sub> + O<sub>2</sub> -> H<sub>2</sub>O is balanced as 2H<sub>2</sub> + O<sub>2</sub> -> 2H<sub>2</sub>O: four H and two O atoms on each side.

2. **Decomposition of calcium carbonate.** CaCO<sub>3</sub> -> CaO + CO<sub>2</sub> on heating. One compound splits into a solid oxide and a gas.

**Practice questions**

1. In a balanced chemical equation, the number of atoms of each element is: (A) Equal on both sides (B) Greater on the reactant side (C) Greater on the product side (D) Not counted
   - *Answer:* Equal on both sides. Balancing conserves mass.
2. The symbol (aq) means the substance is: (A) Dissolved in water (B) A solid (C) A gas (D) A liquid metal
   - *Answer:* Dissolved in water. aq stands for aqueous solution.
3. Balance: Fe + H<sub>2</sub>O -> Fe<sub>3</sub>O<sub>4</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>.
   - *Answer:* Three iron atoms and eight hydrogen atoms require 3Fe and 4H<sub>2</sub>O.

### 1.2 Types of Chemical Reactions and Redox

Reactions are classified by how atoms rearrange. Oxidation involves loss of electrons or gain of oxygen; reduction is the reverse.

**Key points**

- Combination: A + B -> AB, such as CaO + H<sub>2</sub>O -> Ca(OH)<sub>2</sub>.
- Decomposition: AB -> A + B, such as electrolysis of water.
- Displacement: a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its salt.
- Double displacement: ions swap partners, often forming a precipitate.
- In redox, oxidation and reduction happen together.

**Worked examples**

1. **Displacement in a test tube.** Put an iron nail in copper sulphate solution; the blue colour fades and a brown copper coating appears because iron is more reactive and displaces copper.

2. **Rusting as a redox reaction.** Iron is oxidised to iron oxide while oxygen is reduced. Moist air speeds up both halves of the process.

**Practice questions**

1. Which is a decomposition reaction? (A) CaCO<sub>3</sub> -> CaO + CO<sub>2</sub> (B) CaO + H<sub>2</sub>O -> Ca(OH)<sub>2</sub> (C) Fe + CuSO<sub>4</sub> -> FeSO<sub>4</sub> + Cu (D) NaOH + HCl -> NaCl + H<sub>2</sub>O
   - *Answer:* CaCO<sub>3</sub> -> CaO + CO<sub>2</sub>. One compound splits into simpler substances.
2. In the reaction Zn + CuSO<sub>4</sub> -> ZnSO<sub>4</sub> + Cu, zinc is: (A) Oxidised (B) Reduced (C) Unchanged (D) A catalyst
   - *Answer:* Oxidised. Zinc loses electrons and is oxidised while copper ions are reduced.
3. Why is the reaction between iron and copper sulphate a displacement reaction?
   - *Answer:* Iron is more reactive than copper, so it displaces copper from its salt solution.

## Chapter 2. Acids, Bases and Salts

### 2.1 Acids, Bases and the pH Scale

Acids release H<sup>+</sup> ions in solution; bases release OH<sup>−</sup> ions. Their strength is measured on the pH scale.

**Key points**

- Acids turn blue litmus red; bases turn red litmus blue.
- Strong acids (HCl, H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>) ionise completely; weak acids (acetic acid) ionise partially.
- pH below 7 is acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 basic.
- The pH scale is logarithmic: a change of 1 unit means a tenfold change in H<sup>+</sup> concentration.
- Neutralisation: acid + base -> salt + water.

**Worked examples**

1. **Tooth decay and pH.** Bacteria in the mouth produce acid from sugar, lowering pH below 5.5 and dissolving enamel. Brushing with a basic toothpaste neutralises the acid.

2. **Why dilute acid is added to water, not water to acid.** Dilution releases a lot of heat. Adding water to concentrated acid can make it spit and splash; adding acid slowly to water spreads the heat safely.

**Practice questions**

1. A solution of pH 11 is: (A) Basic (B) Acidic (C) Neutral (D) Strongly acidic
   - *Answer:* Basic. pH above 7 indicates a base.
2. Which acid is present in the stomach? (A) Hydrochloric acid (B) Sulphuric acid (C) Nitric acid (D) Acetic acid
   - *Answer:* Hydrochloric acid. Gastric juice contains HCl, which helps digest protein.
3. What happens when a solution of an acid is mixed with a solution of a base?
   - *Answer:* They neutralise each other to form salt and water.

### 2.2 Important Salts and Their Uses

Salts are formed by neutralisation and by reactions of acids with metals or carbonates. Many salts have daily uses.

**Key points**

- Sodium chloride: cooking, preservation, raw material for chlorine and NaOH.
- Sodium bicarbonate (baking soda): baking, antacid, fire extinguisher.
- Washing soda (Na<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub>·10H<sub>2</sub>O): softening hard water, cleaning.
- Bleaching powder: disinfecting water and bleaching cloth.
- Plaster of Paris: making casts and decorative moulds.

**Worked examples**

1. **Baking soda in the kitchen.** Baking soda plus an acid such as lemon juice releases carbon dioxide, which makes batter rise.

2. **Hard water and washing soda.** Calcium and magnesium ions make water hard. Washing soda precipitates them so soap lathers easily.

**Practice questions**

1. Baking soda is chemically: (A) Sodium bicarbonate (B) Sodium carbonate (C) Sodium chloride (D) Calcium carbonate
   - *Answer:* Sodium bicarbonate. NaHCO<sub>3</sub> is sodium hydrogen carbonate.
2. Plaster of Paris is: (A) Calcium sulphate hemihydrate (B) Calcium carbonate (C) Sodium chloride (D) Calcium oxide
   - *Answer:* Calcium sulphate hemihydrate. Heating gypsum gives CaSO<sub>4</sub>·½H<sub>2</sub>O, Plaster of Paris.
3. Why is baking powder used in cakes?
   - *Answer:* It releases carbon dioxide when heated and moistened, making the batter rise and become fluffy.

## Chapter 3. Metals and Non-metals

### 3.1 Properties and Reactivity Series

Metals lose electrons to form positive ions; non-metals gain or share electrons. Reactivity varies and is ordered in the reactivity series.

**Key points**

- Metals are malleable, ductile, sonorous and good conductors.
- Reactivity series: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au.
- More reactive metals displace less reactive ones from salt solutions.
- Metals react with oxygen to form basic oxides; some oxides are amphoteric (Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>, ZnO).
- Non-metals form acidic oxides and are usually poor conductors.

**Worked examples**

1. **Zinc in copper sulphate.** Zinc is above copper in the series, so it displaces copper, turning the blue solution colourless and depositing red-brown copper.

2. **Why gold occurs native.** Gold is at the bottom of the reactivity series, so it does not react with oxygen or water and is found uncombined in nature.

**Practice questions**

1. Which metal is most reactive? (A) Potassium (B) Gold (C) Copper (D) Silver
   - *Answer:* Potassium. Potassium is at the top of the reactivity series.
2. Aluminium oxide is: (A) Amphoteric (B) Only acidic (C) Only basic (D) Neutral
   - *Answer:* Amphoteric. It reacts with both acids and bases.
3. Why does iron displace copper from copper sulphate but copper cannot displace iron from iron sulphate?
   - *Answer:* Iron is more reactive than copper, so only the more reactive metal can displace the less reactive one.

### 3.2 Metallurgy, Corrosion and Alloys

Extracting a metal from its ore depends on its reactivity. Corrosion damages metals; alloys improve their properties.

**Key points**

- Highly reactive metals are extracted by electrolysis; less reactive ones by reduction with carbon.
- Roasting is heating a sulphide ore in air; calcination heats a carbonate ore without air.
- Thermite reaction: Al + Fe<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> -> Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> + Fe, used to weld railway tracks.
- Corrosion: rusting of iron needs both oxygen and moisture; galvanisation coats iron with zinc.
- Alloys are mixtures of metals that are harder and more corrosion-resistant.

**Worked examples**

1. **Galvanising a bucket.** Coating iron with zinc protects it because zinc corrodes first, sacrificing itself — cathodic protection.

2. **Stainless steel.** Adding chromium and nickel to iron produces a protective oxide layer, making the alloy resistant to rust.

**Practice questions**

1. The process of coating iron with zinc is called: (A) Galvanisation (B) Electrolysis (C) Roasting (D) Calcination
   - *Answer:* Galvanisation. Zinc coating prevents rusting.
2. Which is an alloy? (A) Brass (B) Copper (C) Zinc (D) Aluminium
   - *Answer:* Brass. Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc.
3. Why does iron rust in moist air but not in dry air?
   - *Answer:* Rusting needs both oxygen and moisture; dry air lacks water vapour.

## Chapter 4. Carbon and Its Compounds

### 4.1 Covalent Bonding and Hydrocarbons

Carbon has four valence electrons and forms four covalent bonds. It bonds with itself and many elements, giving millions of compounds.

**Key points**

- Covalent bonds form by sharing electrons; carbon forms single, double and triple bonds.
- Carbon shows catenation (self-linking) and tetravalency.
- Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen: alkanes, alkenes and alkynes.
- Alkanes (C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n+2</sub>) are saturated; alkenes (C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n</sub>) and alkynes (C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n−2</sub>) are unsaturated.
- Structural isomers have the same formula but different arrangements.

**Worked examples**

1. **Same formula, different molecules.** Butane and isobutane both have the formula C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>10</sub> but different branching patterns, so they are structural isomers with different properties.

2. **Why there are so many carbon compounds.** Carbon can form long chains, branches and rings and bond to O, N, S and halogens, producing an enormous variety of compounds.

**Practice questions**

1. The general formula of alkanes is: (A) C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n+2</sub> (B) C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n</sub> (C) C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n−2</sub> (D) C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>n</sub>
   - *Answer:* C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n+2</sub>. Saturated hydrocarbons have the maximum number of hydrogen atoms.
2. Which bond is present in ethene? (A) Double bond (B) Single bond (C) Triple bond (D) Ionic bond
   - *Answer:* Double bond. Ethene has a C=C double bond.
3. What is catenation?
   - *Answer:* The ability of an element, especially carbon, to form long chains by bonding with itself.

### 4.2 Functional Groups, Homologous Series and Ethanol

Functional groups give carbon compounds their chemical character. Homologous series share a general formula and show graded properties.

**Key points**

- Functional groups: alcohol (−OH), aldehyde (−CHO), ketone (−CO−), carboxylic acid (−COOH), halide.
- A homologous series has members differing by a −CH<sub>2</sub>− unit.
- Ethanol is a colourless liquid used in alcoholic drinks, fuels and disinfectants.
- Ethanol burns with a blue flame to give CO<sub>2</sub> and water; it reacts with sodium to release hydrogen.
- Ethanoic acid (vinegar) is a weak carboxylic acid.

**Worked examples**

1. **Ethanol and sodium.** 2C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>5</sub>OH + 2Na -> 2C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>5</sub>ONa + H<sub>2</sub>. The hydrogen comes from the −OH group, showing ethanol has an alcohol functional group.

2. **Ester formation.** Ethanol plus ethanoic acid with an acid catalyst gives ethyl ethanoate, an ester with a fruity smell used in perfumes.

**Practice questions**

1. The functional group −COOH is characteristic of: (A) Carboxylic acids (B) Alcohols (C) Aldehydes (D) Ketones
   - *Answer:* Carboxylic acids. −COOH is the carboxylic acid group.
2. The next member of the homologous series after methane is: (A) Ethane (B) Propane (C) Ethene (D) Methanol
   - *Answer:* Ethane. Adding a −CH<sub>2</sub>− unit to CH<sub>4</sub> gives C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>6</sub>, ethane.
3. Why is ethanol used as a fuel?
   - *Answer:* It burns with a blue flame releasing a large amount of heat and produces relatively less soot.

## Chapter 5. Life Processes

### 5.1 Nutrition, Respiration and Transportation

Life processes keep organisms alive: nutrition supplies matter and energy, respiration releases energy and transport moves substances around the body.

**Key points**

- Autotrophic nutrition: photosynthesis in plants; heterotrophic: animals and fungi.
- Digestion breaks food into absorbable molecules; villi absorb nutrients.
- Aerobic respiration: C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>12</sub>O<sub>6</sub> + 6O<sub>2</sub> -> 6CO<sub>2</sub> + 6H<sub>2</sub>O + energy.
- Transport in humans: heart, blood vessels and blood; in plants: xylem and phloem.
- Transpiration pulls water up and cools leaves.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why the heart has four chambers.** Separate oxygenated and deoxygenated blood flows allow efficient oxygen delivery to the body, needed by warm-blooded animals with high energy demands.

2. **Stomata opening and closing.** Guard cells swell with water to open stomata for gas exchange and close them to reduce transpiration when water is scarce.

**Practice questions**

1. Which process releases energy from glucose? (A) Respiration (B) Photosynthesis (C) Transpiration (D) Digestion
   - *Answer:* Respiration. Respiration releases energy in usable form.
2. Water and minerals in a plant move through: (A) Xylem (B) Phloem (C) Stomata (D) Cambium
   - *Answer:* Xylem. Xylem carries water and minerals upward.
3. Why is the small intestine long?
   - *Answer:* Its length and villi greatly increase surface area for absorption.

### 5.2 Excretion and Coordination of Life Processes

Excretion removes wastes; control and coordination keep processes in tune through nerves and hormones.

**Key points**

- Kidneys filter blood, producing urine; the nephron is the filtering unit.
- Excretion in plants uses transpiration and storage in leaves, bark and resins.
- The neuron conducts impulses; the synapse passes signals between neurons.
- The brain and spinal cord form the central nervous system; reflex actions protect the body quickly.
- Hormones such as insulin and thyroxine regulate metabolism; auxins control plant growth.

**Worked examples**

1. **Reflex action.** Touching a hot object triggers a reflex arc through the spinal cord, moving the hand away before the pain signal reaches the brain.

2. **Auxins and phototropism.** Auxin accumulates on the shaded side of a stem, causing cells to elongate, so the shoot bends towards light.

**Practice questions**

1. The functional unit of the kidney is the: (A) Nephron (B) Neuron (C) Alveolus (D) Nephridium
   - *Answer:* Nephron. Each kidney has about a million nephrons.
2. Which hormone lowers blood glucose? (A) Insulin (B) Glucagon (C) Thyroxine (D) Adrenaline
   - *Answer:* Insulin. Insulin helps cells take up glucose.
3. What is a reflex action? Give one example.
   - *Answer:* A rapid automatic response to a stimulus, such as withdrawing the hand from a hot object.

## Chapter 6. Control and Coordination

### 6.1 Nervous System and Reflexes

The nervous system detects stimuli and coordinates responses quickly through electrical impulses along neurons.

**Key points**

- The neuron has a cell body, dendrites and an axon; myelin speeds conduction.
- The synapse is the gap where a neurotransmitter carries a signal to the next neuron.
- The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord; the peripheral system connects to the body.
- Reflex arcs bypass the brain for speed and safety.
- The brain has the cerebrum (thinking), cerebellum (balance) and medulla (involuntary actions).

**Worked examples**

1. **Touching a flame.** The sensory neuron carries the impulse to the spinal cord, which sends a motor signal to arm muscles to withdraw the hand — before the brain consciously feels pain.

2. **Why reflexes matter.** Fast automatic responses prevent injury. If every reaction needed conscious thought, the response would be too slow.

**Practice questions**

1. The gap between two neurons is called a: (A) Synapse (B) Dendrite (C) Axon (D) Neurotransmitter
   - *Answer:* Synapse. Neurotransmitters cross the synapse.
2. Which part of the brain controls balance? (A) Cerebellum (B) Cerebrum (C) Medulla (D) Hypothalamus
   - *Answer:* Cerebellum. The cerebellum coordinates posture and balance.
3. Why are reflex actions faster than conscious actions?
   - *Answer:* The pathway through the spinal cord is shorter than the route to the brain.

### 6.2 Hormones and Plant Coordination

Hormones are chemical messengers that travel in the blood and regulate growth, metabolism and reproduction. Plants use hormones to respond to light and gravity.

**Key points**

- Thyroxine regulates metabolism; insulin controls blood sugar; adrenaline prepares the body for emergencies.
- Growth hormone controls growth; oestrogen and testosterone control sexual development.
- Plants: auxins (growth and phototropism), gibberellins (stem elongation), cytokinins (cell division) and abscisic acid (stress and dormancy).
- Tropisms are directional growth responses: phototropism, geotropism, hydrotropism and thigmotropism.
- Nastic movements are non-directional, such as the folding of touch-me-not leaves.

**Worked examples**

1. **Adrenaline rush.** During danger, adrenaline raises heart rate, opens airways and releases glucose, preparing muscles for fight or flight.

2. **Roots bending down.** Roots grow away from light and towards gravity (positive geotropism) and towards water (hydrotropism), helping the plant reach water.

**Practice questions**

1. Which hormone prepares the body for emergencies? (A) Adrenaline (B) Insulin (C) Thyroxine (D) Auxin
   - *Answer:* Adrenaline. Adrenaline triggers the fight-or-flight response.
2. Growth of a shoot towards light is called: (A) Positive phototropism (B) Negative geotropism (C) Hydrotropism (D) Nastic movement
   - *Answer:* Positive phototropism. Shoots bend towards light.
3. Why is the endocrine system slower but longer-lasting than the nervous system?
   - *Answer:* Hormones travel in the blood and act gradually but sustain effects for longer.

## Chapter 7. How Do Organisms Reproduce?

### 7.1 Asexual and Sexual Reproduction

Reproduction may be asexual (one parent, identical offspring) or sexual (two parents, genetic variation). Variation helps species survive change.

**Key points**

- Asexual methods: binary fission (Amoeba), budding (Hydra, yeast), fragmentation (Spirogyra), regeneration (Planaria), vegetative propagation and spore formation.
- Sexual reproduction involves gametes: sperm and egg.
- Fertilisation may be external (frogs, fish) or internal (humans, mammals).
- In flowering plants, pollination leads to fertilisation and seed formation.
- Variation from sexual reproduction is the raw material for evolution.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why gardeners use cuttings.** A stem cutting produces a plant identical to the parent, preserving desirable traits such as fruit quality or flower colour.

2. **Cross-pollination advantage.** Pollen from a different plant brings new gene combinations, so offspring may be more disease-resistant.

**Practice questions**

1. Which organism reproduces by budding? (A) Hydra (B) Amoeba (C) Spirogyra (D) Planaria
   - *Answer:* Hydra. Hydra forms buds that detach.
2. Genetic variation is greatest in: (A) Sexual reproduction (B) Binary fission (C) Budding (D) Vegetative propagation
   - *Answer:* Sexual reproduction. Two parents contribute different gene sets.
3. What is the advantage of sexual reproduction?
   - *Answer:* It produces genetic variation, helping populations adapt to changing environments.

### 7.2 Human Reproduction and Reproductive Health

Human reproduction involves specialised organs and hormones. Reproductive health includes hygiene, safe behaviour and access to information.

**Key points**

- Male reproductive system: testes produce sperm and testosterone; sperm travel through the vas deferens.
- Female reproductive system: ovaries produce eggs; fertilisation occurs in the fallopian tube.
- The embryo implants in the uterus and develops with the placenta nourishing it.
- Contraception prevents pregnancy; barrier methods also reduce the risk of sexually transmitted infections.
- Reproductive health includes hygiene, nutrition and avoidance of harmful substances.

**Worked examples**

1. **The journey of the egg.** An egg is released from an ovary, travels into the fallopian tube and, if fertilised, implants in the uterus. If not fertilised, it disintegrates and the lining is shed during menstruation.

2. **Why prenatal care matters.** Good nutrition, avoidance of alcohol and tobacco and regular check-ups reduce risks to both mother and foetus.

**Practice questions**

1. Fertilisation in humans occurs in the: (A) Fallopian tube (B) Uterus (C) Ovary (D) Vagina
   - *Answer:* Fallopian tube. Sperm meets the egg in the fallopian tube.
2. Which organ produces sperm? (A) Testis (B) Ovary (C) Uterus (D) Prostate
   - *Answer:* Testis. The testes produce sperm and testosterone.
3. What is the function of the placenta?
   - *Answer:* It exchanges oxygen, nutrients and wastes between mother and foetus.

## Chapter 8. Heredity and Evolution

### 8.1 Mendel's Laws and Inheritance

Traits are passed from parents to offspring through genes. Mendel's experiments with pea plants established the basic laws of inheritance.

**Key points**

- Each trait is controlled by a pair of alleles; one may be dominant, the other recessive.
- Law of segregation: alleles separate during gamete formation.
- Law of independent assortment: different traits are inherited independently (for unlinked genes).
- Phenotype is the visible trait; genotype is the allele combination (TT, Tt, tt).
- Sex determination in humans: XX female, XY male.

**Worked examples**

1. **Monohybrid cross.** Crossing tall (TT) with dwarf (tt) pea plants gives all tall Tt offspring in F1, and a 3 tall : 1 dwarf ratio in F2.

2. **Why some traits skip a generation.** A recessive allele is hidden in a heterozygous carrier but appears again when two carriers have offspring.

**Practice questions**

1. In a monohybrid cross, the F2 ratio is: (A) 3 : 1 (B) 1 : 1 (C) 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 (D) 2 : 1
   - *Answer:* 3 : 1. The phenotypic ratio is 3 dominant : 1 recessive.
2. In humans, the male sex chromosomes are: (A) XY (B) XX (C) YY (D) XO
   - *Answer:* XY. Males have one X and one Y chromosome.
3. State Mendel's law of segregation.
   - *Answer:* The two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete carries only one allele.

### 8.2 Evolution and Speciation

Evolution is change in inherited characteristics of populations over generations. It is driven by variation, selection and isolation.

**Key points**

- Variation arises from mutation and sexual reproduction.
- Natural selection favours traits that improve survival and reproduction.
- Speciation happens when populations become reproductively isolated.
- Evidence comes from fossils, homologous organs and molecular similarities.
- Human evolution is traced through a series of hominid fossils.

**Worked examples**

1. **Peppered moths.** During the Industrial Revolution, dark moths were better camouflaged on soot-covered trees and survived better, so their frequency rose — natural selection in action.

2. **Homologous organs.** The forelimbs of humans, whales and bats have similar bone structure but different functions, indicating common ancestry.

**Practice questions**

1. Natural selection acts on: (A) Variation within a population (B) A single individual (C) Genes directly (D) Fossils
   - *Answer:* Variation within a population. Individuals with advantageous traits leave more offspring.
2. Homologous organs indicate: (A) Common ancestry (B) Convergent evolution (C) No evolution (D) Random mutation
   - *Answer:* Common ancestry. Similar structure suggests a shared ancestor.
3. Why does sexual reproduction speed up evolution?
   - *Answer:* It creates more genetic variation, giving natural selection more material to act on.

## Chapter 9. Light — Reflection and Refraction

### 9.1 Reflection at Spherical Mirrors

Spherical mirrors focus or spread light. Their images depend on the object's position relative to the focus.

**Key points**

- Concave mirrors converge light and can form real or virtual images; convex mirrors diverge.
- Mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f, with sign convention from the pole.
- Magnification m = h'/h = −v/u.
- For a concave mirror, f is negative in the standard sign convention; R = 2f.
- Uses: concave — headlights, shaving mirrors; convex — rear-view mirrors.

**Worked examples**

1. **Object at infinity.** Rays parallel to the axis converge at the focus of a concave mirror, forming a real, inverted and highly diminished image.

2. **Which mirror for a dentist.** A concave mirror gives a magnified virtual image when the object is inside the focus, so dentists use it to see teeth clearly.

**Practice questions**

1. A concave mirror can form: (A) Real and virtual images (B) Only real images (C) Only virtual images (D) No image
   - *Answer:* Real and virtual images. Depending on object position, it forms both types.
2. The focal length of a spherical mirror is: (A) Half of the radius of curvature (B) Twice the radius (C) Equal to the radius (D) Quarter of the radius
   - *Answer:* Half of the radius of curvature. f = R/2.
3. State two uses of a convex mirror.
   - *Answer:* Rear-view mirrors in vehicles and security mirrors in shops, because they give a wide field of view.

### 9.2 Refraction, Lenses and the Eye

Refraction bends light at boundaries between media. Lenses use refraction to form images, and the eye is a natural optical instrument.

**Key points**

- Refractive index n = speed in vacuum ÷ speed in medium.
- Convex lens: converging, can form real images; concave lens: diverging, always virtual.
- Lens formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f; power P = 1/f (in metres), measured in dioptres.
- Myopia is corrected with a concave lens; hypermetropia with a convex lens; presbyopia with bifocals.
- Dispersion splits white light into colours; a prism and raindrops produce spectra.

**Worked examples**

1. **Power of a lens.** A lens with focal length 0.25 m has power P = 1/0.25 = +4 dioptres. A negative power means a diverging lens.

2. **Why a pond looks shallower.** Light from the bottom bends away from the normal as it leaves water, so the apparent depth is less than the real depth.

**Practice questions**

1. The power of a lens is measured in: (A) Dioptres (B) Newtons (C) Watts (D) Joules
   - *Answer:* Dioptres. Power is the reciprocal of focal length in metres.
2. Myopia is corrected using a: (A) Concave lens (B) Convex lens (C) Cylindrical lens (D) Plane mirror
   - *Answer:* Concave lens. A diverging lens moves the image back onto the retina.
3. What is the refractive index of a medium whose light speed is half that in vacuum?
   - *Answer:* n = c/v = c/(c/2) = 2.

## Chapter 10. The Human Eye and the Colourful World

### 10.1 The Human Eye

The eye focuses light on the retina, where photoreceptors convert it into nerve signals. It adjusts for distance by accommodation.

**Key points**

- Cornea refracts most of the light; the lens fine-tunes focus; the iris controls pupil size.
- Rods detect dim light; cones detect colour.
- The optic nerve carries signals to the brain, which interprets the inverted image.
- Accommodation is the ciliary muscle's adjustment of lens curvature.
- Persistence of vision makes still images appear continuous in films.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why reading in dim light strains the eyes.** The iris opens the pupil wide, and the ciliary muscles work hard to focus. Prolonged strain causes fatigue and headaches.

2. **The blind spot.** Where the optic nerve leaves the retina there are no photoreceptors, so images falling there are not seen.

**Practice questions**

1. The part of the eye that controls the amount of light is the: (A) Iris (B) Cornea (C) Retina (D) Lens
   - *Answer:* Iris. The iris regulates pupil size.
2. Which cells detect colour? (A) Cones (B) Rods (C) Nerve cells (D) Ciliary muscles
   - *Answer:* Cones. Cones are colour-sensitive.
3. What is accommodation of the eye?
   - *Answer:* The ability of the eye lens to change focal length to focus on objects at different distances.

### 10.2 Dispersion, Scattering and Atmospheric Optics

White light splits into colours when refracted at different angles. Scattering explains the blue sky and red sunsets.

**Key points**

- Dispersion occurs because each colour has a different refractive index.
- A rainbow forms when raindrops refract, disperse and internally reflect sunlight.
- Rayleigh scattering: shorter wavelengths scatter more, making the sky blue.
- At sunset, light travels farther and blue is scattered away, leaving red and orange.
- Atmospheric refraction makes the Sun visible before actual sunrise.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why the sky is blue but sunsets are red.** Blue light scatters more in all directions, so the sky looks blue. Near the horizon, light passes through more air, losing blue and leaving red.

2. **Advanced sunrise.** Atmospheric refraction bends sunlight around the curve of Earth, so we see the Sun about two minutes before it geometrically rises.

**Practice questions**

1. The sky appears blue because of: (A) Scattering of light (B) Reflection (C) Dispersion (D) Absorption
   - *Answer:* Scattering of light. Short wavelengths scatter most strongly.
2. Which colour deviates most in a prism? (A) Violet (B) Red (C) Green (D) Yellow
   - *Answer:* Violet. Violet has the highest refractive index.
3. Why does the Sun appear reddish at sunrise and sunset?
   - *Answer:* Light travels a longer path through the atmosphere, so blue light is scattered away and red reaches the eye.

## Chapter 11. Electricity

### 11.1 Current, Potential Difference and Ohm's Law

Electric current is the flow of charge. Potential difference drives current and resistance opposes it.

**Key points**

- Current I = Q/t, measured in amperes (A).
- Potential difference V = W/Q, measured in volts (V).
- Ohm's law: V = IR for metallic conductors at constant temperature.
- Resistance R = ρL/A; it increases with length and decreases with cross-sectional area.
- Series resistors add: R = R<sub>1</sub> + R<sub>2</sub> + …; parallel: 1/R = 1/R<sub>1</sub> + 1/R<sub>2</sub> + …

**Worked examples**

1. **Parallel resistors.** Two 6 Ω resistors in parallel give 1/R = 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3, so R = 3 Ω. The equivalent resistance is always less than the smallest resistor.

2. **A bulb and Ohm's law.** A 6 V supply across a 3 Ω bulb gives I = V/R = 2 A. Power P = VI = 12 W.

**Practice questions**

1. The SI unit of resistance is the: (A) Ohm (B) Ampere (C) Volt (D) Watt
   - *Answer:* Ohm. Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω).
2. Two 4 Ω resistors in series give: (A) 8 Ω (B) 2 Ω (C) 4 Ω (D) 16 Ω
   - *Answer:* 8 Ω. Series resistances add.
3. State Ohm's law.
   - *Answer:* Potential difference across a metallic conductor is directly proportional to current, provided temperature is constant.

### 11.2 Heating Effect and Electric Power

Current through a resistor produces heat. Electrical energy and power calculations are used to choose wires, fuses and appliances.

**Key points**

- Power P = VI = I<sup>2</sup>R = V<sup>2</sup>/R.
- Energy = P × t, measured in joules or kilowatt-hours.
- The heating effect is used in irons, heaters and fuses.
- A fuse is rated for a maximum current; exceeding it melts the wire and breaks the circuit.
- Earthing and correct insulation prevent shock and fire.

**Worked examples**

1. **Cost of electricity.** A 100 W bulb used for 10 hours consumes 1 kWh, one unit of electricity.

2. **Why wires have low resistance.** Copper wires are thick and made of a good conductor, so they waste little energy as heat; heating elements use alloys with high resistance to produce heat.

**Practice questions**

1. The commercial unit of electrical energy is the: (A) Kilowatt-hour (B) Joule (C) Watt (D) Volt
   - *Answer:* Kilowatt-hour. One kWh equals 3.6 × 10<sup>6</sup> J.
2. Power can be calculated as: (A) VI (B) V/I (C) I/V (D) Q/t
   - *Answer:* VI. Power is voltage times current.
3. Calculate the energy used by a 60 W lamp in 5 hours in kWh.
   - *Answer:* Energy = 0.06 kW × 5 h = 0.3 kWh.

## Chapter 12. Magnetic Effects of Electric Current

### 12.1 Magnetic Field and Field Lines

A current-carrying conductor produces a magnetic field. Field lines show the direction and strength of the field.

**Key points**

- A straight wire produces circular field lines around it; the right-hand thumb rule gives the direction.
- A solenoid produces a field like a bar magnet.
- Magnetic field lines never cross and are closer where the field is stronger.
- An electromagnet uses a current in a coil around a soft iron core.
- Field direction reverses when current direction reverses.

**Worked examples**

1. **Right-hand thumb rule.** Point your right thumb along the current; your curled fingers show the direction of the magnetic field around the wire.

2. **A doorbell electromagnet.** Current through a coil magnetises a soft iron core that pulls a hammer; the movement breaks the circuit and the cycle repeats.

**Practice questions**

1. Magnetic field lines around a straight wire are: (A) Circular (B) Straight (C) Elliptical (D) Square
   - *Answer:* Circular. They form concentric circles around the wire.
2. The direction of the magnetic field is given by: (A) Right-hand thumb rule (B) Ohm's law (C) Boyle's law (D) Newton's law
   - *Answer:* Right-hand thumb rule. The thumb rule relates current direction to field direction.
3. What is a solenoid?
   - *Answer:* A coil of many turns of insulated wire that produces a uniform magnetic field like a bar magnet when current flows.

### 12.2 Electromagnetic Induction and Electric Generators

A changing magnetic field can induce a current in a conductor — the basis of generators and transformers.

**Key points**

- Faraday's law: induced emf depends on the rate of change of magnetic flux.
- Fleming's right-hand rule gives the direction of induced current in a generator.
- An AC generator produces alternating current by rotating a coil in a magnetic field.
- In India, AC frequency is 50 Hz.
- Transformers change voltage; they work on AC, not DC.

**Worked examples**

1. **Bicycle dynamo.** A magnet spins near a coil; the changing field induces a current that lights the lamp. Faster pedalling gives a brighter light.

2. **Why transformers need AC.** Transformers rely on a changing magnetic flux. DC gives a steady field, so no voltage is induced.

**Practice questions**

1. Electromagnetic induction was discovered by: (A) Faraday (B) Newton (C) Ohm (D) Ampere
   - *Answer:* Faraday. Michael Faraday discovered induction in 1831.
2. A transformer works on: (A) Alternating current (B) Direct current (C) Static charge (D) Heat
   - *Answer:* Alternating current. Changing flux in AC allows voltage transformation.
3. State Faraday's law of induction.
   - *Answer:* The induced emf is proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux through the circuit.

## Chapter 13. Our Environment

### 13.1 Ecosystems and Food Chains

An ecosystem includes living organisms and their physical environment interacting together. Energy flows through food chains; nutrients cycle.

**Key points**

- Producers (plants) capture solar energy; consumers eat other organisms; decomposers break down waste.
- Energy decreases at each trophic level: the 10% law.
- A food web is a network of interconnected food chains.
- Bioaccumulation is the build-up of non-biodegradable substances along a food chain.
- Ozone depletion and greenhouse gases affect the whole biosphere.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why large predators are few.** Only about 10% of energy passes from one trophic level to the next, so top predators have much less energy available and their populations are smaller.

2. **DDT in food chains.** DDT accumulates in fish and then in birds that eat them, causing thin eggshells — a classic case of bioaccumulation.

**Practice questions**

1. Energy passed from one trophic level to the next is about: (A) 10% (B) 50% (C) 90% (D) 1%
   - *Answer:* 10%. The ten per cent law describes energy transfer.
2. Organisms that break down dead matter are: (A) Decomposers (B) Producers (C) Primary consumers (D) Top carnivores
   - *Answer:* Decomposers. Bacteria and fungi decompose organic matter.
3. What is a food chain? Give one example.
   - *Answer:* A sequence of organisms in which each eats the previous one, e.g. grass -> grasshopper -> frog -> snake.

### 13.2 Waste Management and Environmental Action

Human activities generate waste that must be managed. Reduce, reuse, recycle and treat waste to protect the environment.

**Key points**

- Biodegradable waste can be composted; non-biodegradable waste must be recycled or safely disposed.
- Segregation at source makes recycling efficient.
- Plastic pollution harms wildlife and enters food chains.
- Sewage and industrial effluent must be treated before release.
- Sustainable habits — saving energy and water, planting trees — reduce environmental pressure.

**Worked examples**

1. **A school compost pit.** Fruit peels, leaves and paper are layered in a pit and turned periodically; after weeks they become compost for the garden, reducing landfill waste.

2. **Plastic in the ocean.** Bags and bottles break into microplastics that are eaten by fish and can end up in human food.

**Practice questions**

1. Which waste should be composted? (A) Fruit peels (B) Plastic bags (C) Glass bottles (D) Metal cans
   - *Answer:* Fruit peels. Organic waste decomposes to compost.
2. Segregation of waste means: (A) Separating waste by type (B) Burning waste (C) Dumping waste (D) Burying waste
   - *Answer:* Separating waste by type. Separating biodegradable and non-biodegradable waste enables recycling.
3. Why is plastic pollution a serious problem?
   - *Answer:* Plastic is non-biodegradable, persists for centuries and harms wildlife and food chains.

## Chapter 14. Management of Natural Resources

### 14.1 Forests, Water and Conservation

Natural resources must be used in ways that allow renewal. Community participation makes conservation effective.

**Key points**

- Forests protect watersheds, soil and biodiversity and provide livelihoods.
- Dams and canals help irrigation but displace people and affect river ecology.
- Rainwater harvesting recharges groundwater.
- Community forest management, such as joint forest management, can improve forest cover.
- Sustainable use balances present needs with future availability.

**Worked examples**

1. **Chipko movement.** Local villagers hugged trees to prevent felling, showing that conservation succeeds when people have a stake in the resource.

2. **Watershed management.** Building check dams and planting trees in a catchment area slows runoff, recharges wells and improves water availability downstream.

**Practice questions**

1. Which practice recharges groundwater? (A) Rainwater harvesting (B) Flood irrigation (C) Deforestation (D) Burning waste
   - *Answer:* Rainwater harvesting. Harvested rainwater seeps into the ground.
2. The Chipko movement was related to: (A) Protection of forests (B) Mining (C) Fishing (D) Urbanisation
   - *Answer:* Protection of forests. People hugged trees to stop felling.
3. State two benefits of forests.
   - *Answer:* They prevent soil erosion and recharge groundwater (also support biodiversity).

### 14.2 Coal, Petroleum and Sustainable Energy

Fossil fuels are finite and polluting. Energy conservation and renewable alternatives are essential for sustainability.

**Key points**

- Coal and petroleum take millions of years to form, so they are non-renewable.
- Burning fossil fuels releases CO<sub>2</sub>, sulphur dioxide and particulates.
- Renewable options include solar, wind, hydel, biomass and geothermal energy.
- Energy-efficient appliances and public transport reduce demand.
- Sustainable energy policy combines efficiency, renewables and responsible consumption.

**Worked examples**

1. **Solar rooftops.** Rooftop panels generate electricity where it is used, reducing transmission losses and fossil fuel use.

2. **Why efficiency is the cheapest fuel.** An LED bulb uses a fraction of the electricity of an incandescent bulb for the same light, cutting demand immediately without new power plants.

**Practice questions**

1. Which is a non-renewable source? (A) Coal (B) Solar (C) Wind (D) Tidal
   - *Answer:* Coal. Coal is finite and non-renewable.
2. Burning fossil fuels adds ______ to the atmosphere. (A) Carbon dioxide (B) Nitrogen (C) Oxygen (D) Helium
   - *Answer:* Carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide is a major greenhouse gas.
3. Why should we conserve fossil fuels?
   - *Answer:* They are limited and pollute the environment; conserving them extends supplies and reduces emissions.
