## Chapter 1. Nutrition in Plants

### 1.1 Photosynthesis and Plant Nutrition

Green plants make their own food by photosynthesis, using carbon dioxide, water and sunlight, with chlorophyll capturing the light energy.

**Key points**

- Photosynthesis happens mainly in leaves, in the chloroplasts that contain chlorophyll.
- Raw materials: carbon dioxide through stomata, water and minerals through roots, sunlight as energy.
- Products: glucose (food) and oxygen (released).
- Stomata are tiny pores, usually more numerous on the lower surface of a leaf.
- The overall equation is 6CO<sub>2</sub> + 6H<sub>2</sub>O -> C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>12</sub>O<sub>6</sub> + 6O<sub>2</sub> in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll.

**Worked examples**

1. **Testing a leaf for starch.** Boil the leaf in water, then in alcohol to remove chlorophyll, wash it and add iodine. A blue-black colour shows starch was formed. A leaf kept in the dark shows no blue-black, proving light is needed.

2. **Why a potted plant in a dark cupboard yellows.** Without light, chlorophyll is not produced and photosynthesis stops. The plant uses up stored food and its leaves become pale.

**Practice questions**

1. Which gas is taken in during photosynthesis? (A) Carbon dioxide (B) Oxygen (C) Nitrogen (D) Hydrogen
   - *Answer:* Carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide enters through stomata and provides carbon for glucose.
2. The green pigment in leaves is: (A) Chlorophyll (B) Haemoglobin (C) Carotene (D) Melanin
   - *Answer:* Chlorophyll. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy for photosynthesis.
3. List the raw materials needed for photosynthesis.
   - *Answer:* Carbon dioxide, water, sunlight and chlorophyll (with minerals from the soil).

### 1.2 Other Modes of Nutrition: Parasites, Saprophytes and Symbiosis

Not all plants make their own food. Some depend on other plants, some on dead matter, and some live in partnership with other organisms.

**Key points**

- Parasites take food from a living host: Cuscuta (amarbel) on a host plant.
- Saprophytes feed on dead and decaying matter: fungi such as bread mould and mushrooms.
- Insectivorous plants such as the pitcher plant and Venus flytrap trap insects to obtain nitrogen.
- Symbiosis is a partnership in which both partners benefit: lichens (alga + fungus) and leguminous plants with Rhizobium bacteria in root nodules.
- Rhizobium fixes atmospheric nitrogen into usable compounds.

**Worked examples**

1. **Lichens: a partnership.** An alga lives inside a fungus. The alga makes food by photosynthesis and the fungus provides shelter and absorbs water and minerals. Both benefit, so the relationship is mutualistic.

2. **The pitcher plant.** The pitcher's slippery rim and digestive fluid trap and digest insects. The plant still photosynthesises, but the insects supply nitrogen that the poor soil lacks.

**Practice questions**

1. Cuscuta is an example of a: (A) Parasite (B) Saprophyte (C) Producer (D) Insectivore
   - *Answer:* Parasite. Cuscuta (amarbel) draws food from a living host plant.
2. Rhizobium bacteria help leguminous plants by: (A) Fixing atmospheric nitrogen (B) Making chlorophyll (C) Repelling insects (D) Absorbing sunlight
   - *Answer:* Fixing atmospheric nitrogen. They convert nitrogen gas into usable compounds in root nodules.
3. What is symbiosis? Give one example.
   - *Answer:* A partnership where both organisms benefit; for example lichens (alga and fungus).

## Chapter 2. Nutrition in Animals

### 2.1 The Human Digestive System

Digestion breaks food into small soluble molecules the body can absorb. In humans it follows a tube from mouth to anus, with glands adding enzymes along the way.

**Key points**

- Mouth: teeth grind food and saliva (amylase) begins starch digestion.
- Oesophagus: peristalsis pushes the food bolus down to the stomach.
- Stomach: hydrochloric acid kills germs and pepsin digests protein.
- Small intestine: bile (from the liver) emulsifies fats, pancreatic and intestinal enzymes finish digestion, and villi absorb nutrients.
- Large intestine: absorbs water; undigested waste is removed through the anus.

**Worked examples**

1. **Follow a piece of bread.** Chewed bread mixes with amylase in the mouth, travels to the stomach, then the small intestine, where starch is finally broken into glucose and absorbed into blood through villi.

2. **Why the small intestine is long and folded.** Its length and millions of finger-like villi create enormous surface area for absorbing nutrients quickly. A short smooth tube would absorb far less.

**Practice questions**

1. Digestion of starch begins in the: (A) Mouth (B) Stomach (C) Small intestine (D) Large intestine
   - *Answer:* Mouth. Salivary amylase starts breaking starch into sugars in the mouth.
2. Villi are found in the: (A) Small intestine (B) Stomach (C) Oesophagus (D) Large intestine
   - *Answer:* Small intestine. Villi increase the absorbing surface of the small intestine.
3. What is the role of bile in digestion?
   - *Answer:* It emulsifies fats, breaking them into small droplets for enzyme action.

### 2.2 Teeth and Digestion in Other Animals

Teeth are adapted to diet: incisors cut, canines tear, premolars and molars grind. Ruminants and amoeba digest food differently from humans.

**Key points**

- Humans have 32 permanent teeth: 8 incisors, 4 canines, 8 premolars and 12 molars.
- Dental caries is caused by acid produced by bacteria acting on sugar stuck to teeth.
- Ruminants such as cows swallow grass into the rumen, where bacteria partly digest cellulose; the cud is then chewed again.
- Amoeba engulfs food with pseudopodia and digests it in a food vacuole.
- Starfish push out their stomach to digest prey outside the body.

**Worked examples**

1. **Chewing the cud.** A cow eats grass quickly, stores it in the rumen, then later brings it back to the mouth to chew again. Cellulose is tough, so double processing extracts far more energy.

2. **How an amoeba eats.** The amoeba pushes out pseudopodia around a food particle, surrounds it in a food vacuole, digests it with enzymes and absorbs the nutrients — digestion without a digestive system.

**Practice questions**

1. Incisors are used for: (A) Cutting and biting (B) Tearing (C) Grinding (D) Chewing the cud
   - *Answer:* Cutting and biting. Flat chisel-shaped incisors cut food.
2. Amoeba digests food in a: (A) Food vacuole (B) Stomach (C) Rumen (D) Pseudopodium
   - *Answer:* Food vacuole. Food is engulfed into a vacuole where enzymes break it down.
3. Why do ruminants chew the cud?
   - *Answer:* To digest cellulose more fully by reprocessing partly digested grass.

## Chapter 3. Fibre to Fabric (Woollens and Silks)

### 3.1 Wool: From Sheep to Sweater

Wool comes from the fleece of sheep and goats. Its curly, crimped fibres trap air, making it warm.

**Key points**

- Shearing removes the fleece; scouring washes it; sorting separates different qualities of fibre.
- Wool from the sheep's undercoat is soft and fine; outer hair is coarse and used for carpets.
- Wool is a poor conductor of heat, so it prevents body heat from escaping.
- Woollen clothes are best in winter because they insulate; silk and cotton suit summer.
- Angora wool comes from the Angora rabbit, mohair from the Angora goat, cashmere from the Kashmir goat.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why wool feels warm but not hot.** Wool traps air between its crimped fibres. Air conducts heat poorly, so body heat stays in. Wool does not generate heat itself; it slows heat loss.

2. **From fleece to yarn.** After shearing and scouring, fibres are carded to straighten them, spun into yarn, dyed and knitted or woven into sweaters. Each step improves fineness and strength.

**Practice questions**

1. The fleece of a sheep is removed by: (A) Shearing (B) Sorting (C) Scouring (D) Dyeing
   - *Answer:* Shearing. Shearing is the removal of fleece.
2. Wool is warm because it: (A) Traps air between fibres (B) Attracts sunlight (C) Produces heat (D) Contains water
   - *Answer:* Traps air between fibres. Trapped air insulates, slowing heat loss.
3. Name two animals other than sheep that give wool.
   - *Answer:* Goats (mohair, cashmere) and Angora rabbits.

### 3.2 Silk: The Life of the Silkworm

Silk is produced by the larvae of silkworm moths. Rearing silkworms to obtain silk is called sericulture, and each cocoon can yield a long continuous filament.

**Key points**

- A female silk moth lays hundreds of eggs that hatch into larvae (caterpillars).
- Larvae feed on mulberry leaves and grow through moults, then spin a cocoon of silk.
- Cocoons are treated in hot water to remove the gum and unwind the filament — this kills the pupa, which raises ethical questions.
- Silk is strong, lustrous, elastic and absorbs moisture well.
- Ahimsa silk is obtained without killing the silkworm, though the filament is shorter.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why cocoons are boiled.** The silk thread is glued together by sericin. Hot water softens the gum so filaments can be unwound without breaking. The same process prevents the moth from emerging, which is why some producers use ahimsa silk methods instead.

2. **Mulberry and the silk cycle.** Butterflies and moths have a four-stage life cycle: egg, larva, pupa, adult. Silk is harvested at the pupa stage, so sericulture depends on healthy mulberry plants and careful temperature control.

**Practice questions**

1. Sericulture is the rearing of: (A) Silkworms (B) Sheep (C) Honeybees (D) Fish
   - *Answer:* Silkworms. Silk farming is called sericulture.
2. Silkworms feed mainly on: (A) Mulberry leaves (B) Grass (C) Rice (D) Wheat
   - *Answer:* Mulberry leaves. Mulberry leaves are the preferred food of Bombyx mori larvae.
3. Why is silk considered a strong fibre?
   - *Answer:* Its long continuous filaments, twisted together, make a strong, flexible thread.

## Chapter 4. Heat

### 4.1 Temperature, Thermometers and Heat Transfer

Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Temperature is measured with a thermometer, and heat moves in three ways: conduction, convection and radiation.

**Key points**

- A clinical thermometer reads 35–42 °C and has a kink to hold the mercury reading.
- A laboratory thermometer reads −10 to 110 °C and has no kink.
- Conduction: heat travels through solids by direct contact (metal spoon in hot tea).
- Convection: heat moves in liquids and gases by circulation of currents.
- Radiation: heat travels without a medium, as from the Sun, and dark surfaces absorb more.

**Worked examples**

1. **Reading a clinical thermometer.** Wash it, shake the mercury below 35 °C, place it under the tongue for a minute and read at eye level. Normal body temperature is about 37 °C.

2. **Why coastal breezes blow.** Land heats faster than sea. Warm air over land rises and cooler sea air moves in, making a sea breeze in the day; the direction reverses at night as land cools faster.

**Practice questions**

1. Heat transfer through a metal rod is called: (A) Conduction (B) Convection (C) Radiation (D) Insulation
   - *Answer:* Conduction. Particles pass energy along by collision without moving as a whole.
2. A clinical thermometer usually reads from: (A) 35 °C to 42 °C (B) 0 °C to 100 °C (C) −10 °C to 110 °C (D) 30 °C to 60 °C
   - *Answer:* 35 °C to 42 °C. It is designed for body temperature range.
3. Why do we wear light-coloured clothes in summer?
   - *Answer:* Light colours reflect most radiation and absorb less heat.

### 4.2 Effects of Heat: Expansion and Change of State

Heating causes expansion and changes of state: solid melts, liquid boils, vapour condenses and liquid freezes. Every change of state happens at a fixed temperature for a given substance.

**Key points**

- Solids expand on heating; bimetallic strips bend and are used in thermostats and fire alarms.
- Melting point of ice is 0 °C; boiling point of water is 100 °C at sea level.
- During melting or boiling the temperature stays constant even though heat is being supplied — the heat is used to break bonds between particles (latent heat).
- Cooling curves mirror heating curves and are used in refrigeration and air conditioning.
- Water is unusual: it expands on freezing, which is why ice floats and pipes burst in winter.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why ice melts at a constant 0 °C.** A thermometer in melting ice stays at 0 °C until all the ice has melted, although heat is being added. The extra energy goes into changing the arrangement of molecules, not into raising temperature.

2. **Bimetallic strip in a fire alarm.** Brass expands more than iron for the same temperature rise, so a brass-iron strip curls when heated and closes an electric circuit that rings the alarm.

**Practice questions**

1. During boiling, the temperature of water: (A) Remains constant (B) Increases steadily (C) Decreases (D) Doubles
   - *Answer:* Remains constant. Heat supplied goes into changing state, so temperature does not rise.
2. Which liquid expands the most on heating (typical)? (A) Alcohol (B) Water (C) Mercury (D) Glycerine
   - *Answer:* Alcohol. Alcohol expands more than mercury, which is why it is used in cold-climate thermometers.
3. Why does ice float on water?
   - *Answer:* Ice is less dense than water because water expands on freezing.

## Chapter 5. Acids, Bases and Salts

### 5.1 Acids and Bases in Everyday Life

Acids taste sour and turn blue litmus red; bases taste bitter and feel soapy, and turn red litmus blue. Indicators tell us whether a substance is acidic or basic.

**Key points**

- Common acids: lemon (citric), curd (lactic), vinegar (acetic), stomach (hydrochloric), battery (sulphuric).
- Common bases: soap, lime water (calcium hydroxide), baking soda (sodium bicarbonate), milk of magnesia.
- Litmus, turmeric, china rose and phenolphthalein are indicators.
- Acids and bases react to neutralise each other, forming salt and water.
- Farmers treat acidic soil with lime and basic soil with organic matter to adjust pH.

**Worked examples**

1. **Turmeric as an indicator.** A paste of turmeric turns red-brown in soap solution (a base) and stays yellow in lemon juice. This is why a curry stain is hard to remove with soap but fades in acid.

2. **Neutralising a bee sting.** A bee sting contains an acid, so rubbing baking soda (a mild base) soothes it. A wasp sting is basic, so vinegar (an acid) is used instead.

**Practice questions**

1. Litmus paper turns ______ in an acid. (A) Red (B) Blue (C) Green (D) Yellow
   - *Answer:* Red. Acids turn blue litmus red.
2. Which of these is a base? (A) Soap (B) Lemon juice (C) Vinegar (D) Curd
   - *Answer:* Soap. Soap is basic and feels soapy.
3. What is neutralisation? Give one example.
   - *Answer:* A reaction between an acid and a base that forms salt and water; for example, hydrochloric acid plus sodium hydroxide gives sodium chloride and water.

### 5.2 The pH Scale, Salts and Their Uses

The pH scale measures acidity from 0 to 14. Below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral and above 7 is basic. Salts formed in neutralisation have many practical uses.

**Key points**

- pH papers and universal indicators give the approximate pH of a solution.
- Acid rain has pH below 5.6 and damages buildings, soil and water life.
- Common salt (sodium chloride) is used in cooking and to make chlorine and sodium hydroxide.
- Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) releases carbon dioxide and makes cakes rise.
- Washing soda (sodium carbonate) softens hard water; bleaching powder disinfects water.

**Worked examples**

1. **Making a salt.** Mix dilute hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide solution and add a few drops of indicator. At the neutral point the indicator shows pH 7. Evaporate the solution to obtain sodium chloride crystals.

2. **Salt and ice.** Sprinkling salt on icy roads lowers the freezing point of water, so ice melts at a lower temperature. The same principle is used in the ice-cream maker's salt-ice mixture.

**Practice questions**

1. A solution with pH 3 is: (A) Acidic (B) Basic (C) Neutral (D) Alkaline
   - *Answer:* Acidic. pH below 7 means acidic.
2. Which salt is used in baking? (A) Sodium bicarbonate (B) Sodium chloride (C) Calcium carbonate (D) Copper sulphate
   - *Answer:* Sodium bicarbonate. Baking soda releases carbon dioxide, raising dough.
3. Why is acid rain harmful?
   - *Answer:* It lowers soil and water pH and corrodes buildings and monuments.

## Chapter 6. Physical and Chemical Changes

### 6.1 Physical Changes

A physical change alters shape, size or state without forming a new substance, and is usually reversible.

**Key points**

- Melting wax, folding paper, dissolving sugar, cutting wood and breaking glass are physical changes.
- No new substance is formed, so the chemical composition stays the same.
- Physical changes can usually be reversed by changing conditions.
- The mass of the material is conserved.

**Worked examples**

1. **Wax and candle.** Melting wax is a physical change because the wax can be re-solidified; the appearance changes but the substance is still wax.

2. **Dissolving salt.** Salt dissolves in water but the salt can be recovered by evaporation. No new substance is formed.

**Practice questions**

1. Which is a physical change? (A) Melting of ice (B) Burning of coal (C) Rusting of iron (D) Baking a cake
   - *Answer:* Melting of ice. Melting changes state but not chemical identity.
2. A physical change is usually: (A) Reversible (B) Permanent (C) Chemical (D) Irreversible
   - *Answer:* Reversible. Most physical changes can be reversed.
3. Give two examples of physical changes in daily life.
   - *Answer:* Melting of wax and dissolving sugar in water.

### 6.2 Chemical Changes

A chemical change produces a new substance with different properties and is usually irreversible.

**Key points**

- Burning, rusting, digesting food, curdling milk and electrolysis are chemical changes.
- Signs include colour change, gas release, heat or light, smell and precipitate formation.
- Rusting needs both oxygen and moisture; painting or oiling prevents it.
- Chemical changes are used in cooking, metallurgy and medicine.

**Worked examples**

1. **Rusting iron.** An iron nail left in moist air turns reddish-brown. Iron reacts with oxygen and water to form hydrated iron(III) oxide. Keeping it dry or coated stops the change.

2. **Burning magnesium.** A magnesium ribbon burns with a bright white light and leaves a white powder, magnesium oxide — a new substance, so it is a chemical change.

**Practice questions**

1. Rusting requires: (A) Oxygen and moisture (B) Only nitrogen (C) Only sunlight (D) Only carbon dioxide
   - *Answer:* Oxygen and moisture. Both water and oxygen are needed for rust formation.
2. Which evidence suggests a chemical change? (A) Formation of a new substance (B) Change of shape (C) Change of state (D) Dissolving
   - *Answer:* Formation of a new substance. A new substance with new properties indicates a chemical change.
3. Why is burning considered a chemical change?
   - *Answer:* It produces new substances (ash, smoke, gases) that cannot be changed back.

## Chapter 7. Weather, Climate and Adaptations of Animals to Climate

### 7.1 Weather and Climate

Weather is the day-to-day condition of the atmosphere; climate is the average pattern of weather over many years. Elements include temperature, rainfall, humidity and wind.

**Key points**

- Weather changes hour to hour; climate is a long-term average.
- The Sun is the main source of energy that drives weather and climate.
- The Indian Meteorological Department records and forecasts weather.
- Coastal areas have a moderate climate because water heats and cools slowly.
- Deserts have extremes of temperature; polar regions are cold and dry.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why the desert is hot by day and cold at night.** Dry, sandy ground has little water to store heat. It heats fast in sunlight and radiates heat away fast after sunset, so the nights are very cold.

2. **Mumbai versus Delhi.** Mumbai, on the coast, has a narrow temperature range because the sea moderates it. Delhi, inland, swings from very hot summers to cold winters.

**Practice questions**

1. Climate refers to: (A) Average weather over many years (B) Weather today (C) Temperature only (D) Rainfall today
   - *Answer:* Average weather over many years. Climate is the long-term pattern of weather.
2. Which place is likely to have a moderate climate? (A) A coastal city (B) A desert (C) A mountain peak (D) The centre of a continent
   - *Answer:* A coastal city. Coastal areas are moderated by the sea.
3. Differentiate weather and climate.
   - *Answer:* Weather is short-term atmospheric conditions; climate is the long-term average.

### 7.2 Adaptations to Climate and Migration

Animals are adapted to their climate through body features and behaviour. When conditions become too harsh, some animals migrate.

**Key points**

- Polar bears: thick fur, a layer of fat, white camouflage and strong swimming ability.
- Penguins: dense feathers, huddling and fat for insulation.
- Desert animals: camels store fat; many are nocturnal and conserve water.
- Migration: Siberian cranes fly to India, and birds move between breeding and feeding grounds.
- Hibernation: bears and frogs slow their metabolism to survive cold or dry spells.

**Worked examples**

1. **Polar bear adaptations.** White fur blends with snow, a thick fat layer insulates, and a streamlined body helps swimming. Big paws spread weight on snow and ice.

2. **Siberian cranes and Bharatpur.** Each winter the cranes fly thousands of kilometres to wetlands such as Bharatpur in India to escape the frozen Siberian winter. Migration is an adaptation of behaviour.

**Practice questions**

1. Animals that sleep through winter are said to: (A) Hibernate (B) Migrate (C) Acclimatise (D) Adapt
   - *Answer:* Hibernate. Hibernation lowers body activity to save energy during cold or food shortage.
2. Thick fur and fat in polar bears help mainly with: (A) Insulation (B) Camouflage (C) Swimming (D) Speed
   - *Answer:* Insulation. Fat and fur reduce heat loss in extreme cold.
3. Why do some birds migrate?
   - *Answer:* To escape harsh weather and find food and breeding grounds.

## Chapter 8. Winds, Storms and Cyclones

### 8.1 Air Pressure, Wind and Storms

Air has weight and exerts pressure. Wind is air moving from high pressure to low pressure, and storms are strong winds produced by big pressure differences.

**Key points**

- The atmosphere exerts pressure in all directions; a balloon or the Magdeburg hemispheres show air pressure.
- Warm air rises and cooler air moves in, creating convection currents and wind.
- A cyclone forms over warm oceans when low pressure draws in moist air that rises and condenses.
- Thunderstorms bring lightning, thunder and heavy rain; lightning is an electric discharge.
- Cyclone warnings use satellites and radar to track storms and warn coastal areas.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why the balloon blows up.** Blowing into a balloon pushes air inside; the air pressure increases and stretches the rubber. Puncture it and the higher-pressure air rushes out.

2. **How a cyclone forms.** Warm ocean water evaporates; moist air rises and cools, forming clouds. The low pressure at the centre pulls in more air, and Earth's rotation gives the storm its spiral shape.

**Practice questions**

1. Wind blows from: (A) High pressure to low pressure (B) Low pressure to high pressure (C) Cold to hot always (D) Land to sea always
   - *Answer:* High pressure to low pressure. Pressure differences drive air from high to low pressure.
2. Cyclones form mainly over: (A) Warm oceans (B) Cold deserts (C) Mountains (D) Cities
   - *Answer:* Warm oceans. Warm, moist ocean air is the fuel for a cyclone.
3. What is lightning?
   - *Answer:* A huge electric spark caused by charge separation in thunderclouds.

### 8.2 Staying Safe and Preparing for Storms

Understanding storms saves lives. Safety rules differ for lightning, thunderstorms, cyclones and floods.

**Key points**

- During lightning, stay indoors or inside a car; avoid trees, open fields and metal objects.
- Cyclone warnings should be heeded: move to shelters and store water, food and medicines.
- Storm surge flooding follows a cyclone on the coast.
- Cyclone names are assigned by a regional committee from a list of names.
- Doppler radar and satellites improve early warning and reduce casualties.

**Worked examples**

1. **Lightning safety at a picnic.** If thunder follows a flash within seconds, lightning is close. Move into a building or a car. Never shelter under an isolated tree or hold a metal umbrella.

2. **Preparing for a cyclone.** Fishermen return to harbour, families move valuables to safe heights, store drinking water and food, and follow radio or phone alerts until the all-clear.

**Practice questions**

1. During lightning it is safest to: (A) Go indoors (B) Stand under a tree (C) Stand in water (D) Hold a metal pole
   - *Answer:* Go indoors. Buildings and closed cars conduct current safely around you.
2. A storm surge is: (A) Rise in sea level along the coast (B) A river flood (C) An earthquake (D) A heat wave
   - *Answer:* Rise in sea level along the coast. Low pressure and strong winds push sea water onto the coast.
3. Give two safety measures during a thunderstorm.
   - *Answer:* Stay indoors and avoid trees, water and metal objects.

## Chapter 9. Soil

### 9.1 Soil Formation and Composition

Soil forms slowly from weathered rock mixed with organic matter. It contains mineral particles, humus, water, air and living organisms.

**Key points**

- Soil types: sandy (large particles, drains fast), clayey (small particles, holds water) and loamy (a mix, best for crops).
- Humus is dark decomposed organic matter that makes soil fertile.
- Soil erosion is the removal of topsoil by wind or water; terracing and cover crops reduce it.
- About 45% of soil volume is mineral matter, 25% water, 25% air and a small percentage organic matter.
- Loamy soil is best for most plants because it holds both water and air.

**Worked examples**

1. **Testing soil texture.** Rub moist soil between your fingers. Sandy soil feels gritty; clayey soil feels smooth and sticky; loamy soil feels crumbly and forms a weak ball.

2. **Erosion on a bare slope.** On a slope without plants, rainwater runs fast and carries topsoil away. Planting grass or building terraces slows the water and holds the soil.

**Practice questions**

1. Which soil is best for growing most crops? (A) Loamy (B) Sandy (C) Clayey (D) Gravel
   - *Answer:* Loamy. Loam balances drainage and water retention.
2. Humus is: (A) Decomposed organic matter (B) Weathered rock (C) Pure clay (D) A mineral
   - *Answer:* Decomposed organic matter. Dead plants and animals decompose to form humus.
3. How can soil erosion be reduced?
   - *Answer:* Contour ploughing, terracing, cover crops, planting trees and avoiding overgrazing.

### 9.2 Soil as a Living System and Soil Pollution

Soil teems with life: bacteria, fungi, worms and insects break down matter and recycle nutrients. Pollution destroys this living system.

**Key points**

- Earthworms aerate the soil and mix organic matter; they are indicators of healthy soil.
- Rhizobium and other microbes fix nitrogen and decompose waste.
- Pesticides, plastics and industrial waste pollute soil and enter the food chain.
- Removing vegetation causes desertification; sandy soils become arid and unproductive.
- Crop rotation and organic farming maintain soil fertility.

**Worked examples**

1. **The role of earthworms.** Earthworms pull dead leaves underground, digest them and leave nutrient-rich castings. Their burrows let air and water into the soil, which is why gardeners value them.

2. **Plastic in soil.** Polythene bags block air and water movement, and roots cannot penetrate them. They remain in soil for centuries, reducing fertility.

**Practice questions**

1. Which organisms improve soil fertility by decomposing waste? (A) Microorganisms (B) Mammals (C) Birds (D) Fish
   - *Answer:* Microorganisms. Bacteria and fungi break down organic matter into nutrients.
2. Soil pollution may be caused by: (A) Excess pesticides and plastic (B) Rain (C) Sunlight (D) Air movement
   - *Answer:* Excess pesticides and plastic. Chemicals and non-biodegradable waste contaminate the soil.
3. Why is soil considered a living resource?
   - *Answer:* It contains many living organisms that decompose waste and cycle nutrients.

## Chapter 10. Respiration in Organisms

### 10.1 Respiration and Breathing

Respiration releases energy from food; breathing brings oxygen in and carbon dioxide out. It happens at the cellular level in all living things.

**Key points**

- Aerobic respiration needs oxygen: glucose + oxygen gives carbon dioxide + water + energy.
- Anaerobic respiration happens without oxygen and releases less energy; in muscles it produces lactic acid.
- Breathing rate at rest is about 15–18 breaths per minute in adults.
- The diaphragm and rib muscles drive inhalation and exhalation.
- Alveoli in lungs are tiny air sacs with a rich blood supply for gas exchange.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why we pant after running.** Muscles need more energy, so breathing and heart rate rise to supply oxygen and remove carbon dioxide. If oxygen is short, muscles respire anaerobically and lactic acid builds up, causing cramps.

2. **Yeast and fermentation.** Yeast respires anaerobically to produce alcohol and carbon dioxide from sugar — the basis of bread rising and of wine making.

**Practice questions**

1. The chief organs of breathing in humans are the: (A) Lungs (B) Gills (C) Spiracles (D) Leaves
   - *Answer:* Lungs. Lungs exchange gases in humans.
2. Anaerobic respiration in muscles produces: (A) Lactic acid (B) Oxygen (C) Glucose (D) Carbon dioxide only
   - *Answer:* Lactic acid. Lactic acid accumulates and causes fatigue.
3. Write the word equation for aerobic respiration.
   - *Answer:* Glucose + oxygen gives carbon dioxide + water + energy.

### 10.2 Respiration in Plants and Animals

Different organisms have different respiratory surfaces: gills in fish, spiracles in insects, skin in earthworms, and stomata in plants.

**Key points**

- Fish draw water over gills and absorb dissolved oxygen.
- Insects breathe through small pores called spiracles connected to tracheae.
- Earthworms absorb oxygen through moist skin.
- Frogs breathe through skin on land and lungs in air.
- Plants respire through stomata and root hair surfaces; oxygen is used day and night.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why fish die out of water.** Gills need water to keep their delicate filaments moist and supported. In air they collapse and the fish cannot obtain oxygen.

2. **Breathing in an insect.** Air enters spiracles along the abdomen and travels through branching tubes directly to cells. No lungs are needed.

**Practice questions**

1. Fish respire with: (A) Gills (B) Lungs (C) Spiracles (D) Stomata
   - *Answer:* Gills. Gills extract dissolved oxygen from water.
2. The openings through which insects breathe are called: (A) Spiracles (B) Gills (C) Nostrils (D) Stomata
   - *Answer:* Spiracles. Spiracles open into the tracheal system.
3. How does an earthworm breathe?
   - *Answer:* Through its moist skin by diffusion of gases.

## Chapter 11. Transportation in Animals and Plants

### 11.1 Blood Circulation in Humans

Blood carries oxygen, nutrients and wastes. The heart pumps blood through arteries, capillaries and veins, and the circulatory system links all organs.

**Key points**

- The heart has four chambers: two atria and two ventricles.
- Blood flows: body -> right atrium -> right ventricle -> lungs -> left atrium -> left ventricle -> body.
- Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins return blood; capillaries allow exchange with cells.
- Blood components: plasma, red blood cells (haemoglobin carries oxygen), white blood cells (defence) and platelets (clotting).
- The average heart rate of an adult is about 72 beats per minute.

**Worked examples**

1. **Reading a pulse.** Place two fingers on the wrist below the thumb. Each pulse is one heartbeat. After running, the pulse rate rises because muscles need more oxygen.

2. **Why veins have valves.** Blood in veins is at low pressure; valves prevent backflow and ensure blood returns to the heart, especially from the legs.

**Practice questions**

1. Arteries carry blood: (A) Away from the heart (B) Towards the heart (C) Only in lungs (D) Only in legs
   - *Answer:* Away from the heart. Arteries carry oxygen-rich blood away from the heart (except the pulmonary artery).
2. Which blood cells carry oxygen? (A) Red blood cells (B) White blood cells (C) Platelets (D) Plasma
   - *Answer:* Red blood cells. Haemoglobin in red blood cells binds oxygen.
3. Name the four chambers of the human heart.
   - *Answer:* Right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium and left ventricle.

### 11.2 Excretion and Transport in Plants

Waste must be removed: kidneys filter blood into urine, and plants use transpiration and storage to manage excess water and waste.

**Key points**

- Kidneys filter blood and produce urine; removal of waste is called excretion.
- The nephron is the filtering unit of the kidney.
- Plants transport water and minerals in xylem and food in phloem.
- Transpiration is water loss through stomata; it pulls water upward and cools the plant.
- Excess water is lost through guttation and transpiration; some waste is stored in leaves that fall.

**Worked examples**

1. **The xylem-phloem pair.** Xylem is dead, hollow and carries water up. Phloem is living and carries glucose in both directions. Together they connect roots to every leaf.

2. **Why a leafy shoot in a sealed bag shows droplets.** Water evaporating from leaves condenses on the bag. The droplets come from transpiration driven by evaporation from stomata.

**Practice questions**

1. The filtering unit of the kidney is the: (A) Nephron (B) Neuron (C) Alveolus (D) Stoma
   - *Answer:* Nephron. Each kidney has about a million nephrons.
2. Water and minerals are transported in plants by: (A) Xylem (B) Phloem (C) Stomata (D) Lenticels
   - *Answer:* Xylem. Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals upward.
3. What is transpiration? State one of its functions.
   - *Answer:* Loss of water vapour from leaves through stomata; it helps pull water up the plant and cools the leaves.

## Chapter 12. Reproduction in Plants

### 12.1 Sexual Reproduction in Plants

Flowers are the reproductive organs of plants. Pollination transfers pollen to the stigma, then fertilisation produces seeds inside a fruit.

**Key points**

- Parts of a flower: sepals, petals, stamens (anther and filament), pistil (stigma, style, ovary).
- Pollination may be self (same flower or plant) or cross (different plant); agents are wind, water, insects and birds.
- Wind-pollinated flowers are small, dull and produce lots of light pollen; insect-pollinated flowers are colourful and scented.
- After fertilisation, the ovary becomes the fruit and ovules become seeds.
- Seeds germinate when they get water, suitable temperature and air.

**Worked examples**

1. **From flower to mango.** A mango flower is pollinated by insects; the ovule is fertilised and the ovary grows into a mango. The flesh protects the single large seed, which can grow into a new tree.

2. **Seed dispersal.** Dandelion seeds have parachutes and travel by wind; coconut floats on water; burrs stick to animal fur. Dispersal reduces competition among seedlings.

**Practice questions**

1. Pollination is the transfer of pollen from anther to: (A) Stigma (B) Ovary (C) Sepal (D) Petal
   - *Answer:* Stigma. Pollen lands on the sticky stigma.
2. After fertilisation, the ovule becomes the: (A) Seed (B) Fruit (C) Flower (D) Leaf
   - *Answer:* Seed. The ovule develops into a seed and the ovary into a fruit.
3. Give two adaptations of insect-pollinated flowers.
   - *Answer:* Brightly coloured petals and scent (also nectar) to attract insects.

### 12.2 Asexual Reproduction in Plants

Some plants reproduce without seeds from a single parent, producing genetically identical offspring. Vegetative propagation, budding and fragmentation are common.

**Key points**

- Vegetative propagation: potato (eyes), ginger (rhizome), sugarcane (stem cuttings), rose (stem cuttings).
- Bryophyllum leaves produce plantlets on their margins.
- Yeast reproduces by budding; Spirogyra by fragmentation.
- Asexual reproduction is faster and preserves desirable traits but reduces variation.
- Grafting, layering and tissue culture are artificial methods used by farmers and gardeners.

**Worked examples**

1. **Potato eyes.** Each 'eye' on a potato is a bud. Cut a potato with an eye and plant it; a new plant grows from the bud, producing genetically identical potatoes.

2. **Tissue culture.** A tiny piece of plant tissue is grown on a nutrient medium under sterile conditions to produce many identical plantlets quickly — useful for orchards and endangered plants.

**Practice questions**

1. Which plant reproduces from leaf buds? (A) Bryophyllum (B) Potato (C) Sugarcane (D) Rose
   - *Answer:* Bryophyllum. Bryophyllum leaves develop tiny plantlets at the margin.
2. Fragmentation is seen in: (A) Spirogyra (B) Mango (C) Wheat (D) Moss
   - *Answer:* Spirogyra. The filaments of Spirogyra break into pieces, each growing into a new plant.
3. What is vegetative propagation? Give one example.
   - *Answer:* Growing a new plant from a vegetative part such as stem, root or leaf; for example, potato from a tuber.

## Chapter 13. Motion and Time

### 13.1 Speed, Distance and Time

Speed tells how fast something moves. Distance-time graphs let us compare motions visually.

**Key points**

- Speed = distance ÷ time; the SI unit is metre per second (m/s).
- 1 km/h = 5/18 m/s; to convert m/s to km/h, multiply by 18/5.
- Uniform motion covers equal distances in equal time, so the distance-time graph is a straight line.
- Non-uniform motion gives a curved distance-time graph.
- Odometer measures distance travelled; speedometer measures instantaneous speed.

**Worked examples**

1. **A train's speed.** A train covers 120 km in 2 hours, so its average speed is 60 km/h. Converting: 60 × 5/18 = 16.7 m/s.

2. **Reading a distance-time graph.** A straight, rising line means constant speed. A steeper line means a faster object. A flat horizontal line means the object is at rest.

**Practice questions**

1. The SI unit of speed is: (A) m/s (B) km/h (C) m (D) s
   - *Answer:* m/s. Metres per second is the SI unit.
2. A straight-line distance-time graph shows: (A) Uniform speed (B) Rest (C) Acceleration (D) Random motion
   - *Answer:* Uniform speed. A constant slope means constant speed.
3. A car travels 90 km in 1.5 hours. What is its average speed?
   - *Answer:* Speed = distance ÷ time = 90 ÷ 1.5 = 60 km/h.

### 13.2 Measuring Time and Simple Pendulums

Time is measured with clocks; before clocks, the motion of pendulums, sundials and water clocks was used. A simple pendulum's period depends on its length.

**Key points**

- One oscillation of a pendulum is one complete to-and-fro swing.
- The time period of a simple pendulum depends on its length and not on the mass of the bob.
- A grandfather clock uses a pendulum to keep time.
- The second is an SI unit of time; 1 minute = 60 s, 1 hour = 3600 s.
- Pendulum clocks need level ground and careful calibration.

**Worked examples**

1. **Timing oscillations.** Time 20 oscillations of a pendulum and divide by 20 to get the time period. Timing many oscillations reduces the effect of human reaction time.

2. **Why a longer pendulum swings slower.** The time period increases with length: T is proportional to the square root of length. A longer pendulum takes longer for each swing.

**Practice questions**

1. The time period of a simple pendulum depends on its: (A) Length (B) Mass of the bob (C) Colour (D) Material
   - *Answer:* Length. Length is the key factor; the mass of the bob has no effect.
2. 20 oscillations take 40 s. The time period is: (A) 2 s (B) 4 s (C) 0.5 s (D) 20 s
   - *Answer:* 2 s. Time period = 40 ÷ 20 = 2 s.
3. Why is a pendulum clock called a periodic motion device?
   - *Answer:* Because its bob repeats its to-and-fro motion at equal intervals of time.

## Chapter 14. Light

### 14.1 Reflection of Light

Light travels in straight lines and reflects from surfaces. Plane mirrors form virtual, erect and laterally inverted images.

**Key points**

- At reflection, the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection; both are measured from the normal.
- The image in a plane mirror is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.
- The image is virtual, erect and of the same size, and it is laterally inverted.
- A real image can be caught on a screen; a virtual image cannot.
- Concave mirrors converge light; convex mirrors diverge it.

**Worked examples**

1. **Mirror writing.** In a mirror a clock's numbers appear reversed. This is lateral inversion: the left side of the object appears on the left side of the image from the mirror's point of view — swapping left and right.

2. **Concave mirrors in headlights.** A bulb at the focus of a concave mirror sends light out as a parallel beam, giving a bright, far-reaching headlight beam.

**Practice questions**

1. The image in a plane mirror is: (A) Virtual and erect (B) Real and inverted (C) Real and erect (D) Magnified
   - *Answer:* Virtual and erect. Plane mirrors produce upright virtual images.
2. A concave mirror is used in headlights to: (A) Send a parallel beam (B) Block light (C) Make a real image on the road (D) Disperse light
   - *Answer:* Send a parallel beam. Parallel beams travel far because they do not spread.
3. State the laws of reflection.
   - *Answer:* The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, and the incident ray, the normal and the reflected ray all lie in the same plane.

### 14.2 Lenses and Dispersion of Light

Lenses refract light. Convex lenses converge rays to form real images; concave lenses diverge. White light splits into colours in a prism.

**Key points**

- A convex lens is thicker in the middle; it can form real, inverted images and is used as a magnifier.
- A concave lens is thinner in the middle and always forms a virtual, erect, diminished image.
- White light is made of seven colours: violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange and red (VIBGYOR).
- A rainbow is formed when sunlight is refracted and dispersed by water droplets.
- The image in a convex lens depends on the distance of the object relative to the focal length.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why a magnifying glass works.** Holding a convex lens close to a small object makes the object appear bigger because the image is formed farther away and subtends a larger angle at the eye.

2. **The rainbow.** Sunlight entering a raindrop slows and bends; different colours bend by different amounts, so they separate and reflect back to our eyes as a spectrum.

**Practice questions**

1. A convex lens is also called a: (A) Converging lens (B) Diverging lens (C) Plane mirror (D) Prism
   - *Answer:* Converging lens. Convex lenses bring parallel rays to a focus.
2. Which colour bends the most in a prism? (A) Violet (B) Red (C) Yellow (D) Green
   - *Answer:* Violet. Violet has the shortest wavelength and deviates the most.
3. What is dispersion of light?
   - *Answer:* Splitting of white light into its component colours by refraction.

## Chapter 15. Electric Current and Its Effects

### 15.1 Heating and Magnetic Effects of Current

Electric current produces heat and magnetism. These effects are the basis of heaters, fuses, electromagnets and electric bells.

**Key points**

- The heating effect depends on current, resistance and time: more current means more heat.
- A fuse is a thin wire that melts if current is too high, breaking the circuit.
- A filament bulb glows because a thin tungsten filament becomes very hot.
- A current-carrying coil behaves like a magnet; an electromagnet uses this effect.
- The magnetic effect is used in electric bells, motors, loudspeakers and cranes.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why a fuse saves appliances.** If a short circuit or overload occurs, a very large current flows. The fuse wire melts first and breaks the circuit, protecting the rest of the wiring.

2. **The electric bell.** A current in a coil magnetises a soft-iron core, pulling the hammer. As the hammer moves it breaks the circuit, the electromagnet releases, a spring pulls the hammer back and the cycle repeats, producing the ring.

**Practice questions**

1. The heating effect of current is used in: (A) Electric heaters and irons (B) Electric motors (C) Loudspeakers (D) Electromagnets
   - *Answer:* Electric heaters and irons. Heaters and irons convert electrical energy into heat.
2. An electromagnet is made by winding a coil around: (A) A soft iron core (B) A wooden block (C) A copper wire (D) A glass rod
   - *Answer:* A soft iron core. Soft iron becomes strongly magnetic while current flows.
3. What is the purpose of a fuse?
   - *Answer:* It melts and breaks the circuit when current is too high, protecting appliances and wiring.

### 15.2 Electric Circuits, Cells and Bulbs

A closed circuit allows current to flow. Cells supply energy, switches control the circuit and bulbs give light.

**Key points**

- A cell has positive and negative terminals; current flows from + to − in the external circuit.
- A bulb contains a tungsten filament with high resistance; it becomes white-hot and glows.
- More cells in series increase the voltage and make a bulb glow brighter.
- Symbols: cell, battery, bulb, switch, wire and ammeter.
- Circuit diagrams are drawn with standard symbols and must be closed for current to flow.

**Worked examples**

1. **Brighter with two cells.** Connect two cells in series with the same polarity and the bulb glows brighter, because the total voltage is greater and more current flows.

2. **Why a bulb filament is thin.** A thin filament has high resistance, so it heats up strongly and emits light. A thick wire would heat too little to glow.

**Practice questions**

1. Current flows from ______ terminal of a cell in the external circuit. (A) Positive (B) Negative (C) Both (D) Neither
   - *Answer:* Positive. Conventional current flows from + to − outside the cell.
2. The glowing wire in a bulb is made of: (A) Tungsten (B) Copper (C) Iron (D) Plastic
   - *Answer:* Tungsten. Tungsten has a very high melting point.
3. Draw a circuit with a cell, a bulb and a switch.
   - *Answer:* A rectangular loop of wire containing the cell symbol, the bulb symbol and a switch symbol, all connected in series.

## Chapter 16. Water: A Precious Resource

### 16.1 Water Availability and the Water Cycle

Fresh water is a tiny fraction of Earth's water and is constantly recycled by the water cycle. Groundwater is stored in aquifers and recharged by rain.

**Key points**

- About 97% of Earth's water is in oceans and is salty; only a small fraction is usable fresh water.
- The water cycle moves water through evaporation, condensation, precipitation and runoff.
- Groundwater fills the spaces in soil and rock above an impermeable layer.
- The water table is the upper level of the saturated zone and falls when extraction exceeds recharge.
- Dams, lakes, rivers and tanks are traditional sources of surface water.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why the water table drops.** If a town pumps more groundwater than rain can recharge, the water table falls and wells dry up.

2. **The cycle in a monsoon.** Evaporated ocean water condenses into monsoon clouds; rain falls, fills rivers and percolates into the ground, recharging wells.

**Practice questions**

1. Most of Earth's water is found in: (A) Oceans (B) Rivers (C) Lakes (D) Groundwater
   - *Answer:* Oceans. About 97% is salty ocean water.
2. The upper limit of the saturated zone is called the: (A) Water table (B) Aquifer (C) Dam (D) Reservoir
   - *Answer:* Water table. The water table marks the top of groundwater.
3. Why is groundwater important?
   - *Answer:* It supplies wells and boreholes for drinking water and irrigation, especially where surface water is scarce.

### 16.2 Water Management and Conservation

Water must be used sustainably. Rainwater harvesting, drip irrigation and reducing pollution all help.

**Key points**

- Rainwater harvesting collects and stores rain or sends it into the ground.
- Drip irrigation waters roots directly and saves water.
- Dams store water but displace people and affect rivers.
- Water pollution from sewage, chemicals and plastic reduces usable water.
- Traditional water structures such as tanks and bunds recharge groundwater.

**Worked examples**

1. **Rooftop harvesting.** Rain from a roof is channelled into a storage tank or recharge pit, saving water and replenishing groundwater.

2. **Saving water at home.** Fixing leaks, closing taps while brushing and reusing kitchen water for plants make a measurable difference.

**Practice questions**

1. Rainwater harvesting mainly helps to: (A) Recharge groundwater (B) Increase pollution (C) Waste water (D) Lower rainfall
   - *Answer:* Recharge groundwater. Collected rain is stored or allowed to seep into the ground.
2. Drip irrigation saves water because it: (A) Delivers water directly to roots (B) Floods the field (C) Sprays everywhere (D) Uses seawater
   - *Answer:* Delivers water directly to roots. Water goes to the root zone with little evaporation.
3. State two methods of conserving water.
   - *Answer:* Rainwater harvesting and drip irrigation (also fixing leaks and reusing water).

## Chapter 17. Forests: Our Lifeline

### 17.1 The Forest Ecosystem

A forest is a community of plants, animals, microbes and the physical environment. Its layers interact to recycle nutrients, hold water and support life.

**Key points**

- Forest layers: canopy, understorey, shrubs, herbs and forest floor.
- Decomposers break down leaf litter into humus, returning nutrients to the soil.
- Forests recharge groundwater, prevent floods and soil erosion and moderate climate.
- Forest products include timber, fuelwood, honey, gum, resins, medicines and rubber.
- The forest floor is dark and moist because very little light reaches it.

**Worked examples**

1. **Leaf litter to soil.** Leaves fall, decompose with the help of microorganisms and insects, and form humus, which makes the soil fertile. Nutrients return to the roots of living trees.

2. **Forests and floods.** A forest canopy slows rainfall and roots hold the soil, so water seeps into the ground instead of rushing down slopes and flooding rivers.

**Practice questions**

1. Nutrients of a forest are returned to the soil by: (A) Decomposers (B) Herbivores (C) Sunlight (D) Wind
   - *Answer:* Decomposers. Microorganisms break down litter into humus.
2. Which layer of a forest receives the least sunlight? (A) Forest floor (B) Canopy (C) Understorey (D) Shrub layer
   - *Answer:* Forest floor. The dense canopy blocks light from the floor.
3. Give two ways forests help the environment.
   - *Answer:* They recharge groundwater and prevent soil erosion and floods.

### 17.2 Conservation of Forests and Wildlife

Deforestation destroys habitats, disrupts water cycles and increases carbon dioxide. Conservation protects ecosystems and the people who depend on them.

**Key points**

- Deforestation causes habitat loss, soil erosion, floods and loss of biodiversity.
- Afforestation, protection of wildlife sanctuaries and community forest management help.
- Protected areas include national parks, wildlife sanctuaries and biosphere reserves.
- Sacred groves are forests protected by local communities.
- Reducing paper and wood use and recycling reduce pressure on forests.

**Worked examples**

1. **The chipko movement.** Villagers in the Himalayan hills hugged trees to prevent them from being felled, showing that forest conservation can begin with communities.

2. **Why biodiversity matters.** Each species has a role in the food web. Losing a predator can cause prey numbers to rise and damage vegetation, destabilising the whole forest.

**Practice questions**

1. What is deforestation? (A) Clearing of forests by cutting trees (B) Growing new forests (C) Protecting forests (D) A type of soil
   - *Answer:* Clearing of forests by cutting trees. Deforestation is the large-scale removal of forest.
2. A protected forest area for wildlife is called: (A) A wildlife sanctuary (B) A pasture (C) A plantation (D) A garden
   - *Answer:* A wildlife sanctuary. Sanctuaries protect animals in their natural habitat.
3. State two consequences of deforestation.
   - *Answer:* Loss of habitat and increased soil erosion and floods.

## Chapter 18. Wastewater Story

### 18.1 Wastewater and Water Treatment

Wastewater from homes, industries and farms contains harmful substances. Treating it makes it safe enough to return to rivers or reuse.

**Key points**

- Domestic sewage contains organic waste, detergents, soaps and human excreta.
- Treatment steps: bar screening, grit removal, sedimentation, aeration (bacterial digestion), filtration and disinfection.
- Aeration helps bacteria break down organic matter in activated sludge.
- Sludge is used as fertiliser after treatment; the treated water can be used for irrigation.
- Untreated sewage causes waterborne diseases such as cholera and typhoid.

**Worked examples**

1. **Following the water.** Water from the kitchen sink flows into a drain, then to a sewage treatment plant. Screening removes solids, bacteria digest organic matter, and chlorine kills remaining germs before the water is released.

2. **Why do we boil drinking water.** Boiling kills disease-causing microorganisms. Filtering and chlorination are other ways to make water safe.

**Practice questions**

1. Wastewater treatment plants use ______ to break down organic matter. (A) Bacteria (B) Oxygen gas only (C) Heat (D) Salt
   - *Answer:* Bacteria. Microorganisms digest organic waste in the aeration tank.
2. Untreated sewage in drinking water causes: (A) Waterborne diseases (B) Clean water (C) More oxygen (D) More fish
   - *Answer:* Waterborne diseases. Cholera, typhoid and diarrhoea spread through contaminated water.
3. List any three steps in wastewater treatment.
   - *Answer:* Screening, sedimentation and aeration (followed by filtration and disinfection).

### 18.2 Sanitation and the 3Rs

Better sanitation requires good drains, toilets and habits. Reducing, reusing and recycling water and waste protects rivers and groundwater.

**Key points**

- Drains should be covered and cleaned regularly to stop breeding of mosquitoes and flies.
- Toilets help prevent open defecation and the spread of disease.
- Rainwater harvesting reduces demand on rivers and groundwater.
- Using less detergent and avoiding chemicals in drains prevents water pollution.
- Wastewater can be recycled for gardening and industry after treatment.

**Worked examples**

1. **A clean school.** Covered drains, clean toilets, handwashing with soap and bins for wet and dry waste keep the school healthy and prevent water pollution.

2. **Reusing greywater.** Water from washing clothes or vegetables can be poured on plants after the soap is diluted, saving fresh water and reducing wastewater.

**Practice questions**

1. Which practice prevents water pollution? (A) Treating sewage before discharge (B) Dumping plastic in rivers (C) Washing clothes in ponds (D) Open defecation
   - *Answer:* Treating sewage before discharge. Treatment removes or kills harmful substances before release.
2. Open drains are unhealthy mainly because they: (A) Breed mosquitoes and spread disease (B) Look bad (C) Smell dry (D) Use water
   - *Answer:* Breed mosquitoes and spread disease. Standing water becomes a breeding ground for disease vectors.
3. Why should chemical waste not be poured into drains?
   - *Answer:* It poisons water, kills useful microbes and can contaminate groundwater.
