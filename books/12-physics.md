## Chapter 1. Electric Charges and Fields

### 1.1 Coulomb's Law and Electric Field

Charged objects exert forces described by Coulomb's law. The electric field describes the force per unit charge at a point.

**Key points**

- Coulomb's law: F = kq<sub>1</sub>q<sub>2</sub>/r<sup>2</sup>, with k = 9 × 10<sup>9</sup> N·m<sup>2</sup>/C<sup>2</sup>.
- Electric field E = F/q = kq/r<sup>2</sup>, a vector pointing away from positive charge.
- Field lines point from positive to negative and never cross.
- Superposition: the net field is the vector sum of individual fields.
- Electric flux φ = E·A cos θ, measured in N·m<sup>2</sup>/C.

**Worked examples**

1. **Force between two charges.** Two 1 μC charges 1 m apart repel with F = 9 × 10<sup>9</sup> × 10<sup>−6</sup> × 10<sup>−6</sup>/1 = 9 × 10<sup>−3</sup> N.

2. **Electric field of a point charge.** At 0.3 m from a 2 μC charge, E = 9 × 10<sup>9</sup> × 2 × 10<sup>−6</sup>/(0.3)<sup>2</sup> = 2 × 10<sup>5</sup> N/C.

**Practice questions**

1. The SI unit of electric field is: (A) N/C (B) C/N (C) N·m (D) J/C
   - *Answer:* N/C. Electric field is force per unit charge.
2. Electric field lines due to a positive charge point: (A) Away from the charge (B) Toward the charge (C) Along a circle (D) Nowhere
   - *Answer:* Away from the charge. Field lines point away from positive charges.
3. State Coulomb's law.
   - *Answer:* The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance.

### 1.2 Electric Dipole and Gauss's Law

A dipole consists of two equal and opposite charges. Gauss's law relates electric flux through a closed surface to enclosed charge.

**Key points**

- Dipole moment p = q × 2a, directed from −q to +q.
- Torque on a dipole in a uniform field: τ = pE sin θ.
- Gauss's law: φ = q<sub>enclosed</sub>/ε<sub>0</sub>.
- The field inside a conductor is zero in electrostatic equilibrium.
- Field due to an infinite sheet: E = σ/(2ε<sub>0</sub>); inside a spherical shell: zero.

**Worked examples**

1. **Field of a charged sheet.** A sheet with σ = 10<sup>−6</sup> C/m<sup>2</sup> produces E = 10<sup>−6</sup>/(2 × 8.85 × 10<sup>−12</sup>) ≈ 5.6 × 10<sup>4</sup> N/C, independent of distance.

2. **Torque on a dipole.** A dipole of moment 2 × 10<sup>−6</sup> C·m in a field of 10<sup>4</sup> N/C at 90° feels τ = 0.02 N·m.

**Practice questions**

1. Gauss's law relates electric flux to: (A) Enclosed charge (B) Surface area (C) Potential (D) Current
   - *Answer:* Enclosed charge. Flux depends only on the charge inside the closed surface.
2. The electric field inside a charged conductor is: (A) Zero (B) Maximum (C) σ/ε<sub>0</sub> (D) Infinite
   - *Answer:* Zero. Charges reside on the surface in equilibrium.
3. Why does a charged spherical shell produce no field inside?
   - *Answer:* By Gauss's law no charge is enclosed by an interior surface, so the flux and the field are zero.

## Chapter 2. Electrostatic Potential and Capacitance

### 2.1 Potential and Potential Energy

Electric potential is potential energy per unit charge. Equipotential surfaces simplify calculations of work.

**Key points**

- Potential V = kq/r for a point charge; potential is a scalar.
- Potential difference V<sub>AB</sub> = W<sub>AB</sub>/q.
- Potential energy of two charges U = kq<sub>1</sub>q<sub>2</sub>/r.
- Equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to field lines.
- The field is the negative gradient of potential: E = −dV/dr.

**Worked examples**

1. **Potential of a point charge.** At 0.2 m from a 1 μC charge, V = 9 × 10<sup>9</sup> × 10<sup>−6</sup>/0.2 = 4.5 × 10<sup>4</sup> V.

2. **Work in moving a charge.** Moving 2 C through a potential difference of 10 V requires W = qV = 20 J.

**Practice questions**

1. Electric potential is a: (A) Scalar (B) Vector (C) Tensor (D) Force
   - *Answer:* Scalar. Potential has magnitude but no direction.
2. Equipotential surfaces are ______ to electric field lines. (A) Perpendicular (B) Parallel (C) Inclined at 30° (D) Identical
   - *Answer:* Perpendicular. No work is done moving along an equipotential surface.
3. Why is the potential inside a charged conductor constant?
   - *Answer:* The field is zero inside, so no work is done moving a charge and the potential does not change.

### 2.2 Capacitors and Dielectrics

Capacitors store charge and energy. Their capacitance depends on geometry and the dielectric between plates.

**Key points**

- Capacitance C = Q/V; the unit is the farad (F).
- Parallel plate capacitor: C = ε<sub>0</sub>A/d; with dielectric C = Kε<sub>0</sub>A/d.
- Energy stored U = ½CV<sup>2</sup> = Q<sup>2</sup>/2C.
- Series: 1/C = 1/C<sub>1</sub> + 1/C<sub>2</sub>; parallel: C = C<sub>1</sub> + C<sub>2</sub>.
- A dielectric polarises and reduces the field, increasing capacitance.

**Worked examples**

1. **Energy in a capacitor.** A 10 μF capacitor charged to 100 V stores U = ½ × 10<sup>−5</sup> × 10<sup>4</sup> = 0.5 J.

2. **Effect of a dielectric.** Inserting a dielectric with K = 4 multiplies the capacitance by 4 and stores more charge at the same voltage.

**Practice questions**

1. Capacitance is measured in: (A) Farads (B) Coulombs (C) Volts (D) Ohms
   - *Answer:* Farads. The farad is the unit of capacitance.
2. Energy stored in a capacitor is: (A) ½CV<sup>2</sup> (B) CV (C) QV (D) V/C
   - *Answer:* ½CV<sup>2</sup>. U = ½CV<sup>2</sup>.
3. Why does a dielectric increase capacitance?
   - *Answer:* It polarises and reduces the effective field between plates, allowing more charge at the same voltage.

## Chapter 3. Current Electricity

### 3.1 Ohm's Law, Resistance and Drift Velocity

Current flows due to potential difference. Resistance depends on material, length and area.

**Key points**

- Current I = dQ/dt; conventional current flows opposite to electron flow.
- Ohm's law: V = IR at constant temperature.
- Resistance R = ρL/A; resistivity ρ is a material property.
- Drift velocity v<sub>d</sub> = I/(neA); typical values are very small.
- Current density J = I/A = nev<sub>d</sub>.

**Worked examples**

1. **Resistance of a wire.** A copper wire of length 2 m, area 10<sup>−6</sup> m<sup>2</sup> and resistivity 1.7 × 10<sup>−8</sup> Ω·m has R = 1.7 × 10<sup>−8</sup> × 2/10<sup>−6</sup> = 0.034 Ω.

2. **Drift velocity.** A 1 A current in a wire of area 10<sup>−6</sup> m<sup>2</sup> with n = 8 × 10<sup>28</sup> m<sup>−3</sup> has v<sub>d</sub> = 1/(8 × 10<sup>28</sup> × 1.6 × 10<sup>−19</sup> × 10<sup>−6</sup>) ≈ 7.8 × 10<sup>−5</sup> m/s.

**Practice questions**

1. Resistance of a wire is directly proportional to: (A) Length (B) Area (C) Current (D) Voltage
   - *Answer:* Length. R = ρL/A.
2. The SI unit of resistivity is: (A) Ω·m (B) Ω (C) V/m (D) A/m<sup>2</sup>
   - *Answer:* Ω·m. Resistivity has units of ohm-metre.
3. Why is drift velocity small even though lights turn on instantly?
   - *Answer:* The electric field propagates near light speed, so all electrons start moving almost simultaneously.

### 3.2 Cells, EMF and Kirchhoff's Rules

Circuits are analysed with EMF, internal resistance and Kirchhoff's rules.

**Key points**

- EMF ε is the maximum potential difference a cell can provide.
- Terminal voltage V = ε − Ir, where r is internal resistance.
- Kirchhoff's junction rule: sum of currents entering a junction equals sum leaving (charge conservation).
- Kirchhoff's loop rule: sum of potential changes around a loop is zero (energy conservation).
- A potentiometer and Wheatstone bridge measure EMF and resistance precisely.

**Worked examples**

1. **Internal resistance.** A cell of EMF 2 V with internal resistance 0.5 Ω delivering 1 A has terminal voltage 2 − 0.5 = 1.5 V.

2. **Wheatstone bridge balance.** The bridge is balanced when P/Q = R/S, so an unknown resistance can be found from three known ones.

**Practice questions**

1. Terminal voltage of a cell is: (A) EMF minus Ir (B) EMF plus Ir (C) Always equal to EMF (D) Zero
   - *Answer:* EMF minus Ir. Internal resistance causes a voltage drop.
2. Kirchhoff's junction rule is based on: (A) Conservation of charge (B) Conservation of energy (C) Ohm's law (D) Faraday's law
   - *Answer:* Conservation of charge. Charge entering a junction must leave it.
3. What is the internal resistance of a cell?
   - *Answer:* The resistance offered by the electrolyte and electrodes to current flow inside the cell.

## Chapter 4. Moving Charges and Magnetism

### 4.1 Magnetic Force and Motion of Charges

Moving charges experience magnetic forces and produce magnetic fields. The force is perpendicular to velocity and field.

**Key points**

- Force F = qvB sin θ; a charge at rest experiences no magnetic force.
- A charge moving along the field direction feels no force.
- Circular motion in a uniform field: r = mv/(qB).
- Cyclotron frequency f = qB/(2πm).
- Biot-Savart law gives the field of a current element.

**Worked examples**

1. **Radius of a circular path.** An electron with v = 10<sup>6</sup> m/s in B = 0.01 T has r = 9.1 × 10<sup>−31</sup> × 10<sup>6</sup>/(1.6 × 10<sup>−19</sup> × 0.01) ≈ 5.7 × 10<sup>−4</sup> m.

2. **Why a charge follows a helix.** If velocity has a component along the field, that component stays constant while the perpendicular component circles, producing a helical path.

**Practice questions**

1. A stationary charge in a magnetic field experiences: (A) No force (B) Maximum force (C) Electric force (D) Centripetal force
   - *Answer:* No force. Magnetic force requires motion.
2. Radius of circular motion in a magnetic field is: (A) mv/(qB) (B) qB/(mv) (C) mvB/q (D) qv/(mB)
   - *Answer:* mv/(qB). r = mv/(qB).
3. Why does a magnetic force do no work on a moving charge?
   - *Answer:* The force is always perpendicular to velocity, so it changes direction but not speed.

### 4.2 Current-Carrying Conductors and Magnetism

Current-carrying wires produce magnetic fields and experience forces in external fields. This is the basis of motors and meters.

**Key points**

- Field of a long straight wire: B = μ<sub>0</sub>I/(2πr).
- Field inside a solenoid: B = μ<sub>0</sub>nI.
- Force on a wire: F = BIL sin θ.
- Two parallel wires attract if currents are in the same direction.
- A moving-coil galvanometer uses torque on a coil in a magnetic field.

**Worked examples**

1. **Field near a wire.** A 10 A current at 0.05 m gives B = 4π × 10<sup>−7</sup> × 10/(2π × 0.05) = 4 × 10<sup>−5</sup> T.

2. **Motor principle.** A current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences torque, making it rotate — the basis of an electric motor.

**Practice questions**

1. The magnetic field inside a long solenoid is: (A) μ<sub>0</sub>nI (B) μ<sub>0</sub>I/(2πr) (C) Zero (D) BIL
   - *Answer:* μ<sub>0</sub>nI. The field is uniform and equals μ<sub>0</sub>nI.
2. Two parallel wires with currents in the same direction: (A) Attract (B) Repel (C) Do not interact (D) Rotate
   - *Answer:* Attract. Each wire's field exerts an attractive force on the other.
3. State Ampere's circuital law.
   - *Answer:* The line integral of the magnetic field around a closed loop equals μ<sub>0</sub> times the total current through the loop.

## Chapter 5. Magnetism and Matter

### 5.1 Bar Magnets and Earth's Magnetism

Magnetic materials interact through fields. A bar magnet behaves like a magnetic dipole, and Earth has its own magnetic field.

**Key points**

- A magnetic dipole has a magnetic moment m from south to north pole.
- Torque τ = mB sin θ; potential energy U = −mB cos θ.
- Earth's magnetic field has components: declination, dip and horizontal component.
- Magnetic field lines emerge from the north pole and enter the south pole.
- Magnetic materials align along field lines.

**Worked examples**

1. **Compass needle.** The needle aligns with the horizontal component of Earth's field, pointing toward magnetic north.

2. **Magnetic torque.** A magnet of moment 0.5 A·m<sup>2</sup> in a 0.2 T field at 30° experiences τ = 0.5 × 0.2 × 0.5 = 0.05 N·m.

**Practice questions**

1. The magnetic moment of a magnet is directed from: (A) South to north pole (B) North to south pole (C) East to west (D) Up to down
   - *Answer:* South to north pole. The magnetic moment points from the south to the north pole inside the magnet.
2. The angle of dip at the magnetic equator is: (A) 0° (B) 90° (C) 45° (D) 180°
   - *Answer:* 0°. The field is horizontal at the magnetic equator.
3. What is the angle of declination?
   - *Answer:* The angle between magnetic north and geographic north at a place.

### 5.2 Magnetic Materials and Hysteresis

Materials respond differently to magnetic fields: diamagnetic, paramagnetic and ferromagnetic. Hysteresis affects energy loss in transformers.

**Key points**

- Diamagnetic materials are weakly repelled by magnetic fields.
- Paramagnetic materials are weakly attracted; alignment is lost when the field is removed.
- Ferromagnetic materials can be strongly magnetised and retain magnetism (domains).
- Soft iron has a narrow hysteresis loop and is used in electromagnets.
- Steel has a wide loop, retaining magnetism — used for permanent magnets.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why soft iron for transformer cores.** Its narrow hysteresis loop means little energy is lost as heat during repeated magnetisation cycles.

2. **Why steel holds magnetism.** Domain walls resist movement in steel, so the magnetisation remains after the external field is removed.

**Practice questions**

1. Ferromagnetic materials: (A) Are strongly attracted to magnets (B) Are repelled (C) Do not interact (D) Are always magnets
   - *Answer:* Are strongly attracted to magnets. They have domains that align strongly with fields.
2. A material with a narrow hysteresis loop is good for: (A) Electromagnet cores (B) Permanent magnets (C) Insulation (D) Dielectrics
   - *Answer:* Electromagnet cores. Low hysteresis loss suits repeated magnetisation.
3. What is hysteresis?
   - *Answer:* The lag between magnetisation and the applied magnetic field in a ferromagnetic material.

## Chapter 6. Electromagnetic Induction

### 6.1 Faraday's and Lenz's Laws

Changing magnetic flux induces an EMF. Lenz's law gives the direction of the induced current.

**Key points**

- Magnetic flux φ = BA cos θ.
- Faraday's law: induced EMF ε = −dφ/dt.
- Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change producing it.
- A changing field or changing area can change flux.
- Induced EMF appears in a conductor moving through a field: ε = Bvl.

**Worked examples**

1. **Motional EMF.** A rod 0.5 m long moving at 2 m/s perpendicular to a 0.1 T field develops ε = 0.1 × 2 × 0.5 = 0.1 V.

2. **Dropping a magnet through a coil.** The changing flux induces a current whose field opposes the magnet's motion, so the magnet falls more slowly.

**Practice questions**

1. Faraday's law says the induced EMF equals: (A) Negative rate of change of flux (B) Flux times area (C) Field times distance (D) Current times resistance
   - *Answer:* Negative rate of change of flux. ε = −dφ/dt.
2. Lenz's law is a consequence of conservation of: (A) Energy (B) Charge (C) Mass (D) Momentum
   - *Answer:* Energy. The induced current opposes the change, conserving energy.
3. Why does a magnet fall slowly through a copper tube?
   - *Answer:* The induced eddy currents create a field opposite to the magnet's motion, producing a retarding force.

### 6.2 Self and Mutual Induction

Changing current in a coil induces EMF in the same or a nearby coil. This is inductance.

**Key points**

- Self-inductance ε = −L dI/dt; L is measured in henries (H).
- Energy stored in an inductor U = ½LI<sup>2</sup>.
- Mutual inductance M relates flux in one coil to current in another.
- Inductors oppose changes in current, analogue to inertia.
- Transformers use mutual induction to change AC voltage.

**Worked examples**

1. **Energy in an inductor.** A 2 H inductor carrying 3 A stores U = ½ × 2 × 9 = 9 J.

2. **Transformer turns ratio.** A transformer with 100 primary turns and 500 secondary turns steps 220 V up to 1100 V (ignoring losses).

**Practice questions**

1. The unit of inductance is the: (A) Henry (B) Farad (C) Tesla (D) Weber
   - *Answer:* Henry. Inductance is measured in henries.
2. Energy stored in an inductor is: (A) ½LI<sup>2</sup> (B) LI (C) LI<sup>2</sup> (D) I<sup>2</sup>/L
   - *Answer:* ½LI<sup>2</sup>. U = ½LI<sup>2</sup>.
3. Why is mutual induction useful in transformers?
   - *Answer:* Changing current in the primary creates a changing flux that induces a voltage in the secondary.

## Chapter 7. Alternating Current

### 7.1 AC Circuits and Reactance

Alternating current varies sinusoidally. Resistors, capacitors and inductors respond differently to AC.

**Key points**

- RMS values: I<sub>rms</sub> = I<sub>0</sub>/√2 and V<sub>rms</sub> = V<sub>0</sub>/√2.
- Inductive reactance X<sub>L</sub> = ωL; capacitive reactance X<sub>C</sub> = 1/(ωC).
- Impedance Z = √(R<sup>2</sup> + (X<sub>L</sub> − X<sub>C</sub>)<sup>2</sup>).
- Resonance occurs when X<sub>L</sub> = X<sub>C</sub> at ω = 1/√(LC).
- Power in AC: P = V<sub>rms</sub>I<sub>rms</sub> cos φ, where cos φ is the power factor.

**Worked examples**

1. **Impedance calculation.** With R = 30 Ω, X<sub>L</sub> = 40 Ω and X<sub>C</sub> = 0, Z = √(900 + 1600) = 50 Ω, so I<sub>rms</sub> = 220/50 = 4.4 A.

2. **Resonance in a radio.** Tuning a capacitor changes the resonant frequency of an LC circuit so it matches the station.

**Practice questions**

1. Power factor is: (A) cos φ (B) sin φ (C) tan φ (D) Z/R
   - *Answer:* cos φ. The power factor is the cosine of the phase angle.
2. At resonance in an LCR series circuit: (A) Impedance is minimum (B) Impedance is maximum (C) Current is zero (D) Voltage is zero
   - *Answer:* Impedance is minimum. X<sub>L</sub> and X<sub>C</sub> cancel, leaving only R.
3. Calculate the RMS value of a sinusoidal current with peak 10 A.
   - *Answer:* I<sub>rms</sub> = 10/√2 ≈ 7.07 A.

### 7.2 Transformers and Power Transmission

Transformers change AC voltages for efficient transmission of power over long distances.

**Key points**

- A step-up transformer increases voltage; a step-down transformer decreases it.
- Turns ratio: V<sub>s</sub>/V<sub>p</sub> = N<sub>s</sub>/N<sub>p</sub>.
- Power is roughly conserved: V<sub>p</sub>I<sub>p</sub> ≈ V<sub>s</sub>I<sub>s</sub>.
- High-voltage transmission reduces I<sup>2</sup>R losses in cables.
- Transformers work on AC and cannot change DC voltages.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why high voltage for long lines.** For the same power, raising voltage lowers current; losses I<sup>2</sup>R fall with the square of the current.

2. **Step-down at home.** Substations step 11 kV down to 220 V for homes, reducing voltage for safety.

**Practice questions**

1. A transformer works on: (A) AC (B) DC (C) Both AC and DC (D) Static charge
   - *Answer:* AC. It depends on changing flux, which AC provides.
2. High-voltage transmission reduces: (A) I<sup>2</sup>R losses (B) Voltage (C) Power (D) Frequency
   - *Answer:* I<sup>2</sup>R losses. Lower current means less resistive loss.
3. Why can a transformer not change DC voltage?
   - *Answer:* DC produces a constant flux, so no EMF is induced in the secondary coil.

## Chapter 8. Electromagnetic Waves

### 8.1 Displacement Current and EM Waves

Maxwell introduced displacement current, showing light is an electromagnetic wave. EM waves have electric and magnetic components oscillating perpendicular to each other.

**Key points**

- Displacement current I<sub>d</sub> = ε<sub>0</sub> dφ<sub>E</sub>/dt completes Ampere's law.
- EM waves are transverse; E and B are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation.
- Speed of EM waves in vacuum c = 1/√(μ<sub>0</sub>ε<sub>0</sub>) ≈ 3 × 10<sup>8</sup> m/s.
- In a medium v = c/n, where n is the refractive index.
- EM waves carry energy and momentum and need no medium.

**Worked examples**

1. **Why EM waves need no medium.** Oscillating fields regenerate each other, so they propagate through vacuum, unlike sound.

2. **Light through glass.** Light slows in glass because its interaction with atoms gives a smaller effective speed, n > 1.

**Practice questions**

1. In an EM wave, E and B are: (A) Perpendicular to each other (B) Parallel (C) Antiparallel (D) Random
   - *Answer:* Perpendicular to each other. The E and B fields oscillate at right angles.
2. The speed of light in vacuum is about: (A) 3 × 10<sup>8</sup> m/s (B) 3 × 10<sup>6</sup> m/s (C) 9.8 m/s (D) 1.5 × 10<sup>8</sup> m/s
   - *Answer:* 3 × 10<sup>8</sup> m/s. Light travels at about 3 × 10<sup>8</sup> m/s.
3. What is displacement current?
   - *Answer:* A current-like term due to a changing electric field that produces a magnetic field, completing Ampere's law.

### 8.2 The Electromagnetic Spectrum

EM waves span radio to gamma rays. Wavelength determines energy and applications.

**Key points**

- Radio waves: long wavelength, communication and broadcasting.
- Microwaves: radar, microwave ovens and satellite communication.
- Infrared: remote controls and thermal imaging.
- Visible light: 400–700 nm; ultraviolet: sterilisation and vitamin D synthesis.
- X-rays and gamma rays are ionising and used in imaging and cancer therapy.

**Worked examples**

1. **Microwave oven.** Microwaves match the natural frequency of water molecules, heating food from within.

2. **Why UV is harmful.** Ultraviolet photons carry enough energy to break chemical bonds, so they damage DNA and skin.

**Practice questions**

1. Which waves have the shortest wavelength? (A) Gamma rays (B) Radio waves (C) Infrared (D) Microwaves
   - *Answer:* Gamma rays. Gamma rays have the highest frequency and shortest wavelength.
2. Microwave ovens work by: (A) Heating water molecules (B) Cooling food (C) Emitting sound (D) Using X-rays
   - *Answer:* Heating water molecules. Microwaves excite water molecules, generating heat.
3. Why are X-rays used in medical imaging?
   - *Answer:* They penetrate soft tissue but are absorbed by dense bone, producing contrast images.

## Chapter 9. Ray Optics and Optical Instruments

### 9.1 Reflection, Refraction and Lenses

Light bends at surfaces and through lenses. Ray diagrams predict image position and size.

**Key points**

- Refractive index n = c/v; Snell's law n<sub>1</sub> sin i = n<sub>2</sub> sin r.
- Lens formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f; power P = 1/f in dioptres.
- Magnification m = v/u.
- Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from denser to rarer medium beyond the critical angle.
- Optical fibres use total internal reflection for communication.

**Worked examples**

1. **Critical angle for glass.** For glass of n = 1.5, the critical angle is sin<sup>−1</sup>(1/1.5) ≈ 41.8°.

2. **Power of a lens combination.** Powers add: +2 D and +3 D lenses in contact give +5 D, so focal length 0.2 m.

**Practice questions**

1. Total internal reflection occurs when light goes from: (A) Denser to rarer medium (B) Rarer to denser (C) Air to glass (D) Vacuum to air
   - *Answer:* Denser to rarer medium. It needs the denser-to-rarer case beyond the critical angle.
2. The power of a lens is measured in: (A) Dioptres (B) Farads (C) Henry (D) Tesla
   - *Answer:* Dioptres. Power = 1/f in metres.
3. Why does a diamond sparkle?
   - *Answer:* Its high refractive index gives a small critical angle, so light undergoes total internal reflection many times.

### 9.2 Optical Instruments and the Eye

Microscopes and telescopes use lenses to magnify. The human eye is an adjustable optical system.

**Key points**

- A simple microscope uses a convex lens to give angular magnification.
- A compound microscope uses objective and eyepiece lenses.
- An astronomical telescope produces a magnified image of distant objects.
- The eye's accommodation adjusts for near and far vision.
- Defects: myopia (concave lens), hypermetropia (convex lens), astigmatism (cylindrical lens).

**Worked examples**

1. **Telescope magnification.** M = f<sub>o</sub>/f<sub>e</sub> for a telescope at normal adjustment; a longer objective and shorter eyepiece give more magnification.

2. **Why near objects strain the eye.** The ciliary muscles must contract more to increase lens power, which tires the eye over time.

**Practice questions**

1. A myopic eye is corrected by a: (A) Concave lens (B) Convex lens (C) Plane mirror (D) Prism
   - *Answer:* Concave lens. A diverging lens moves the focus back to the retina.
2. Microscope magnification is increased by: (A) Shorter focal length objective (B) Longer eyepiece (C) Bigger aperture (D) Stronger light
   - *Answer:* Shorter focal length objective. Magnification is inversely related to focal length.
3. Why does a compound microscope use two lenses?
   - *Answer:* The objective magnifies the specimen and the eyepiece magnifies that image further.

## Chapter 10. Wave Optics

### 10.1 Interference and Young's Double Slit

Light behaves as a wave in interference and diffraction. Coherent sources produce a stable interference pattern.

**Key points**

- Interference produces bright and dark fringes from path differences.
- Path difference for bright fringe: Δ = nλ; for dark fringe: Δ = (2n − 1)λ/2.
- Fringe width in YDSE: β = λD/d.
- Coherent sources have constant phase difference and the same frequency.
- Diffraction also confirms the wave nature of light.

**Worked examples**

1. **Fringe width.** With λ = 600 nm, D = 1 m and d = 0.5 mm, β = 6 × 10<sup>−7</sup> × 1/(5 × 10<sup>−4</sup>) = 1.2 mm.

2. **Why fringes are coloured in white light.** Different wavelengths give different fringe widths and positions, so colours separate.

**Practice questions**

1. Fringe width in YDSE is directly proportional to: (A) Wavelength (B) Slit separation (C) 1/D (D) Frequency
   - *Answer:* Wavelength. β = λD/d.
2. Coherent sources must have: (A) Constant phase difference (B) Different frequencies (C) Different wavelengths (D) Random phase
   - *Answer:* Constant phase difference. Coherence is required for stable interference.
3. What is diffraction?
   - *Answer:* The spreading of light around obstacles or edges, showing its wave nature.

### 10.2 Polarisation and Resolving Power

Polarisation shows light is transverse. Optical instruments have limits on resolution set by diffraction.

**Key points**

- Polarisation restricts oscillations to one plane.
- Brewster's law: tan θ<sub>p</sub> = n gives the polarising angle.
- Polaroids and polarising filters can block specific orientations.
- Resolving power depends on wavelength and aperture diameter (Rayleigh criterion).
- Polarised sunglasses reduce glare from horizontal surfaces.

**Worked examples**

1. **Polarising sunglasses.** Reflected glare is partly polarised horizontally; vertical polarising filters block it, reducing glare.

2. **Brewster angle for glass.** For n = 1.5, θ<sub>p</sub> = tan<sup>−1</sup>(1.5) ≈ 56.3°, at which reflected light is fully polarised.

**Practice questions**

1. Polarisation shows light is: (A) Transverse (B) Longitudinal (C) Mechanical (D) A particle beam
   - *Answer:* Transverse. Only transverse waves can be polarised.
2. Polaroid films work by: (A) Blocking one polarisation direction (B) Reflecting all light (C) Refracting light (D) Absorbing all colours
   - *Answer:* Blocking one polarisation direction. They transmit only oscillations along one axis.
3. What is Brewster's angle?
   - *Answer:* The angle of incidence at which reflected light is completely polarised, given by tan θ<sub>p</sub> = n.

## Chapter 11. Dual Nature of Radiation and Matter

### 11.1 Photoelectric Effect

Light behaves as particles called photons. The photoelectric effect shows that light of sufficient frequency ejects electrons.

**Key points**

- A photon has energy E = hf = hc/λ.
- Photoelectric emission needs frequency above a threshold, regardless of intensity.
- Kinetic energy: K<sub>max</sub> = hf − φ, where φ is the work function.
- Stopping potential V<sub>0</sub>: eV<sub>0</sub> = K<sub>max</sub>.
- Intensity affects the number of electrons, not their maximum energy.

**Worked examples**

1. **Threshold frequency.** A metal with work function 2 eV needs light above f = φ/h ≈ 4.8 × 10<sup>14</sup> Hz.

2. **Photon energy calculation.** A 400 nm photon has E = hc/λ = 1240/400 ≈ 3.1 eV.

**Practice questions**

1. The photoelectric effect shows that light: (A) Behaves as particles (B) Is continuous (C) Has no energy (D) Is a sound wave
   - *Answer:* Behaves as particles. Photons explain the threshold frequency.
2. Maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons depends on: (A) Frequency of light (B) Intensity only (C) Distance (D) Angle
   - *Answer:* Frequency of light. K<sub>max</sub> = hf − φ.
3. Why does increased intensity not increase maximum kinetic energy?
   - *Answer:* Intensity increases the number of photons, not the energy per photon.

### 11.2 De Broglie Waves and Matter Waves

Particles also show wave behaviour. The de Broglie wavelength connects momentum and wavelength.

**Key points**

- de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p = h/(mv).
- Heavier or faster particles have shorter wavelengths.
- Electron diffraction experiments confirm matter waves.
- Matter waves explain the Bohr quantisation condition.
- Wave-particle duality applies to both light and matter.

**Worked examples**

1. **Electron wavelength.** An electron accelerated through 100 V has λ ≈ 0.12 nm, comparable to atomic spacing, so crystals diffract electrons.

2. **Why we do not see a cricket ball diffract.** Its momentum is enormous, so its de Broglie wavelength is far too small to notice.

**Practice questions**

1. de Broglie wavelength is: (A) h/p (B) hp (C) p/h (D) h/m
   - *Answer:* h/p. Wavelength is inversely proportional to momentum.
2. A heavier particle has a ______ de Broglie wavelength. (A) Shorter (B) Longer (C) Zero (D) Infinite
   - *Answer:* Shorter. λ = h/(mv), so larger mass means shorter wavelength.
3. Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of a 0.1 kg ball moving at 10 m/s.
   - *Answer:* λ = h/(mv) = 6.6 × 10<sup>−34</sup>/(0.1 × 10) = 6.6 × 10<sup>−34</sup> m, unmeasurably small.

## Chapter 12. Atoms

### 12.1 Rutherford's Model and Bohr's Theory

Rutherford's scattering experiment revealed the nucleus. Bohr's model explained atomic spectra by quantising angular momentum.

**Key points**

- Rutherford showed the atom is mostly empty space with a dense positive nucleus.
- Bohr's postulates: electrons occupy discrete orbits and emit/absorb photons between levels.
- Angular momentum is quantised: mvr = nh/2π.
- Energy of hydrogen levels: E<sub>n</sub> = −13.6/n<sup>2</sup> eV.
- The Bohr model explains the hydrogen spectrum but fails for multi-electron atoms.

**Worked examples**

1. **Hydrogen transition.** The n = 2 to n = 1 transition emits a photon of energy 13.6 − 3.4 = 10.2 eV, in the ultraviolet (Lyman series).

2. **Radius of the first orbit.** Bohr radius r<sub>1</sub> ≈ 0.53 Å; radii scale as n<sup>2</sup>.

**Practice questions**

1. The energy of the hydrogen ground state is: (A) −13.6 eV (B) −3.4 eV (C) −1.51 eV (D) 0 eV
   - *Answer:* −13.6 eV. E<sub>1</sub> = −13.6 eV.
2. Bohr's model quantises: (A) Angular momentum (B) Charge (C) Mass (D) Spin only
   - *Answer:* Angular momentum. mvr = nh/2π is the quantisation condition.
3. Why do atomic spectra consist of discrete lines?
   - *Answer:* Electrons transition between discrete energy levels, emitting photons of specific energies.

### 12.2 Spectral Series and Lasers

Hydrogen spectra fall into series. Stimulated emission underlies lasers.

**Key points**

- Lyman series: transitions to n = 1 (UV).
- Balmer series: transitions to n = 2 (visible).
- Paschen, Brackett and Pfund series lie in the infrared.
- 1/λ = R(1/n<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> − 1/n<sub>2</sub><sup>2</sup>) gives the Rydberg formula.
- Lasers use population inversion and stimulated emission to produce coherent light.

**Worked examples**

1. **Balmer line.** The n = 3 to 2 transition gives the red H-alpha line at 656 nm.

2. **Laser in surgery.** Coherent, intense, monochromatic laser light can cut tissue precisely with minimal damage to surrounding areas.

**Practice questions**

1. The Balmer series lies mainly in the: (A) Visible region (B) Ultraviolet (C) Infrared (D) Microwave
   - *Answer:* Visible region. Transitions to n = 2 produce visible lines.
2. A laser produces light that is: (A) Coherent (B) Incoherent (C) Polychromatic (D) Randomly phased
   - *Answer:* Coherent. Laser light has a constant phase relationship.
3. State the Rydberg formula.
   - *Answer:* 1/λ = R(1/n<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> − 1/n<sub>2</sub><sup>2</sup>), giving wavelengths of hydrogen spectral lines.

## Chapter 13. Nuclei

### 13.1 Nuclear Structure and Binding Energy

Nuclei contain protons and neutrons. Mass defect and binding energy explain nuclear stability.

**Key points**

- Atomic number Z = protons; mass number A = protons + neutrons.
- Mass defect Δm = mass of nucleons − mass of nucleus.
- Binding energy = Δm c<sup>2</sup>; binding energy per nucleon measures stability.
- Iron-56 has near maximum binding energy per nucleon.
- Nuclear forces are short-range and strong, overcoming proton repulsion.

**Worked examples**

1. **Binding energy per nucleon.** Iron-56 has about 8.8 MeV per nucleon, the highest among common nuclei, making it very stable.

2. **Why heavy nuclei fission.** Splitting a heavy nucleus increases binding energy per nucleon, releasing energy.

**Practice questions**

1. The mass number of a nucleus is: (A) Protons + neutrons (B) Protons only (C) Neutrons only (D) Electrons + protons
   - *Answer:* Protons + neutrons. Mass number counts nucleons.
2. Which nucleus has the highest binding energy per nucleon? (A) Iron-56 (B) Uranium-238 (C) Hydrogen-1 (D) Helium-4
   - *Answer:* Iron-56. Iron-56 is near the peak of the binding energy curve.
3. What is mass defect?
   - *Answer:* The difference between the sum of masses of nucleons and the mass of the nucleus, appearing as binding energy.

### 13.2 Radioactivity and Nuclear Reactions

Unstable nuclei decay by alpha, beta or gamma emission. Fission and fusion release energy.

**Key points**

- Alpha decay: emission of a helium nucleus; A decreases by 4 and Z by 2.
- Beta decay: a neutron converts to a proton, emitting an electron and antineutrino.
- Gamma decay: emission of a high-energy photon without change in A or Z.
- Half-life is the time for half the nuclei to decay.
- Fission splits heavy nuclei; fusion joins light nuclei.

**Worked examples**

1. **Half-life calculation.** After 3 half-lives, 1/8 of the original sample remains.

2. **Carbon dating.** Living things maintain a constant carbon-14 level; after death it decays with a 5730-year half-life, dating organic remains.

**Practice questions**

1. Alpha decay changes the mass number by: (A) −4 (B) −1 (C) 0 (D) +4
   - *Answer:* −4. An alpha particle removes 4 nucleons.
2. In beta-minus decay, a neutron becomes a: (A) Proton (B) Electron (C) Gamma ray (D) Alpha particle
   - *Answer:* Proton. A neutron converts to a proton, emitting an electron.
3. Why does fusion release energy?
   - *Answer:* Light nuclei combine into a more tightly bound nucleus, so the mass defect converts into energy.

## Chapter 14. Semiconductor Electronics

### 14.1 Semiconductors and Doping

Semiconductors have conductivity between conductors and insulators, controlled by doping.

**Key points**

- Silicon and germanium are the common semiconductors.
- N-type semiconductors are doped with pentavalent atoms, adding electrons.
- P-type semiconductors are doped with trivalent atoms, creating holes.
- The depletion region forms at a p-n junction.
- A p-n junction diode conducts in one direction.

**Worked examples**

1. **Doping silicon.** Adding phosphorus (5 valence electrons) creates free electrons, making n-type silicon.

2. **Holes as current carriers.** In p-type silicon, missing electrons behave as positive holes that move under an electric field.

**Practice questions**

1. N-type semiconductors have: (A) Extra electrons (B) Extra holes (C) No charge carriers (D) Only holes
   - *Answer:* Extra electrons. Pentavalent doping provides electrons.
2. A diode conducts when: (A) Forward biased (B) Reverse biased (C) Unbiased (D) Broken
   - *Answer:* Forward biased. Forward bias reduces the depletion barrier.
3. Why do p-n junctions act as one-way valves?
   - *Answer:* They allow current in forward bias but block it in reverse bias.

### 14.2 Diodes, Rectifiers and Transistors

Semiconductor devices are used in power supply, amplification and digital electronics.

**Key points**

- A half-wave rectifier uses one diode; a full-wave rectifier uses a bridge of four.
- A Zener diode works in reverse breakdown to stabilise voltage.
- A transistor has emitter, base and collector; a small base current controls a large collector current.
- Transistors operate as amplifiers or switches.
- Logic gates and integrated circuits are built from semiconductors.

**Worked examples**

1. **Full-wave rectifier.** Four diodes in a bridge convert both halves of the AC cycle into DC.

2. **Transistor as a switch.** A small base current turns on a larger collector current, allowing a tiny signal to control a large load.

**Practice questions**

1. A transistor has: (A) Emitter, base and collector (B) Source, drain and gate (C) Anode and cathode (D) Input and output only
   - *Answer:* Emitter, base and collector. These are the three regions of a bipolar junction transistor.
2. A Zener diode is used for: (A) Voltage regulation (B) Amplification (C) Rectification (D) Oscillation
   - *Answer:* Voltage regulation. It stabilises voltage in reverse breakdown.
3. Explain why a transistor can amplify a signal.
   - *Answer:* A small change in base current produces a much larger change in collector current.
